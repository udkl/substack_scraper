There is a certain peril in a crowded narrative.

When a story about a market, an industry, or a company becomes so widely accepted that it calcifies into dogma, objectivity becomes the first casualty.

We, as investors, are wired for narrative. We seek patterns, we crave simple explanations, and in doing so, we often construct intellectual prisons from which it is difficult to escape.

The mind, once committed to a worldview, filters reality to fit it. Uncomfortable truths are discarded; confirming evidence is amplified.

This is the essence of Graham's warning: the greatest risks we face are often not external, but internal; the cognitive biases that make us our own worst enemy.

Nowhere has a narrative been more crowded or more grim than in the U.S. coal sector. For years, the story has been one of terminal decline, a slow fade into obsolescence at the hands of cleaner, cheaper alternatives. The market, in its collective wisdom, seemed to agree. A stock price index of publicly traded U.S. coal companies lost a staggering 23.0% of its value in the first half of 2025 alone, a period when the broader market was holding its ground. With the Global Coal Index down 20%. The verdict appeared unanimous: the ghost of coal was finally being laid to rest.

And yet, when the books were closed on the second quarter of 2025, a strange and inconvenient set of facts emerged.

The on-the-ground reality, it turned out, was far more complex and, for many, surprisingly robust. Of seven select U.S. coal producers, a clear majority (four companies) did not just meet analyst expectations; they decisively beat them.

Some turned projected losses into solid profits, exposing a profound disconnect between the consensus narrative and corporate performance.

This earnings season, then, becomes more than a simple financial report.

It is a lesson from Graham's famous allegory, "Mr. Market". Mr. Market, the manic-depressive business partner, had spent the first half of the year in a deep gloom, offering to sell shares in these coal companies at prices that reflected a hopeless future.

The earnings reports, however, suggest he was in the grip of one of his irrational moods. This raises a critical question for the thoughtful strategist: What fundamental truths did the market's emotional, voting-machine mentality overlook? And more importantly, what can this episode teach us about the mental models required to navigate such profound disconnects between perception and reality?

An Autopsy of the Analysts' Surprise

To understand the depth of the market's misjudgment, one must first examine the raw numbers.

The story of Q2 2025 is not one of a uniform revival but of a stark divergence, where operational execution and company-specific realities overwhelmed the simplistic, top-down narrative of decay.

Divergence

The second-quarter earnings is best understood through a direct comparison.

The following table lays out the performance of the seven select companies, revealing a clear pattern: the largest players stumbled, while many of their smaller peers soared past expectations.

Where the Models Broke Down

The four companies that beat expectations did so not by small margins, but in ways that suggest a fundamental flaw in the analytical models used to forecast their performance.

Warrior Met Coal Inc. (HCC) stands out as a prime example of this modeling failure. Analysts had projected a loss of $0.36 per share. Instead, the company reported a profit of $0.11 per share.

This is not a rounding error; it is a categorical misreading of the company's operational capabilities.

The beat was driven by relentless execution in a tough environment. While the average net selling price for its metallurgical coal plummeted by 30% year-over-year, Warrior Met Coal countered by increasing both production and sales volumes by 6% and, crucially, slashing its cash cost of sales by 18% compared to the prior quarter.

This operational grit, the ability to control costs when prices are uncontrollable, is a qualitative factor that generic, top-down financial models often fail to capture.

The most dramatic beat, however, belongs to Alpha Metallurgical Resources Inc. (AMR).

The consensus estimate foresaw a catastrophic loss of $2.76 per share.

The actual reported loss was a mere $0.38 per share. The sheer magnitude of this discrepancy (a beat of $2.38 per share) points to a profound misunderstanding of Alpha's cost structure, contract book, or both.

While the company's year-over-year performance was indeed weak, with revenue and net income down substantially, its ability to manage the downturn far exceeded the market's bleak forecast.

The stories at Peabody Energy Corp. (BTU) and Hallador Energy Co. (HNRG) echo this theme. Both were expected to post losses but instead delivered profits.

Peabody reported earnings of $0.09 per share against an expected loss of $0.05. Hallador Energy's performance was even more striking, reporting a profit of $0.19 per share when analysts had braced for a loss of $0.15.

For Hallador, this constituted a massive 211.76% positive earnings surprise, driven by strong operational performance that also pushed revenues well past estimates.

Analyzing the Misses

A balanced analysis requires understanding the misses as well. These were not, however, evidence of the broad "coal is dead" narrative.

Instead, they were stories of company-specific headwinds and strategic choices, providing crucial context to the broader industry picture.

Core Natural Resources Inc. (CNR), the largest company in the group, posted the most significant miss, reporting a loss of $0.68 per share against an expected profit of $0.65.

This miss was not the result of a fundamental collapse in its business model but was directly attributable to an acute operational issue: a combustion event at its Leer South mine.

The costs associated with extinguishing the fire and idling the mine amounted to $21.2 million in the quarter, a specific, non-recurring event that dragged down the bottom line.

Alliance Resource Partners LP (ARLP), the second-largest producer, missed by a much narrower margin, reporting EPS of $0.55 versus a $0.60 estimate.

The shortfall was a function of the brutal pricing environment; lower-than-anticipated coal sales prices and transportation revenues simply overwhelmed the positive impact of higher sales volumes.

This highlights the intense margin pressure facing even the most efficient operators.

Finally, Ramaco Resources Inc. (METC), the smallest company in the cohort, also missed its EPS target, reporting a loss of $0.29 versus an expected loss of $0.20.

For Ramaco, however, the earnings miss is almost a footnote to a much larger and more compelling strategic narrative. The company is in the midst of a capital-intensive pivot to become a major domestic producer of rare earth elements (REEs).

This strategic investment, which is crucial for its long-term valuation, led to an increase in selling, general, and administrative (SG&A) costs that weighed on near-term profitability. Judging Ramaco solely on its Q2 coal EPS is to miss the forest for the trees.

The pattern of these results reveals a deeper truth. The most significant earnings beats occurred at the smaller, less-covered companies.

Investment banks and research firms, facing their own resource constraints, often allocate analytical firepower based on market capitalization and investor interest.

In a deeply out-of-favor sector like coal, smaller players such as Alpha Metallurgical and Warrior Met Coal likely receive scant attention. Analyst models for these firms are updated infrequently, relying on broad, pessimistic assumptions about pricing and costs.

Meanwhile, these companies, fighting for their survival, have become hyper-focused on operational excellence and cost control; the very "un-modellable" factors that allow them to outperform in harsh conditions.

The result is a vast chasm between the gloomy consensus and the gritty reality, creating an environment ripe for the kind of massive earnings surprises seen this quarter.

For the contrarian investor, this suggests that the greatest information arbitrage opportunities often lie in the most neglected corners of the market.

Washington Tailwind and Data Center Boom

The strong operational performance seen in Q2 was not happening in a vacuum. It was amplified by powerful macro forces that many market observers, fixated on the narrative of decline, appear to have underestimated.

A political shift in Washington and a technological revolution in data processing converged to create a potent tailwind for the U.S. coal industry.

The "One Big Beautiful Bill Act": More Than Just a Slogan

In the second quarter, the Trump administration's pro-coal stance transitioned from rhetoric to tangible financial policy with the passage of the "One Big Beautiful Bill Act" on July 4, 2025.

This legislation fundamentally altered the cost structure for many U.S. coal producers, providing direct and quantifiable benefits that flowed straight to the bottom line.

Executive commentary during earnings calls provided direct evidence of its impact. Peabody Energy's CEO, James C. Grech, was explicit, describing the bill as delivering "long-overdue relief". He quantified the benefit of one key provision, the reduction of the federal royalty rate on mining leases from 12.5% to 7%, as generating an expected net benefit of $15 million to $20 million for Peabody in 2025 alone.

This change directly improves the competitiveness of its vast Powder River Basin (PRB) operations. The CEO of Core Natural Resources also highlighted this royalty reduction as a significant positive for the company's PRB and West Elk mines.

Furthermore, the bill introduced a 2.5% production tax credit for metallurgical coal used in steelmaking, a direct subsidy that benefits producers like Peabody's Shoal Creek mine.

These policy changes are not minor tweaks; they represent a structural shift in the industry's cost basis. The savings from lower royalties and the benefits from tax credits are key variables that legacy models, built on assumptions from a different political era, failed to incorporate, contributing directly to the positive earnings surprises.

The New Demand Shock: From Megawatts to Terawatts

Simultaneously, a new and potentially paradigm-shifting demand driver emerged: the rapidly growing energy appetite of artificial intelligence and data centers.

For years, the demand side of the equation for thermal coal was a simple story of decline.

The AI revolution has violently rewritten that script.

Industry leaders see this not as a temporary blip but as a fundamental reshaping of the energy landscape. Alliance Resource Partners' CEO, Joe Craft, described the domestic market outlook as the "most encouraging... since early twenty twenty-three," explicitly citing the expansion of AI data centers as a primary driver.

Peabody's CEO went further, quoting an independent system operator forecast that predicted a 32-gigawatt increase in power demand by 2030, with a staggering 30 gigawatts of that growth attributable to data centers.

He put this in perspective by likening it to adding 20 million new homes to the grid.

This executive optimism is not unfounded. It is validated by broader market data.

The International Energy Agency (IEA), in its mid-year update, noted a surprising and significant development: U.S. coal demand was estimated to have increased by 10% in the first half of 2025.

This was driven by strong growth in electricity demand combined with higher natural gas prices, which improved coal's position in the generation stack. The heat of summer, combined with the constant, baseload power required by data centers, is creating a demand profile that intermittent renewable sources struggle to meet, forcing a renewed reliance on dependable thermal generation.

The convergence of a supportive political agenda with a technology-driven electricity demand shock creates a powerful, multi-year tailwind that threatens to invalidate prior models of secular decline.

The analytical error was to view the world through a single lens.

Analysts focused on the long-term energy transition missed the immediate impact of new legislation. They focused on the retirement of old coal plants and missed the construction of new, power-hungry data centers.

This was not a simple cyclical bounce; it was the collision of two unforeseen structural forces. The meeting of a policy that fundamentally alters the industry's cost structure with a technological boom that introduces a new, non-linear demand variable creates what Charlie Munger would call a "lollapalooza effect."

The combined impact is far greater than the sum of its parts, a synergy that caught the consensus flat-footed and suggests the investment thesis for U.S. thermal coal may have shifted from managing decline to serving as a critical, and newly profitable, bridge fuel for the AI revolution.

a Better Latticework for a Cyclical Beast

The Q2 earnings season for coal serves as a powerful case study, elevating the discussion from a specific market event to a broader lesson in investment philosophy.

It emphasises the profound wisdom in the teachings of investors like Charlie Munger, who long advocated for a more robust, multidisciplinary approach to thinking.

Munger's Latticework and the Failure of the Single Model

Charlie Munger's core intellectual contribution is arguably the concept of a "latticework of mental models".

He argued that to truly understand a complex system, one must draw upon the big ideas from all the major disciplines: physics, biology, psychology, economics, and so on.

To rely on a single model, no matter how comfortable, is to invite disaster.

As Munger famously said, "To the man with a hammer, the world looks like a nail".

The failure of analysts to accurately forecast coal earnings is a textbook example of this single-model trap.

The dominant model was "the energy transition," a powerful but incomplete framework. Armed with this single hammer, every piece of data was pounded to fit.

As Munger warned, "If you have just one or two that you're using, the nature of human psychology is such that you'll torture reality so that it fits your models".

The market tortured the reality of rising electricity demand and new political support to fit its pre-existing "coal is dead" model.

A proper understanding of the U.S. coal sector in 2025 required a far richer latticework. It demanded a model from political science to understand the financial impact of the "One Big Beautiful Bill Act." It needed a model from technology to grasp the exponential energy demands of the AI boom. It required a model from engineering to appreciate the operational leverage and cost-control capabilities of individual companies. And, crucially, it needed a model from behavioral finance to recognize the irrational pessimism that had gripped Mr. Market.

The earnings surprise occurred not within any single one of these domains, but at their intersection.

The Cyclical Trap: Why Low P/Es Can Be a Trap

This intellectual blind spot leads directly to classic investment errors, particularly in cyclical industries.

As an analyst wisely notes, "the best time to buy a cyclical stock is typically when they have high PE ratios, and the worst time is when they have low PE ratios."

This seemingly counterintuitive statement holds a deep truth about market psychology.

A high or negative P/E ratio occurs when earnings (the "E") are at a cyclical trough. The market, extrapolating this poor performance into the future, assigns the company a low valuation.

This is precisely where the coal sector found itself in early 2025.

The focus on depressed near-term earnings caused investors to miss the turn. The Q2 earnings beats are the first concrete signal that the "E" is beginning a powerful recovery.

As earnings rebound, stocks purchased at the point of maximum pessimism will, in retrospect, look extraordinarily cheap.

Conversely, a low P/E ratio often marks a cyclical peak, when earnings are unsustainably high: a siren song luring investors in just before the cycle turns down.

The market's failure in Q2 was a failure to appreciate this fundamental rhythm of cyclical industries.

Stock Watchlist

Ramaco Inc. (METC)

If the broader sector demonstrates the need for multiple models, the case of Ramaco Resources Inc. (METC) illustrates the necessity of building bespoke models for unique situations.

To analyze Ramaco as just another coal company is a profound category error.

Ramaco is aggressively executing a strategic pivot to become a "dual platform" company, focused on both high-quality metallurgical coal and the mining and processing of rare earth elements (REEs).

Combining its low-cost metallurgical coal operations with what may be one of the most strategically important mineral deposits in the United States: the Brook Mine in Wyoming.

This is the first new REE mine to open in the U.S. in over 70 years, directly challenging China’s stranglehold on minerals critical for defense, electric vehicles, and high-tech manufacturing.

What the market is missing are three critical points:

  1. A Unique Geological Advantage: Unlike conventional hard-rock REE deposits, the minerals at Brook Mine are commingled with soft coal and clay. This makes them easier and cheaper to mine and, crucially, means the project is not burdened with the radioactivity issues that plague many other REE ventures.

  2. A Staggering Valuation Mismatch: An independent preliminary economic assessment (PEA) conducted by Fluor Corporation assigned the Brook Mine project a pre-tax Net Present Value (NPV) of $1.2 billion. This figure alone is nearly commensurate with Ramaco's entire enterprise value of approximately $1.7 billion, suggesting the market is ascribing little to no value to this transformative asset.

  3. A De-Risking Inflection Point: In early August 2025, Ramaco raised approximately $200 million in an upsized public offering. The stated use of proceeds is to "fund the acceleration of its development of its rare earth elements and critical minerals project". This is not just dilution; it is a powerful signal of management's confidence and, more importantly, it removes financing risk from the equation. The project is now fully funded to accelerate towards commercialization.

The investment thesis for Ramaco is not about a quarterly earnings beat. It is a sum-of-the-parts arbitrage. Investors are buying a stable, cash-flowing metallurgical coal business and, for free, are receiving a call option on a multi-billion-dollar, strategically vital, and now fully-funded domestic rare earths powerhouse.

The lesson is clear.

True alpha in today's complex world is rarely found through deeper specialization in a single field. It is found at the intersection of seemingly unrelated disciplines.

The analyst who could have anticipated the Q2 coal surprise was not the one with the most detailed spreadsheet on thermal coal prices. It was the one who could connect the dots between a new law passed in Washington, a new chip designed in Silicon Valley, the operational leverage of a cyclical industry, and the timeless irrationality of the investing crowd.

Alpha Metallurgical Resources (AMR)

If Ramaco is a story of strategic transformation, Alpha Metallurgical Resources is a classic deep-value case of the market failing to see the bigger picture.

The consensus narrative was bleak, with analysts forecasting a catastrophic loss of $2.76 per share for Q2.

Instead, AMR delivered a loss of just $0.38 per share: a beat of impressive proportions that points to a fundamental flaw in the market's models.

This was not an accounting trick.

It was the result of exceptional operational execution in a brutal market.

Management achieved its best quarterly cost of coal sales performance since 2021, slashing costs to $100.06 per ton through a 10% increase in productivity (tons per man-hour) and disciplined reductions in maintenance and supply expenses.

While the market remains fixated on weak metallurgical coal prices, it is ignoring two facts that form the foundation of the AMR thesis:

  1. A Fortress Balance Sheet: As of June 30, 2025, Alpha had total liquidity of $556.9 million against negligible long-term debt of just $5.8 million. This financial strength provides an immense margin of safety, allowing the company to not only survive the downturn but to emerge from it stronger than its more leveraged peers.

  2. A Coiled Spring of Capital Returns: Alpha has approximately $400 million remaining on its $1.5 billion share repurchase authorization. Management paused buybacks due to market softness but explicitly stated on their Q2 call that with liquidity now meaningfully increased, they plan to restart the program "on an opportunistic basis". This represents a powerful and imminent catalyst that could absorb a significant portion of the company's public float at what are clearly depressed prices.

The investment thesis for Alpha is a pure play on the market's failure to weigh substance. An investor can acquire a best-in-class, operationally superior producer with a pristine balance sheet at a valuation that reflects trough-of-the-cycle pessimism. The combination of extreme operational leverage to a recovering met coal price and the imminent catalyst of a massive share buyback creates a deeply compelling, asymmetric risk-reward profile.

Weighing Votes, Weighing Substance

The events of the second quarter of 2025 in the U.S. coal sector offer a powerful, real-world lesson in intellectual independence. They serve as a vivid reminder of the distinction Benjamin Graham drew between the market as a "voting machine" in the short run and a "weighing machine" in the long run.

In the first half of the year, the voting machine was in full effect. The market cast its ballots, and the verdict was a resounding vote of no confidence, reflected in the 23% plunge in the coal stock index.

The narrative was simple, compelling, and wrong.

The Q2 earnings reports were the first arrival of the weighing machine. They began the process of measuring the actual substance of these businesses: their operational cash flows, their cost structures, their ability to adapt and execute.

The weight of these fundamentals, it turned out, was far greater than the votes had suggested.

This is not a call to be blindly bullish on coal.

The long-term challenges facing the industry remain significant. Rather, it is a call to build a more robust intellectual toolkit. The Q2 earnings season was a stress test for analytical models, and the simple, narrative-driven ones failed spectacularly. It demonstrated the urgent need to question the comfort of the consensus, to seek out disconfirming evidence, and to have the courage to focus on intrinsic value when Mr. Market is lost in one of his manic-depressive episodes.

The enduring lesson is that our chief problem truly is ourselves.

The market's greatest mistakes are born from intellectual laziness and the herd instinct.

The greatest opportunities, therefore, are reserved for those who do the difficult work of forging their own latticework of models, who can weigh substance when others are merely counting votes, and who understand that the most profitable positions are often the most uncomfortable ones to hold.

The challenge is not merely to consume information, but to build the mental tools necessary to see the world as it is, not as the crowd insists it should be.
