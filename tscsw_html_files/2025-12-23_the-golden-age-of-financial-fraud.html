The financial history of technological revolutions is often written in two distinct chapters.

The first is characterized by limitless euphoria, a suspension of disbelief, and the rapid capital formation around a transformative narrative. The second chapter, invariably, is one of reckoning. A period where the gravitational forces of unit economics, solvency, and accounting reality reassert themselves.

We currently stand at the conjunction between these two phases in the Artificial Intelligence cycle.

The winning narrative driving the current equity market super-cycle is simple and historically attractive: in a gold rush, one should not mine for gold, but rather sell picks and shovels. This adage (which has been repeated maybe a million times now) has moved trillions of dollars of institutional and retail capital into the hardware, data center, and semiconductor layers of the AI stack.

The consensus view holds a cult-like certainty that regardless of which Large Language Model (LLM) ultimately prevails (or indeed, whether a “killer app” for generative AI emerges immediately) the infrastructure providers are the guaranteed, rent-seeking beneficiaries of the build-out (spoiler alert they are wrong).

However, if we actually look at the underlying capital structures, revenue quality, and accounting maneuvers instead of just believing headlines like the analysts at Wallstreet, we’ll quickly see some major issues.

This post will look at the four central cracks in the AI infrastructure thesis:

  • The “Golden Age of Fraud”: A market environment where complex technology obscures fundamental weaknesses, allowing “round-tripped” revenue to masquerade as organic growth.

  • Shadow Debt Expansion: A massive accumulation of leverage disguised as “finance leases,” with Microsoft alone hiding $46.2 billion in liabilities that don’t hit the CapEx line.

  • The Deflationary Reality: A 28% year-over-year drop in H100 rental yields that destroys the ROI assumptions for multi-billion dollar data clusters.

  • The Unprofitable Customer: A demand curve driven not by solvent public utilities, but by VC-backed startups burning cash to rent capacity they cannot profitably monetize.

Macro-Financial Context

Casino Culture & “Golden Age of Fraud”

To understand the specific fragility of the AI trade, lets first look at a broader macro-financial environment that has emerged in the post-pandemic era.

Jim Chanos, the founder of Kynikos Associates and the man who famously exposed Enron and Wirecard, argues that the financial system has become “steeped in a casino culture than it has been in a very long time”.

This “casino culture” describes a structural shift in how capital is allocated and how risk is priced.

In a zero-interest-rate environment (ZIRP) that persisted for over a decade, the cost of capital was artificially suppressed, allowing business models with negative unit economics to flourish.

While rates have normalized, the behavioral patterns of that era persist.

Investors, conditioned to “buy the dip” and chase momentum, have migrated from meme stocks and cryptocurrencies to the perceived 'safety of AI infrastructure.

This period is a “Golden Age of Fraud” because the mechanisms of accountability have been eroded.

In a market where stock prices are detached from fundamental cash flows, management teams are incentivized to maintain the narrative at all costs.

This environment creates a fertile breeding ground for “aggressive accounting,” “strategic partnerships” that exist solely to massage revenue figures, and the widespread use of non-GAAP metrics to hide deteriorating profitability (who would’ve thought!).

The “DeepSeek” Event - Anatomy of a Black Swan

The fragility of this ecosystem was in my opinion illustrated nicely by the recent market dislocation involving DeepSeek.

This event is a paradigmatic example of the “unpredictable events” that pose the greatest risk to US markets over the next six to twelve months.

While the technical specifics of the DeepSeek collapse (a sudden and catastrophic loss of value in a major Chinese AI entity) are complex (so we will just skip it), the market reaction was simple and brutal.

It wiped roughly $1 trillion in market value from US stocks in a contagion event that defied simple geographic boundaries. This event serves as a critical rebuttal to the “decoupling” thesis. It demonstrated that the AI trade is a monolithic, highly correlated bet.

“The real risks will be something like DeepSeek that comes out of left field that changes people’s thinking,” Chanos noted in a Bloomberg interview. “By definition, we do not know what that is.”

The significance of the DeepSeek event lies in what it revealed about the market’s “froth.”

While the market hasn’t quite reached the peak insanity of the 2021 boom, it is heavily laden with “speculative froth”.

The collapse showed that the valuation multiples of US semiconductor and cloud companies are implicitly pricing in a seamless, interruption-free global growth trajectory for AI. When a major player like DeepSeek falters, it calls into question the solvency of the entire demand curve. If one major AI lab can fail, implies the market, perhaps the insatiable demand for H100 GPUs is not as inelastic as the bulls believe.

Political Theater & Tariff Threat

Adding a layer of volatility to this fragile structure is the resurgence of geopolitical risk, which Chanos categorizes as “political theater”.

The interdependence of the AI supply chain (relying on design in the US, lithography in the Netherlands, and advanced packaging in Taiwan and China) makes it uniquely vulnerable to trade friction.

One must closely watching the changing tariff situation under President Donald Trump. Specifically, he expresses skepticism that a 10% levy against China will be the ceiling. “I think 10% is not going to do it,” Chanos argued, pointing out that to generate significant revenue, the administration would need to raise tariffs “substantially on both China and the EU”.

This presents a dual threat to the “Pick and Shovel” play:

  1. Cost Inflation: Higher tariffs on imported components (PCBs, cooling systems, rare earth metals) would directly compress the gross margins of hardware manufacturers.

  2. Demand Destruction: Retaliatory measures could lock US hyperscalers out of key growth markets or restrict the flow of capital from Asian entities that are significant limited partners (LPs) in the venture funds driving the AI boom.

The “political theater” is a fundamental variable in the cost-of-goods-sold (COGS) equation for the entire industry. In a “Golden Age of Fraud,” these external shocks often serve as the catalyst that exposes internal weaknesses. When the tide of easy money and geopolitical stability goes out, we discover who has been swimming naked.

Demand - “Unprofitable Customer” Thesis

The Fundamental Divergence: 2000 vs. 2025

A central pillar of the bull case for AI infrastructure is the comparison to the internet build-out of the late 1990s.

The argument goes that even if many Dotcom companies failed, the companies that built the routers (Cisco) and laid the fiber (Global Crossing, though they faced their own accounting scandals) were selling into a real technological shift.

But many people are missing the sharp and unfavorable distinction between the two eras.

In the Dotcom era, the infrastructure was being built to service a network that ultimately connected billions of consumers to services they paid for (broadband, e-commerce, content). In the current AI cycle, the “unprofitable” nature of the customers makes the risk profile significantly higher.

The core issue is the source of the funds used to purchase the infrastructure. In a healthy market, capital expenditures (CapEx) are funded by operating cash flow (OCF).

A utility company builds a power plant because its customers are paying for electricity. In the current AI market, a massive portion of the CapEx deployed by hyperscalers is being built to service startups that are funded entirely by Venture Capital (VC).

These customers are not just unprofitable in the accounting sense (net loss); they are structurally unprofitable on a unit economics basis. They are subsidizing the cost of compute to gain user adoption, hoping that future efficiency gains will eventually yield a margin.

This is a precarious foundation for a trillion-dollar infrastructure build-out.

The Circular Economy

The most damning critique of the current market structure is the phenomenon of “circularity” or “round-tripping” of revenue.

This dynamic creates an illusion of strong demand that is, in reality, a closed loop of recycled capital.

Bloomberg has visualized this as the “AI Circular Economy”. The mechanism works as follows:

  1. The Injection: A Hyperscaler (e.g., Microsoft, Oracle) or a Chip Designer (Nvidia) invests billions of dollars of cash into a “Hot AI Startup” (e.g., OpenAI, Cohere, CoreWeave).

  2. The Commitment: As part of the investment deal, the Startup commits to spending a significant portion of that capital on cloud services or hardware provided by the investor.

  3. The Booking: The Hyperscaler books this spend as “Revenue” from its cloud division.

  4. The Valuation: The Startup uses this “partnership” to justify a higher valuation in its next funding round, which allows the Hyperscaler to mark up the value of its equity investment.

This loop, Capital Injection → Cloud Revenue → Valuation Markup, creates a situation where the hyperscaler is effectively recognizing its own invested capital as revenue. Mind you many great substack writers have said this many times, one of my favorites is The Credit Strategist.

It should be pretty fucking obvious that you as an investor must question “whether today’s capital structures, return assumptions, and demand quality justify the confidence embedded” in these valuations.

The implications of this circularity are not a joke.

If the venture capital spigot tightens, perhaps due to a macro shock or a realization that the “killer app” is delayed, the startup’s ability to pay the hyperscaler evaporates.

The “revenue” was never organic. It was a return of capital disguised as sales.

When the startup fails, the hyperscaler takes a double hit: the revenue stream disappears, and the equity investment is written down to zero.

The “False Promise” of the Backlog

This skepticism extends to the massive backlogs reported by these infrastructure providers.

Oracle, for instance, has a $455 billion backlog. In a traditional industrial context, a backlog of this magnitude would imply a decade of secured, high-margin work. In the “Golden Age of Fraud,” however, the quality of the backlog is suspect.

Gil Luria, Head of Technology Research at D.A. Davidson, argues that many of these deals are “false promises” rather than binding contracts. Quoth the Raven makes a nice argument on this point in their post “This Is What It Looks Like When Shit Hits The Fan,” a great read indeed.

Companies like OpenAI have no intention of living up to those obligations in a literal sense; they view them as “flexible arrangements” to consume capacity if and only if they need it and have the funding to pay for it.

Chanos specifically targets the solvency assumptions underpinning these backlogs.

How a startup could realistically finance a $60 billion annual commitment?

If the customer cannot pay, the backlog is not an asset; it is a liability, representing capacity that has been built for a buyer who cannot close the transaction. This mismatch between the contracted demand and the solvency-adjusted demand is the fault line upon which the short thesis rests.

Hardware Issue - GPU Economics in Decline

If the “unprofitable customer” is the demand-side risk, the deteriorating economics of the hardware itself represent the supply-side risk. The narrative of “infinite demand” for GPUs is beginning to clash with the reality of price deflation in the rental market.

The Silicon Data Index

While equity investors focus on quarterly earnings calls, the true health of the AI trade is visible in the high-frequency spot market for compute. Silicon Data Indexes, which brings “price transparency to the AI infrastructure economy,” publishes the Bloomberg Hopper GPU Rental Index (SDH100RT), tracking the hourly cost of renting Nvidia H100 chips.

The data from this index contradicts the story of scarcity.

In late 2024, the H100 rental index was trading near 3.0, driven by panic buying and the training of frontier models.

However, throughout 2025, the index has steadily declined. The index is down around 28% year-over-year.

Great Decoupling - Training vs. Inference

A nuanced analysis of the data reveals a critical “divergence” between the H100 (the newest flagship chip) and the A100 (the previous generation workhorse).

  • The H100: Prices cooled “earlier and more gradually” as the initial wave of hyperscaler training cycles tapered off and supply caught up with demand.

  • The A100: Prices climbed and peaked later, driven by “secondary market liquidity, inference use cases, and long-tail capacity utilization”.

This divergence is structurally significant.

The H100 is primarily a “training” chip. Its pricing power is a proxy for the launch of new massive models. The fact that its price is falling suggests that the cadence of new foundation model training runs may be slowing, or at least that the hardware supply has successfully flooded the zone.

The implications for ROIC (Return on Invested Capital) are serious, and I genuinely mean this.

Hyperscalers bought H100s modeling a certain rental yield (e.g., $4/hour). If the market rate clears at $2.50/hour, the payback period for a multi-billion dollar data cluster extends by years.

In a rapidly evolving technological industry (using Moore’s law), a hardware asset with a 5-year payback period is a distressed asset, because the H200 or Blackwell chips will render it obsolete before it has paid for itself.

As Silicon Data notes, “H100 pricing reflects frontier training cycles and hyperscaler demand shocks,” while “A100 pricing is driven more by... long-tail capacity utilization”.

The drop in the H100 index is a leading indicator that the “shock” phase of demand is over, and the market is entering a phase of commoditization.

Accounting Gimmicks – The Lease Loophole

In the “Golden Age of Fraud,” financial engineering is as important as software engineering.

Chanos and other forensic analysts (like us) have identified a pervasive accounting maneuver used by Big Tech to obscure the true cost of the AI arms race: the aggressive use of Finance Leases. I nice piece was released recently from Les Barclays about Conduit Financing (I recommend the read).

ASC 842 & the illusion of FCF

The standard metric for valuing mature tech companies is Free Cash Flow (FCF). Investors prize FCF because it represents the cash available to be returned to shareholders after the company has paid for its operations and its capital investments (CapEx).

Traditionally, building a data center is a CapEx event.

You spend $1 billion, your CapEx line goes up by $1 billion, and your FCF goes down by $1 billion. This imposes a discipline on spending; if you spend too much, your FCF turns negative, and your stock price suffers.

However, companies like Microsoft and Oracle have circumvented this discipline by shifting their spending to Finance Leases. Under accounting standard ASC 842, a finance lease allows a company to acquire an asset without an immediate massive cash outflow. Instead, they sign a long-term obligation to pay.

The “magic” lies in the Cash Flow Statement classification:

  • CapEx: Reduces Operating Cash Flow (via FCF calculation) immediately.

  • Finance Lease Principal Payments: Are classified as Financing Activities, not Operating Activities or Investing Activities.

This means that the principal repayments on these massive leases (which are effectively deferred CapEx) do not reduce the headline “Free Cash Flow” number that most analysts quote. It creates a massive divergence between reported FCF and the actual cash-on-cash economics of the business.

The Scale Shadow Debt

The magnitude of this accounting arbitrage is staggering. No shit.

  • Microsoft: In fiscal year 2025, Microsoft’s total finance-lease liabilities surged to $46.2 billion, a 70% increase from $27.1 billion the previous year. This is nearly $20 billion of additional infrastructure spending that did not hit the traditional CapEx line in the way investors expect.

  • Oracle: The shift is even more dramatic. Oracle recorded $4 billion in finance-lease liabilities for its most recent August quarter, a significant jump from a year prior when it had essentially zero balance in this category.

By “circumventing massive up-front cash outflows,” these companies are able to report FCF numbers that look great while their actual committed liabilities are exploding.

They are effectively borrowing from the future to preserve the optics of the present.

This doesn’t take a genius (albeit even a Layman) to know it’s a red flag. It represents the “financialization” of the business model.

When a company stops paying for assets with cash and starts paying with complex debt structures disguised as leases, it increases the leverage of the firm. If the asset (the GPU cluster) maintains its value, the leverage works.

But if the asset depreciates rapidly (as suggested by the falling H100 index) or if the customer defaults (the “unprofitable startup”), the company is left with the debt service and a stranded asset.

Microsoft vs. Oracle

While the entire sector faces these headwinds, the risks are not evenly distributed. A distinct bifurcation has emerged between the “healthy” incumbents and the “desperate” chasers. The contrast between Microsoft (MSFT) and Oracle (ORCL) provides the clearest case study of this dynamic.

Microsoft - The “Good” Behavior

Microsoft, despite its heavy use of lease accounting, possesses what Gil Luria calls “healthy” demand characteristics. Its AI build-out is anchored by a massive, diversified base of enterprise customers (Fortune 500) who are already integrated into the Azure/Office 365 ecosystem.

  • Customer Solvency: Microsoft’s customers are largely profitable corporations paying with operating cash flow, not just VC-backed startups.

  • Demand Visibility: Luria notes that Microsoft is building data centers for demand that is “already sold three years in advance”.

  • Cash Position: Microsoft has the “cash on hand” to weather a downturn or a credit cycle contraction.

Oracle - The “Bad” Behavior

Oracle, conversely, is engaged in what Luria explicitly labels “bad behavior” and an “irresponsible investment” strategy. Oracle is attempting to leapfrog into the hyperscaler tier by taking on excessive risk.

  • The Startup Gamble: Oracle is “borrowing to provide capacity for startups”. It is acting as a lender of last resort for AI capacity, signing deals with companies that Microsoft or Amazon might deem too risky.

  • Margin Erosion: The economics of these deals are poor. While Oracle’s legacy database business commands ~80% gross margins, its new AI infrastructure deals are being signed at 30-40% gross margins. Management touts the revenue growth from these deals, but ignores the margin dilution. They are trading high-quality software revenue for low-quality, capital-intensive utility revenue.

  • The 2027 Question: The skepticism peaks with Oracle’s $300 billion OpenAI partnership. Chanos notes a critical detail: the deal is “not scheduled to begin until 2027”. This two-year gap is an eternity in AI time. It suggests that this is not a firm commitment for current capacity, but a future option. If OpenAI implodes or shifts strategy before 2027, that $300 billion “backlog” vanishes.

The valuation disparity further highlights the risk. Despite the higher quality of its business, Microsoft trades at a forward P/E of ~32x, while Oracle commands a steeper ~41x. The market is paying a premium for Oracle’s riskier, lower-margin growth, a classic sign of late-cycle exuberance where “growth at any cost” is rewarded over “profitable growth.”

When the Music Stops

The convergence of these factors, unprofitable customers, falling rental yields, opaque accounting, and geopolitical fragility, paints a picture of a sector priced for perfection but built on a fault line.

Jim Chanos is not a Luddite; he is not disputing that AI will be transformative. His thesis is distinct from a technological critique. He is shorting the financial structure of the deployment, not the code itself.

The “Golden Age of Fraud” is characterized by the belief that “this time is different”, that traditional metrics of solvency, margin, and return on capital do not apply because the technology is so revolutionary.

For the investor, the “the real risks will be something... that comes out of left field”. When that event occurs, be it a credit contraction, a trade war, or a major startup failure, the music will stop. Those left holding the leases for empty data centers, built for customers who no longer exist, will face a reckoning that no amount of accounting maneuvering can hide.

Key Takeaways for a Strategist (such as you and me)

  1. Discount the Backlog: Treat multi-year, massive backlog numbers from companies like Oracle as “options” rather than “revenue.” Apply a significant discount rate to any deal that starts in 2027 or beyond.

  2. Adjust the Debt: When valuing hyperscalers, manually add finance lease liabilities back to the Enterprise Value (EV). Do not rely on screener-reported debt figures.

  3. Monitor the Spot Market: The Silicon Data H100 Index is the canary in the coal mine. If it continues to drop, expect earnings misses and guidance cuts from the hardware providers in subsequent quarters.

  4. Governance Matters: Avoid companies with high degrees of “circularity” where they are the primary investor in their own largest customers. This is a governance red flag that historically precedes massive write-downs.

The AI revolution is real. This financial bullshit built on top of it, however, is a separate entity.
