Owner Earnings and Normalized Earning Power

The first task for any owner is to convert the fiction of “Net Income” into the reality of “Owner Earnings.” Net income is what a company reports; owner earnings are what you, the owner, could theoretically pocket in cash at the end of the year without impairing the business’s long-term competitive position.

The Cash Flow Engine

The story of CATL’s cash flow is the entire story.

For a normal, fast-growing industrial, growth is a massive drain on cash. The company must spend cash today to buy inventory and finance receivables, which it will only get paid for in 60 or 90 days. Growth strangles the company of cash.

CATL is not a normal industrial. It is a bank.

An examination of its business cycle is stunning. It holds its inventory for about 84 days. It waits 61 days to get paid by its customers (like Tesla and BMW). But it waits 256 days to pay its own suppliers for the raw lithium and nickel.

This creates a permanent, negative, and growing cash balance. It is a zero-cost loan from the entire battery supply chain. This means that as CATL grows, its working capital is a significant source of cash, not a drain. This “float” adds to its owner earnings.

The Proof: Free Cash Flow Conversion

In both periods, the company generated significantly more cash from operations than it reported in accounting profit. The earnings are of exceptionally high quality, and the working capital “float” is real and durable.

Growth drivers

The market’s baseline assumption is simple: a 15% CAGR until 2050. This transforms a $150 billion market into a $1 trillion (14TWh) one.

The immediate reality is a 40% demand spike this year (1.2TWh to 1.7TWh).

But the consensus narrative misses the second-order effect. While EVs are the primary driver, Energy Storage (ESS) is the emerging parallel thesis. By 2050, ESS alone is projected to account for 3-8TWh of that total.

Two main growth drivers for battery makers and CATL:

EVs 25% CAGR (CATL aims to facilitate the generational shift from ICE to EV): +25% growth in 2025 from 800GWh to 1000GWh after +30% in 2024.

ESS 30% CAGR, an explosive growth driver (CATL focuses on reducing reliance on fossil fuel power generation and making the grid more resilient and intelligent to integrate renewables and meet the load growth demand globally in an electrifying and digitalizing world): +100% growth in 2025 from 300GWh to 600GWh after +60% in 2024.

EV growth - 25%:

The global sales volume of EVs increased from 3.3m units in 2020 to 17.8m in 2024 and is expected to increase further to 55.6m in 2030, representing a CAGR of 21% from 2024 to 2030. The global EV penetration rate is expected to expand from 20.2% in 2024 to 55.7% in 2030.

EV batteries are expected to grow about 25 percent annually to roughly 3,750 GWh by 2030 from about 950 GWh in 2024. Both NMC and LFP will see around the same growth over the period and both remain equally important for EVs.

Of course, the adoption of EVs is different by geography but NEV adoption and therefore EV battery sales are growing rapidly everywhere:

○ China: China already has around 50% of its new vehicle sales that are NEV (hybrid or EVs), a number that should reach >90% by 2030, NEVs should grow at around 18% by 2030 from 12m to 33m. EV batteries sales in China should grow 24% from 550GWh to 2000GWh over the same period.

○ Europe: The European market is the most important growth driver for the EV industry, with current NEV penetration at 17% (3m NEV) going to 60% by 2030 (12m NEV), a 26.5% CAGR leading to a 30% growth in EV battery sales over the same period from 190GWh to 900GWh.

○ US: As everyone knows, with the Trump administration, the EV transition has taken a step back (especially with the end of tax credits), but NEV penetration should continue from a lower base going from 10% today (1.5 NEV) to 32% by 2030 (6m NEV) meaning 25% growth translating to 27% growth in EV battery sales from 140GWh to 600GWh.

ESS growth - 30%:

The consensus is fixated on the EV market. The owner’s analysis must focus on the second, parallel catalyst: Energy Storage Systems (ESS).

The numbers here are not ambiguous. The ESS market has grown 80% since 2020, is projected to grow at a 30% CAGR this decade (300 GWh in 2024 to 1400 GWh by 2030), and is set to double next year to 600 GWh.

ESS is the mechanism that makes the new energy grid function. These lithium-ion batteries are vital for stabilizing power output, “peak-shaving,” and frequency regulation. They exist in two forms: Front-of-the-Meter (FTM) for grid stability and congestion relief, and Behind-the-Meter (BTM) for data centers, industrial, and residential backup. The industry is in its early stages, driven by policy, the rise of clean energy, and falling battery costs.

The duration of use (rising from 2-6 hours as costs fall).

Risks & Reverse Thesis

1. The EV Slowdown

The market’s primary fear is a simple one: 70% of CATL’s 2024 revenue came from EV battery systems. The consensus narrative points to a wall of worries, macro, tariffs, regulation, infrastructure, as catalysts for a slowdown.

This analysis is incomplete. The underlying technology is now mature and cost-competitive. It is cheaper than ICE in China and on a clear path to being so in Europe. Penetration will continue.

The real variable is not if, but at what pace and where. The immediate risk is a Chinese market slowdown as tax credits expire. This, however, simply shifts the geography of growth. Europe, with its lower penetration, becomes the main driver. This is a risk of timing, not a structural failure of the thesis.

2. The AI Sentiment Contagion

A new, abstract risk has emerged: AI. The data center buildout is pulling in ESS orders, and the market, in its confusion, is re-labeling all battery makers as “AI stocks.”

This is happening despite this trend being, for now, only marginally important to CATL’s core P&L. The market is trading on secondary signals, inflections in ESS demand, comments from Musk.

The risk here is pure sentiment contagion. CATL has been placed in the “AI winner basket.” If hyperscaler capex stumbles, CATL’s stock will be punished by association, regardless of its own fundamentals.

3. Raw Material Hikes

We return to the “margin compression” narrative. The bear case is anchored by a single number: raw materials accounted for 74% of CATL’s COGS in 2024. A sharp, swift rise in lithium or nickel will, as an accounting fact, compress the gross margin.

But the market is watching the wrong metric. The owner’s metric is net profit per unit. The company’s history shows a clear and consistent ability to protect actual profit through continuous innovation and, most critically, scale efficiency, even as input costs fluctuate.

4. The Competitive Landscape

The dominance is not in question. CATL is #1 globally, shipping 475 GWh in 2024. Its moat is funded by an R&D budget that exceeds all other battery manufacturers combined.

The market’s fear is a technological “black swan”; that a competitor will suddenly leapfrog CATL with a breakthrough in price or performance, likely solid-state batteries. This scenario conveniently ignores the facts. Given its technological leadership and massive R&D spend, CATL itself is the most probable entity to be first-to-market with that disruptive technology.

5. Geopolitical and Operational Risk

Finally, the consensus points to “overseas uncertainty.” As CATL builds its physical manufacturing footprint (Germany, Hungary, a JV in Spain, projects in Indonesia), it necessarily imports risk.

It must navigate unfamiliar laws, political shifts, and regulatory standards. This is the standard, unavoidable cost of becoming a global industrial. While the text notes a “small part” of revenue comes from the US, this is the true flashpoint. The risk is not a failure to execute in Hungary; it is the political risk of being locked out of key future markets.

Though the company faces price cuts and rebate pressure from EV manufacturers (especially given the intense competition in the Chinese market), CATL has been able to continuously launch new products and improve its manufacturing efficiency, which supports its stable unit net profit.
