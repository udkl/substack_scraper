Here is how both numbers can be right simultaneously, because this is the only frame that matters for everything that follows. Trueflation measures demand-pull inflation: what consumers are voluntarily paying for things they choose to buy. It is correct. Demand inflation is beaten. The oil shock is something categorically different. It is a supply-push tax, a forced transfer from consumers to producers administered through a disrupted chokepoint, not a signal from excess demand. The tension is not a contradiction. It is a diagnostic. The question worth spending time on is not which number is right. It is this: at what sustained oil price does the supply tax overwhelm the demand-deflationary backdrop and re-anchor inflation expectations upward?

The 5-year breakeven inflation rate, T5YIE, is the market’s real-time answer. It sits at approximately 2.67% as of writing. Elevated. Not broken. The threshold that historically triggers Fed defensiveness is 2.75% to 3.0%. Goldman estimates each sustained $10 per barrel increase adds roughly 5 basis points to core CPI.

The move from $60 baseline to current levels implies 20 to 30 basis points of supply-push CPI pressure. That is not enough to break the 2.75% threshold on its own. But it is enough to prevent the Fed from cutting, which is a different and more damaging problem than re-anchoring expectations. Watch the T5YIE, not the headline CPI print. The breakeven is forward-looking and updated daily. The CPI print is backward-looking and revised quarterly.

I want to be upfront about one methodological problem before we go any further. That 0.97 Trueflation-CPI correlation was calibrated during a period where oil was not doing what it is doing now. A supply shock of this magnitude, hitting simultaneously across crude, LNG, fertilizer, and freight, is precisely the kind of regime break that kills historical correlations. It is possible that Trueflation is telling me what I want to hear because its sample period does not include a shock like this one. I am using it anyway, because it is the best real-time demand signal available. But you should hold the conclusion at a discount.

Link One: Input Cost Repricing

The damage from sustained high oil concentrates with brutal specificity in sectors where petroleum is an input cost rather than a revenue line. And the market has spent the last four months rotating capital directly into those sectors.

The airline exposure was established in Parts 1 and 2: the fuel hedging collapse across US carriers, American Airlines at 3.5x the fuel cost sensitivity of its closest peer, Southwest flying naked after dropping its hedge program. That thesis has only gotten worse. But the part that was not covered, and the part that matters now, is the transatlantic gap.

European carriers hedged 76% to 87% of fuel exposure. Air France-KLM sits at 87%. Ryanair locked at $77 a barrel for the next twelve months, and its stock fell 2.2% while US carriers dropped 6% to 8% on the same day. O’Leary made a specific financial decision to pay for certainty. No US major made the same call.

The obvious trade is long European carriers relative to American ones. The problem is the obvious trade is only half right. The fuel hedge protects the cost side of the P&L. The revenue side is getting hit separately. European carriers have significant exposure to Middle Eastern airspace closures: Dubai, Doha, and Abu Dhabi are offline, and Gulf connecting traffic drives material revenue for IAG and Lufthansa in particular. The cleanest expression is Ryanair specifically, because its route network is almost entirely intra-European and has minimal Gulf exposure. A long Ryanair / short American Airlines pair isolates the hedging divide without taking uncompensated revenue risk on Gulf routes. One operational caveat: Ryanair trades in EUR terms while American Airlines trades in USD. If the dollar section of this note is right about USD strength, that mechanically depresses the EUR-denominated long leg. Hedge the currency or accept the basis risk.

Logistics and trucking face immediate margin compression because fuel surcharges lag real-time price increases by one to two weeks. UPS fell over 10% and FedEx over 7% on March 9. Diesel at $4.60, up $0.83 in a single week. Old Dominion Freight plunged 7.9% on March 7 alone.

The agriculture timing matters but the mechanism is misunderstood. Most US farmers locked nitrogen contracts in January and February. A March disruption does not change acreage. It changes application rates: farmers facing $520 to $550 spot nitrogen will under-apply, which means lower yield per acre on the same acreage.

The acreage rotation into soybeans is a 2027 thesis if the disruption persists. The 2026 story is a yield story. The US is a net nitrogen exporter, so the physical availability crisis is overstated, but the price increase is real and it transmits to livestock feed in Q3 and meat prices in Q4, long after the oil headlines have faded. There is no Q2 trade here. It is a late-2026 inflation input.

Goldman’s estimate that a sustained $10 per barrel increase reduces GDP growth by roughly 10 basis points is a linear approximation, and linear approximations break down at the extremes. The marginal damage from the fourth consecutive $10 increase is larger than the first, because each increment pushes more businesses past breakeven thresholds and more consumers past discretionary spending limits. At $110+ Brent, the actual GDP impact of the move from $60 is likely worse than the arithmetic implies. I cannot tell you exactly how much worse. I can tell you the direction of the error is not ambiguous.

The Value Rotation Paradox: Paying More for Worse Exposure

This is the section that should concern every allocator who thinks they rotated into safety over the last four months. They did not. They rotated into a value trap at the 98th percentile of historical valuations, and the stocks they bought are more exposed to this oil shock than the ones they sold.

Read the numbers slowly.

Walmart trades at a forward PE of 33 to 44x. Costco sits at roughly 50 to 55x. These are the “defensive” stocks the market has been buying. Meanwhile, the stocks being sold: Nvidia at 21 to 27x forward earnings. Meta at 22x. Microsoft at 23x.

The S&P 500 Consumer Staples Index is trading at a forward PE of 23.0, the 98th percentile of its own distribution since 1999. Bloomberg’s RV screen puts the exact reading at 98.41. The 27-year mean is 18.78. The median is 19.00. The current reading is 1.67 standard deviations above the mean. On February 27, 2026, ten days before this publication, the index printed 25.62, the highest forward PE in the entire 27-year dataset, surpassing even the December 2000 peak during the original dot-com rotation into staples. It has pulled back to 23.0. It is still in the 98th percentile. Investors fleeing growth for safety are buying the most expensive consumer staples in recorded history. On every traditional valuation metric.

PEG ratios make the absurdity sharper: Nvidia at 0.44x. Microsoft at 1x. Walmart at 3x. Costco at 4x. Procter and Gamble at nearly 5x.

The value rotation is a rotation from value into the most expensive stocks in the index. Nobody knows this because they are buying ETF titles rather than looking at fundamentals.

The valuation absurdity is only half the argument. The oil exposure asymmetry makes this doubly dangerous.

Consumer staples companies carry petroleum exposure through three overlapping channels: packaging (plastic resins and films), transportation (trucking companies dedicating 20% to 25% of costs to fuel), and petrochemical raw materials (surfactants, chemical inputs). I want to be careful here. These channels overlap, because the plastic packaging is itself a petrochemical product, so stacking them produces double-counting. A non-inflated estimate of petroleum-derived COGS for a diversified consumer staples company is somewhere in the 15% to 25% range, varying significantly by category: cleaning products sit at the high end due to surfactant and plastic-bottle dependency, packaged food at the low end because the primary inputs are agricultural. The range is wide enough that it matters which company you are talking about, and anyone who gives you a single number for the sector is guessing.

What is not in doubt is the pass-through lag. Pre-COVID, full cost recovery through pricing took 6 to 12 months. Post-COVID, P&G, Unilever, and Nestle all demonstrated accelerated 2-to-3-quarter pricing cycles. The lag is closer to 4 to 9 months now. Shorter, but not zero. Procter and Gamble saw chemical costs rise up to 60% year over year during the 2022 inflation peak and faces an estimated $1 billion in tariff-related costs for fiscal 2026 on top of commodity headwinds. That is the margin compression window where the damage accumulates before pricing power catches up.

The PPI-CPI lead-lag relationship shows why. PPI year-over-year sits at 0.8%, CPI at 2.4%. In 2021-2022, PPI peaked months before CPI and at roughly double the magnitude. The oil shock hits PPI first. The margin compression is the gap between when PPI moves and when companies can pass it through to CPI. That gap is the window where branded staples manufacturers bleed.

But pricing power is not the whole story. When P&G raises prices, not all consumers pay. Some switch to private label: Walmart’s Great Value, Costco’s Kirkland, Target’s up&up. Private label penetration is the actual mechanism through which branded consumer staples companies lose share in a high-input-cost environment. And Walmart and Costco’s private label margins are often higher than their branded goods margins. In a world where consumers trade down from Tide to Great Value, Walmart’s mix improves even as P&G’s deteriorates. The blanket “consumer staples lose from oil exposure” is too simple. The branded manufacturers (P&G, Colgate, Clorox) lose. The retailers with strong private label programs may actually gain on the mix shift. That does not justify paying 33 to 55x for them. But the losers within staples are the branded companies, not the retailers.

Now look at tech. Energy exposure for technology companies runs through data center electricity at roughly 3% to 8% of operating costs. Microsoft paid $800 million more in data center energy costs due to European price increases. That is a rounding error against $305 billion in annual revenue.

I covered the structural power deficit in detail in There Is No Power. The relevant point here is narrower: even if data center electricity costs double, the margin impact on a 47% operating margin business is single-digit basis points. The margin impact on a 4% operating margin business is existential.

Meta’s primary input is engineering talent and compute, not petroleum.

The margin cushion is the killer comparison, though it requires a caveat I am going to give you up front. Microsoft operates at 46.7% operating margin. Meta above 30%. Nvidia at 58.8%. Walmart at 4.1%. When oil spikes $30 per barrel, the high-margin company absorbs the hit more easily. Obviously. But margin level is not the same as margin resilience, and I want you to hold that distinction carefully. Walmart has operated at 3.5% to 5% margins for three decades through every commodity cycle. Its management team has demonstrated extraordinary cost discipline at that margin level precisely because there is no cushion and never has been. Microsoft has never been tested by a sustained energy input shock to its data center infrastructure. I think the level argument dominates at this magnitude of oil move, because no amount of cost discipline absorbs a 15% to 25% COGS spike when your margin is 4%. But I could be wrong about the margin resilience asymmetry, and if I am, the tech versus staples pair tightens faster than the valuation gap suggests.

The counter I need to address honestly: in demand destruction, tech earnings get hit on the revenue line through advertising and enterprise budget cuts while staples volumes hold because people still buy toothpaste. The 2008 and 2022 precedents support this for the first 90 days. And the supposedly cheap tech forward PEs are based on current consensus. If dollar strength takes 15% to 20% off tech earnings through FX translation, Nvidia’s 21 to 27x becomes 25 to 33x and the gap narrows. Both points are correct on a 90-day horizon. Neither is fatal on a longer one. The margin cushion survives any plausible earnings revision.

Here is how I think about the timing, because “Q2 trade” and “months four through twelve thesis” look like a contradiction until you separate them.

Leg one is a Q2 valuation trade. XLP at the 98th percentile of forward PE, with branded consumer staples names trading at PEG ratios of 3x to 5x while the names being sold trade at 0.44x to 1x, is historically extreme regardless of what oil does.

The forward PE has peaked above 21.5x four times since 1999. Each time the multiple mean-reverted within six to nine months. June 2016: peaked at 22.0x, reverted to 20.0x by November. August 2020: peaked at 22.5x, reverted to 20.2x by February 2021. December 2021: peaked at 23.0x, reverted to 19.6x by September 2022. December 2000: peaked at 23.7x, reverted to 18.1x by June 2001. In the two non-recessionary episodes where XLP price data covers the full window, XLP underperformed the S&P 500 by 14 to 21 percentage points over the subsequent twelve months. In June 2016, XLP returned negative 0.4% over the following year while the S&P returned positive 16.8%. In August 2020, XLP returned positive 10.2% while the S&P returned positive 31.3%. The multiple compressed even when the price rose, because earnings growth caught up. But relative to the index, you lost 17 to 21 points of performance by hiding in staples from a valuation peak. The current reading is more extreme than any of those four episodes. You enter in Q2 because the entry is priced for you today, not because the COGS catalyst arrives in Q2.

Leg one makes money on the valuation gap alone. Size it at full conviction.

Leg two is the months four through twelve margin compression trade: COGS exposure catching up through the 4 to 9 month pass-through lag, pricing power insufficient to fully offset the input cost spike, private-label penetration accelerating as consumers trade down. You do not need to predict the exact timing when you enter in Q2. You are buying a cheap structural option; the premium is the valuation gap itself. The trigger for full position sizing on leg two is the Q3 earnings season, September to October 2026, when the first margin misses confirm the lag is playing out. Initial position in Q2 at full conviction on leg one, half conviction on leg two. Double leg two on the first earnings confirmation. If Q3 earnings show zero margin compression despite the input cost spike, the leg two thesis is wrong and you exit that portion.

The short is on branded staples manufacturers, not on the retailers. Running XLP short gets you Walmart and Costco at the wrong side of the private label trade.

Link Two: The Dollar Wrecking Ball

The dollar is doing more damage than the oil price itself, and the two are feeding each other in a way that has not been adequately priced.

The DXY stands at approximately 99.5 to 99.7, its highest level since November 2025, having surged roughly 5% from the January lows near 95. Six weeks ago consensus was overwhelmingly bearish on the dollar. Goldman targeted EUR/USD at 1.25. The Iran war reversed that through safe-haven flows and oil-driven inflation expectations.

The BIS identified something that may make this cycle uniquely punishing. The historical negative correlation between oil and the dollar appears to have flipped positive since roughly 2021, because the US became a net energy exporter. Previously, a 10% increase in Brent knocked the dollar down about 1.5%. Now it pushes the dollar up about 1.5%. The relationship inverted. I want to be genuinely careful here: that is a 4 to 5 year sample, and the US has only been a net petroleum exporter since roughly 2019. Whether this is a genuine regime change or a short-sample artifact is honestly unknown. I am treating it as directionally correct but not gospel, and I am sizing the EM trades with that ambiguity in mind rather than pretending I have more evidence than I do.

If the correlation flip is real, commodity importers face a devastating double hit: higher dollar-denominated oil prices, and a stronger dollar increasing the local-currency cost further. India’s oil import bill in rupee terms may have increased 80% or more factoring both the crude price surge and currency depreciation. Every $1 increase in crude adds roughly $2 billion to India’s annual import bill before you adjust for the rupee. After you adjust, it is worse.

There is an offset I need to acknowledge, because ignoring it would be sloppy. A stronger dollar makes every non-petroleum import cheaper for the American consumer: apparel, electronics, food ingredients. The dollar is simultaneously raising the cost of gasoline and lowering the cost of a television. This is part of what Trueflation is picking up, and it is why oil shocks in a strong-dollar environment are less inflationary for the US than the headline crude move suggests. For EM importers, who get no such offset because their currencies are weakening, the effect is purely additive. The dollar is not reacting to the crisis. For anyone outside the United States, the dollar is the crisis.

The gold signal

Gold at $5,100 to $5,180 per ounce is rallying alongside the dollar. Most readers will skip past this. Do not.

Gold is priced in dollars. The 20-year correlation between DXY and gold spot is approximately negative 0.42, meaning gold mechanically declines when the dollar rises. When both rise simultaneously, the mechanical headwind has been absorbed and then overcome.

Put the mechanical drag in concrete terms. A 5% DXY rally, applied through a –0.42 correlation, should have pushed gold down roughly 2% to 3% on dollar strength alone. Instead gold is up 18% from its late 2024 base. Someone absorbed the full headwind and then bought enough on top of it to drive a 20-plus-point swing in net performance. You can argue about the precise dollar amount required. You cannot argue about who is doing it. Central banks purchased 863 tonnes of gold in 2025.

For the first time since 1996, gold accounts for a larger share of central bank reserves than US Treasuries. China has reduced Treasury holdings to $688.7 billion, their lowest level in 17 years, while extending gold purchases for 15 consecutive months. These are sovereign portfolio decisions made by institutions with access to every asset class in existence. They are choosing the one with no counterparty over the one with the deepest liquidity. That is not a momentum trade.

The silver market is running the same structural deficit I documented in 800 Million Ounces Short, five consecutive years of draws against a supply base that cannot respond this decade. Gold is the headline. Silver is the levered expression of the same thesis. Both are telling you the same thing about what institutional capital thinks of counterparty risk.

Four times in the past 18 years, gold and the dollar have sustained simultaneous rallies of meaningful magnitude: Q4 2008 when Lehman collapsed; August 2011 when S&P downgraded US sovereign debt; March 2020 during the COVID liquidity freeze; and February 2022 during the first week of Russia’s invasion of Ukraine.

The common thread is not the surface cause. It is the nature of the demand. Treasuries rally in a dollar-system crisis because they are the safe asset within the system. Gold rallies in a crisis of the system itself because it has no counterparty and no issuer. When you see both simultaneously, the demand is not for yield. It is for the absence of promise.

This distinction matters enormously for what the Fed can do about it. A dollar-system flight resolves when the Fed acts decisively, as in March 2020 with unlimited QE. A flight from the system does not resolve when the Fed acts, because more dollars is not the solution to distrust of dollars.

The signal is diagnostic, not predictive. It tells you what kind of crisis you are in, not when it ends. When gold and the dollar both rally, the market is pricing a system-level stress event, not a cyclical one. System-level stress events do not resolve on cyclical timelines. I am sizing the positions in this note accordingly.

Link Three: Asia’s $100 Oil Nightmare

Before any other country in Asia matters, China matters, because China is the variable that determines whether everything below is severe or catastrophic.

Beijing holds approximately 1.47 billion barrels in combined commercial and strategic petroleum storage. There are roughly 200 million barrels of sanctioned Iranian crude floating at sea on VLCC tankers, effectively pre-positioned for Chinese refiners who have been the primary buyers of discounted Iranian product for three years. Drawing down floating inventory does not require entering the spot market, does not move the Brent price, and does not show up in the Kpler import data that everyone is watching. China can remain effectively invisible as a buyer for approximately four months while drawing these reserves.

That invisibility is the price cap. If China draws floating storage quietly, the physical crude market never tightens as dramatically as the Hormuz headlines imply. Brent at $85 to $95 is defensible with Chinese spot demand suppressed. Every EM importer below faces a manageable, painful but survivable energy shock.

The week China stops drawing floating inventory and starts buying spot crude aggressively is the week $85 Brent becomes a $95 floor and the damage in every section below accelerates. The signal: watch Kpler’s weekly Chinese crude import data, not Hormuz transit counts. Kpler measures what arrives in Chinese ports. Floating storage drawdown does not show up there. A sudden jump in spot purchases does. That data series is more important for this trade than anything else available publicly.

Why would China stop drawing and start buying spot? Two reasons. First, if the conflict extends past four months, the floating inventory is exhausted and China becomes a spot buyer of necessity. Second, if Ras Laffan’s LNG capacity comes offline for longer than currently priced, China faces an LNG shortage that crude reserves cannot solve. The LNG constraint is what converts China from a price-stabilizing force into a price-destabilizing one, and it is the scenario the market has most severely underpriced.

Japan carries the highest risk score among major economies. It imports 87% of total energy from fossil fuels and relies on the Middle East for roughly 75% of oil needs. The Nikkei 225 fell over 5%, touching 7% intraday, on March 9. Japan holds approximately 150 days of strategic petroleum reserves. That sounds comfortable. It should not.

Japan’s total LNG storage capacity is approximately 8.1 million tonnes. Working inventory, what is actually available for immediate draw, sits at roughly 4.4 million tonnes. Against annual consumption of approximately 66 million tonnes, that working inventory represents around 3.5 weeks of cover. There is no IEA emergency sharing mechanism for natural gas. The IEA formally acknowledges that no framework for collective action in response to a gas supply disruption exists. Oil has a 90-day coordinated reserve architecture built after 1973. Gas has nothing. Japan’s 150 days of oil reserves exist inside a system designed to deploy them. Japan’s 3.5 weeks of LNG cover exist inside no system at all.

Ras Laffan processes 77 million tonnes per annum, roughly 19% of global LNG trade. Japan’s direct Qatar imports have fallen to approximately 4.4% of its total LNG supply following the expiration of legacy contracts, so the first-order exposure looks manageable. It is not. Sixty-four percent of global LNG trades on long-term contracts. The remaining 36% trades spot or short-term. When 19% of global supply is threatened, the spot market does not reprice linearly. It reprices the way JKM repriced in March 2022, when the spot benchmark hit $84.76 per MMBtu. Japan is a price-taker in a spot market that goes vertical when supply is constrained, and its contract coverage is thinner than it was five years ago.

This is where the China section above connects. If Ras Laffan stays online, China draws floating crude reserves quietly and the LNG market stays orderly. If Ras Laffan comes offline for longer than currently priced, China pivots from price-stabilizing crude drawdowns to aggressive spot LNG buying, and Japan faces a bidding war against the world’s largest energy importer with 3.5 weeks of cover and no emergency sharing architecture to fall back on. The market has not figured out that Japan’s vulnerability is gas, not oil. Short Japanese utilities with heavy LNG import dependency through EWJ puts, or for single-stock access, short Tokyo Gas and JERA-exposed names on the LNG supply crunch.

South Korea’s KOSPI suffered its worst crash since 2008, plunging up to 12% intraday on March 4 and triggering circuit breakers. Net oil imports represent 2.7% of GDP, the highest ratio among major Asian economies. The energy-intensive export industries, semiconductors, shipbuilding, petrochemicals, automobiles, steel, face simultaneous input cost shocks and demand destruction in their end markets, while the won depreciates against the dollar at the same time. The double hit from the dollar section, playing out in real time in Seoul.

India imports approximately 85% of its crude oil, 4.2 million barrels per day, with 46% to 50% transiting Hormuz. India holds less than 30 days of strategic reserves. In rupee terms, the oil import bill has increased somewhere north of 80%. India’s current account deficit was already under pressure. Multiply that by the dollar move and you have a balance-of-payments problem, not just an energy problem.

ING’s rule of thumb: every 10% oil price rise deteriorates EM current accounts by 40 to 60 basis points and raises CPI by about 0.2 points. The IMF finds that oil shocks hit EM core inflation more than twice as hard as advanced economies.

The watchlist beyond the three above is short and obvious: Pakistan, which sources 99% of LNG imports from Qatar and the UAE; Egypt and Turkey, both running structural current account deficits into the shock. These are not trade ideas. They are the countries that show up in headlines over the next 90 days if Brent holds.

Trade expression: short EWY (South Korea, export-heavy, energy-intensive, dollar-funded). Short INDA on duration if the rupee break accelerates and the current account deficit blows out. Long USD/INR and USD/KRW as direct expressions of the double-hit thesis. Third-week trades, not first-week trades.

Link Four: Private Credit, the $3.5 Trillion Pressure Cooker

The oil shock does not arrive into a healthy credit market.

Fitch reported US private credit defaults hit a record 9.2% in 2025, 38 defaults among 28 borrowers in a monitored universe of 302 companies, up from 8.1% in 2024. Most concentrated among smaller issuers with EBITDA below $25 million on floating-rate structures with minimal interest rate hedges. KBRA identified a record 61 borrowers rated CCC-, and found that nearly 30% of companies with near-term maturities were carrying leverage above 10x or negative EBITDA. UBS projected in November 2025 that private credit defaults could climb by an additional 3 percentage points in 2026. That was before the oil shock.

BCRED, Blackstone’s $82 billion private credit fund, reported record 7.9% redemption requests in Q1 2026, roughly $3.8 billion, well above its typical 5% quarterly cap.

Blackstone injected $400 million of firm capital to honor requests. Across non-traded BDCs with over $1 billion in assets, investors requested more than $2.9 billion in Q4 2025 redemptions, a 200% increase from the prior quarter.

PIK interest now accounts for over 8% of total BDC income, double the pre-COVID level. When borrowers cannot service their debt with cash and are paying with IOUs instead, that is not temporary stress. That is a late-cycle indicator.

Public credit spreads offer minimal cushion. The ICE BofA US High Yield Index OAS stands at approximately 284 basis points, in the tightest 5% of readings over the past 25 years against a 20-year average of 490. BBB investment grade spreads sit near 98 basis points against a 20-year average of 170.

The precedent everyone reaches for is the 2014 to 2016 oil collapse, where energy high-yield defaults peaked at 23.3%. But that was oil crashing to $26, not spiking to $110. Energy companies are printing money at these prices, not going bankrupt. The relevant precedent is what the New York Fed found: oil price movements explained roughly 50% of yield spread increases for non-energy firms. Not because those firms had energy exposure. Because investors sold liquid non-energy bonds to cover losses elsewhere, and the forced selling widened spreads across the entire credit complex regardless of credit quality.

Here is the part everyone gets wrong about the private credit exposure. The borrower base is not energy companies. It is healthcare services, tech-adjacent businesses, consumer-facing companies. Oil does not hit them the way it hits an airline. It hits them through the back door: their customers spend less because gasoline is eating discretionary budgets, their enterprise clients freeze projects, and the M&A exits they were counting on to refinance dry up because buyers pull back. That takes quarters to show up in default rates, not weeks. Which is why the credit section will be the last link in the chain to arrive and the one nobody is positioned for when it does.

The question I keep returning to is whether this is 2008 or 2015. Less leverage sits on major bank balance sheets this time. “Less than 2008” is a low bar, but it is a meaningful one. The private credit market’s opacity means we have less visibility into where the bodies are buried than in the mortgage market, where at least the structures were standardized.

Trade expression: CDX.NA.HY is the hedging instrument, but the risk is timing. If credit deterioration takes six months to show up in spreads, you have paid six months of carry for a position that is eventually right but ruinously early. Size it as a hedge on an existing portfolio, not a standalone bet. BDC names to watch for NAV discount widening: ARCC, BXSL, FSK, and GSBD. When NAV discounts widen past 10%, the marks are stale and the real losses are larger than reported. The liquidity spillover is the second-order opportunity: when BDC investors sell liquid high-grade bonds to meet redemptions, investment-grade credit spreads widen for reasons unrelated to underlying credit quality. The expression is protective rather than directional.

Link Five: The Consumer at Stall Speed and the Fed Trap

Retail sales fell 0.2% in January 2026, the first decline since October 2025. That was before the oil shock hit. Those retail sales numbers are not adjusted for inflation. If inflation is running at 3%, retail sales declining in nominal terms means real spending is falling faster than the headline suggests. And if the Trueflation caveat from the intro is right (that the demand-supply diagnostic breaks down under a shock this large) the true inflation rate may be higher than 3%, which means the real spending decline is worse still.

The concentration of spending power is what makes this structurally dangerous. The top 10% of earners drove 49.2% of consumer spending in Q2 2025 per Moody’s Analytics, the highest level in series history dating to 1989. The typical stockholder in the top 10% holds $1.1 million in equities, and 43% of all American household financial assets are now in stocks, the highest share ever recorded. The top leg of the K depends on the wealth effect, which depends on the stock market, which cannot rally without the mega caps that are actively being sold by the value rotation. Four hundred and fifty stocks have been offsetting the decline in fifty. That has kept the index rangebound. It cannot drive a breakout.

The bottom leg is getting hit by gasoline as a regressive tax. Every sustained $10 per barrel increase costs the average household roughly $450 per year (Moody’s Mark Zandi). The move from $60 to $100 is four increments of that, $1,800 per household annualized. Bank of America data shows lower-income household spending growing just 0.6% year over year versus 2.6% for higher-income. Lower-income households now dedicate 59% to 61% of spending to essentials, above pre-pandemic levels.

The personal savings rate sits at 3.6% of disposable income, half its 7.5% historical average and barely above the 2.2% low printed in June 2022. There is no cushion left to absorb the hit.

Gasoline at $3.45 nationally and $4.81 in California as of last week, both already stale against $110 Brent. The retail pump lags crude by two to three weeks. National gasoline is heading toward $4.00 to $4.50 and California toward $5.50+ if Brent holds.

Aggregate the math carefully, because stacking these channels additively produces a number an investment committee can tear apart.

Two primary channels. One multiplier. One pre-existing condition.

Primary channel one is gasoline as regressive transfer, operating on the bottom income cohort. At $85 to $100 Brent, national pump prices reach $4.00 to $4.50, representing an annualized cost of approximately $1,200 to $1,800 per lower-income household with no discretionary cushion. This channel transmits within weeks. In PCE terms: approximately 0.3% to 0.5% drag on aggregate consumer spending.

Primary channel two is wealth effect deterioration, operating on the top income cohort. A 10% equity market decline reduces spending by roughly 0.5% of PCE for this cohort, based on the standard Manski wealth effect coefficient. The equity decline is partial and ongoing. The wealth effect is lagged by one to two quarters. In PCE terms: approximately 0.2% to 0.4% additional drag, arriving in Q3 rather than Q2.

These two channels are genuinely independent because they operate on different income cohorts through different mechanisms. Adding them is legitimate. Combined: 0.5% to 0.9% PCE drag.

The credit tightening is not a third additive channel. It is a multiplier on the primary ones: slower hiring depresses labor income and amplifies channel one; frozen enterprise project spending compresses corporate margins and amplifies channel two through the wealth effect. The magnitude of the multiplier is not directly observable in real time, and I am not going to invent a precise number to make the arithmetic feel cleaner than it is.

What is observable: the Fed’s Senior Loan Officer Survey already showed net tightening in lending standards for three consecutive quarters before the oil shock. KBRA’s record 61 CCC- borrowers and the BDC redemption data from Link Four confirm the credit environment was deteriorating before crude moved.

The amplifier is not hypothetical. It is running. The unamplified range is 0.5% to 0.9% PCE drag. The amplified range is wider and skews higher, but the floor does not change materially because credit tightening operates with a longer lag than either primary channel. Call it 0.6% to 1.2%, with the width of the range reflecting genuine uncertainty about the multiplier rather than false precision about it.

The February payrolls print, negative 55,000 to 64,000 adjusted for the Kaiser Permanente strike, is not an oil-shock channel. It pre-dates the conflict. It is the pre-existing condition that lowers the threshold at which the primary channels trigger a spending contraction.

The GDP arithmetic: consumer spending is approximately 70% of GDP. A 0.6% to 1.2% PCE drag translates to a 0.4% to 0.8% GDP headwind. Against a 2% baseline, you get 1.2% to 1.6% growth. Against the pre-existing labor weakness, you get stall speed. Not a textbook recession. Stall speed. Which is worse for policy, as I will explain in a moment.

The Fed trap, properly diagnosed

Every strategist on the street has written the same paragraph. Inflation argues for holding, labor market deterioration argues for cutting, the Fed is trapped between them. That framing is not wrong. It just describes the symptoms and mistakes them for the disease.

The real trap operates one level deeper.

The post-Volcker Federal Reserve is not primarily a rate-setting body. It is a commitment device. Forty years of anchored inflation expectations rest on a single foundational promise purchased during 1979 to 1982, roughly 10% unemployment and two back-to-back recessions, establishing one institutional fact: when forced to choose between growth and price stability, the Fed chooses price stability. Every basis point of credibility deployed since is interest on that original deposit.

In a clean recession, the Fed cuts without touching that deposit. Demand destruction kills inflation organically. The mandate conflict resolves itself. The credibility framework survives because it was never actually tested.

In stall speed with 5-year breakevens at 2.67%, cutting sends a different signal entirely. That the Volcker commitment has an asterisk. That when growth gets uncomfortable enough, the Fed blinks. That signal cannot be recalled once transmitted. Inflation expectations reprice permanently upward, not because current inflation warrants it, but because every economic actor has updated their model of what the Fed does next time. The credibility infrastructure does not collapse. It corrodes. The next inflation episode becomes structurally harder to fight because the commitment is no longer fully believed.

That is why stall speed is more dangerous for monetary policy than a clean recession. A recession resolves the mandate conflict and gives the Fed permission to act without consequence. Stall speed forces it to choose between acting and preserving the credibility that makes acting effective. An intertemporal trap, not a cyclical one.

In every prior episode where this dilemma appeared, there was a second actor with a functional lever. Congress. Fiscal stimulus absorbed demand weakness and gave the Fed operational room to hold rates without the labor market deteriorating past political tolerance.

That backstop is gone in 2026, and not because Congress is unwilling.

The 10-year yield rose on weak employment data. Inflation expectations push the 10-year regardless of payrolls. A rising 10-year on weak payrolls is a fiscal signal. The bond market is pricing war costs into the term premium, which the New York Fed’s ACM model puts at 64 basis points and rising, near its highest level since 2014, against a deficit that was structurally unsustainable before the first strike on Iranian infrastructure. The marginal buyer of Treasuries is less reliable than at any point in the past decade. The bid is thinner.

Congress cannot pass stimulus that the bond market will not immediately punish through yield increases that negate it. The fiscal multiplier is being compressed in real time by the term premium. The backstop that made prior Fed credibility preservation viable is structurally unavailable.

The Fed is therefore not choosing between two mandates. It is the last actor with a functional lever in a system where every other lever is either broken or being withdrawn. And the specific way its lever is broken means deploying it risks permanently impairing the institution’s future effectiveness.

That is the genuine trap. The last resort cannot act without becoming less capable of acting next time.

The signal to watch is not the next CPI print. It is the unemployment rate relative to the Fed’s published 4.4% threshold. When unemployment crosses that level in a supply-shock environment, the credibility cost of cutting stops being theoretical. The Fed must choose visibly and publicly. That is the moment the trap closes.

The Kill Switches and What to Do About It

I am not going to summarize what I just wrote. You need to know what breaks the thesis and what to do if it holds.

What makes this wrong.

The fastest kill switch is a ceasefire and Hormuz reopening. If that happens, Brent retraces to $70 to $75 within weeks, the geopolitical premium evaporates, and every second-order effect described above becomes a theoretical exercise. The value rotation paradox survives on its own terms, because the valuation argument does not depend on oil, but it loses its near-term catalyst.

I want to give you an honest probability on this rather than a precise one I cannot defend. I do not have a clean number. Anyone giving you one with two decimal places, including any prediction market, is confusing confidence with precision. What I can tell you is what the market is actually pricing: the crude options curve has significant backwardation embedded in Q2 and Q3 contracts without the sharp convergence you would expect if traders were pricing a near-term resolution. The front-month implied vol premium over the back end is consistent with a market that thinks this lasts at least through mid-Q2. That is a softer signal than a point estimate, but it is a more honest one. Historical precedent for US military confrontations with Iran, from Operation Praying Mantis in 1988 to the post-Soleimani episode in 2020, shows fast resolution for contained strike exchanges. A direct strike on Iranian nuclear and energy infrastructure is categorically different. There is no clean comparable. Size accordingly.

The second kill switch is an OPEC+ flood: if Saudi Arabia moves from the 206,000 bpd compromise to a genuine 500,000+ bpd surge, and if that spare capacity is real rather than nameplate fiction, the physical market rebalances faster than the political situation resolves. This does not collapse the thesis as cleanly as a ceasefire because the EM current account damage and the dollar move persist. But it caps oil at $80 to $85, which means the staples COGS thesis weakens, the airline thesis moderates, and the credit thesis loses its primary transmission mechanism. The “if the spare capacity is real” clause is doing a lot of work here. The gap between OPEC’s stated and actual spare capacity is not directly observable.

The third kill switch is slower: US shale producers hedge aggressively at $80+ and lock in H2 2026 production increases. This does not resolve the current crisis. It caps the duration. The trades in this note are Q2 trades. Do not marry them.

What to do if the thesis holds.

Ranked by conviction.

Highest conviction: the value rotation into XLP at the 98th percentile is wrong on a 6-to-12-month horizon. The valuation gap is historically extreme, the COGS exposure is asymmetric, and the margin structure argument survives every counter I can construct. This is true even if oil retraces to $80. Short branded staples manufacturers, P&G, Colgate, Clorox, not the retailers.

High conviction: the dollar-oil correlation flip hits EM importers through a double channel that almost nobody is pricing. Short EWY and INDA on duration in weeks two and three.

Moderate conviction: the gold/dollar simultaneous rally signal says the crisis is not priced. The historical sample is four observations. That is not enough for statistical confidence but it is enough to take seriously as a regime indicator, particularly given the central bank reserve data corroborating the same thesis from a different direction.

Lower conviction but positive expected value: CDX.NA.HY protection as a portfolio hedge. The thesis is sound, the timing is unknowable, the carry cost is real. Size it as insurance. Short American Airlines on duration, week three or four, not week one. Long US LNG exporters on the JKM-Henry Hub spread, contingent on Ras Laffan damage assessment.

The duration distinction matters more than anything else in this note. On a 90-day horizon, staples probably hold up relatively. On a 6-to-12-month horizon, they do not. Position for the horizon you actually have.

Five Bloomberg screens that should be running: ECFC for EM GDP and CPI revisions. CDSW and WCDS for credit spread widening in real time. SPLC for supply chain dependencies on Middle Eastern inputs. PORT for the stress test (”What if Brent hits $120?”). And the EQS screen that tells you which names actually pass the valuation filter versus the ones you assume pass it.

The question is not whether you believe the thesis. It is whether you have sized the positions to survive being wrong about the timing while being right about the direction.

Every framework has a kill switch. These are mine. Now go find yours.
