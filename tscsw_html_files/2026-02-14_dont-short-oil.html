The Anatomy of a “Glut”

The IEA’s headline number for 2025 is a 477 million barrel inventory build, roughly 1.3 mb/d on average. That sounds terrifying. It’s also deeply misleading once you decompose it, which the consensus has shown remarkably little interest in doing.

Line 1: China’s “commercial” stockpiling, 111 million barrels that will never see the market

Chinese crude stocks built by 111 million barrels in 2025, per the IEA’s own data. China was stockpiling at nearly 1 million b/d throughout the year (confirmed independently by Gunvor’s Frederic Lasserre at APPEC and by the EIA at ~900,000 b/d through August). Beijing mandated state companies to purchase up to 140 million barrels for strategic reserves between July 2025 and March 2026, according to Energy Aspects. Reuters’ Clyde Russell documented the same pattern independently, showing that Chinese surplus crude flowing to storage tracked between 500,000 and 1.1 million b/d throughout 2025, with volumes adjusting inversely to price levels. Stillwater Associates’ comparative analysis of US and Chinese SPR strategies reached the same conclusion: China has been “aggressively using market opportunities to accelerate strategic crude oil stockpiling,” absorbing what would otherwise appear as surplus supply.

A new law passed in early 2025 formally merged strategic and commercial storage categories, making private firms “long-term strategic storage partners for the government,” as the IEA’s own Toril Bosoni acknowledged.

Here is the part that matters: China has adhered to a “fill-and-hold” doctrine across the entire history of its SPR. One test release. Ever. A measly 7.38 million barrels in late 2021. Total reserves now sit at roughly 1.2-1.3 billion barrels, providing 75-81 days of import cover. Fill rate at approximately 56%, with 11 new storage sites adding 169 million barrels of capacity.

I want to be very precise about what I’m saying here. These barrels are locked away for national security: war preparation, sanctions insurance, hedging against a Strait of Malacca blockade scenario. As Dr. Anas Alhajji put it on MacroVoices last week, everything China is doing “looks like it’s a preparation for either war or sanctions.” And he’s right. Look at the pattern. They’re stockpiling oil, natural gas, and coal simultaneously, all to record highs. They’re investing heavily in domestic upstream production regardless of cost. They’re building out storage batteries at an extraordinary rate despite the expense. You cannot argue with a strategic decision using commercial logic. When you’re talking about regime survival, financial arguments “do not make any sense,” as Alhajji correctly noted.

Classifying 111 million barrels of Chinese strategic reserves as commercial surplus fundamentally mischaracterises the nature of the demand. These are barrels purchased under a state security mandate, stored in facilities governed by military planning timelines, and historically released exactly once in 7.38 million barrel quantities. Including them in a surplus calculation without adjustment produces a headline figure that overstates available supply by roughly a quarter.

Even the EIA now concedes the point. Its February 2026 Short-Term Energy Outlook explicitly states that “a large portion of oil inventory builds last year were in strategic stockpiles in China, which limited downward price pressures because these builds acted as a source of demand.” The EIA assumes China will continue building at approximately 1.0 million b/d through 2026. Read that carefully: the US government’s own energy agency is telling you that the China stockpile is demand, not surplus, and that it will continue absorbing barrels at nearly the same pace this year. The EIA is quietly saying what I’m saying loudly, yet the sell-side continues to include these barrels in its surplus calculations without adjustment.

The obvious counterargument is that Beijing could reverse course and release strategic barrels, particularly if domestic economic conditions deteriorate to the point where cheap energy becomes a stimulus tool. This deserves honest engagement. China’s GDP growth has been slowing, and property sector weakness persists.

Could Beijing sell SPR crude to refiners at a discount to stimulate industrial activity? In principle, yes. But the institutional architecture works against it. China’s SPR is managed by the National Food and Strategic Reserves Administration under a centralised, security-first mandate. Xi Jinping’s consolidation of authority has moved reserve management further from market-responsive agencies and closer to military planning.

The 2025 law merging strategic and commercial categories was designed to increase state control, not market flexibility. And the sole historical test release, 7.38 million barrels in 2021, was described by the Oxford Institute for Energy Studies as “a test of mechanisms rather than a show of market might.” Would a genuine economic crisis override these institutional barriers? Perhaps.

But the scenario in which Beijing simultaneously faces economic distress severe enough to trigger SPR releases AND global oil demand is strong enough to sustain the surplus the IEA projects is internally contradictory. If China’s economy is that weak, global demand collapses and the surplus narrative shifts from “manufactured” to “demand destruction,” which is a different thesis entirely.

Line 2: Oil on water, 248 million barrels of shipping route arithmetic

Oil on water increased by 248 million barrels in 2025, with 72% linked to sanctioned barrels, per the IEA. A record 1.4 billion barrels of crude sitting on the world’s oceans, according to Bloomberg. The bears triumphantly point to this as evidence of oversupply.

The bearish interpretation misreads what the data actually shows, and the error is structural rather than subtle.

The Brazil example illustrates the distortion clearly. Total Brazilian oil exports decreased in 2025, but exports to China increased substantially at the expense of shipments to the United States and Europe, per Kpler shipping data. The shipping distance from Brazil to China is roughly three times the distance to the US and twice the distance to Europe. So more oil appears to be “on water” for longer periods without any actual change in supply. It’s an arithmetic illusion created by trade route reconfiguration.

Canada’s new Trans Mountain Pipeline sends approximately 400,000 b/d to the West Coast and Asia. That oil previously moved through US pipelines where it was invisible to waterborne tracking. Now it shows up as “oil on water.” Did supply increase? No. Did barrels on the ocean increase? Yes. Is this bearish? Only if you’re confusing location with quantity.

UBS confirmed the punchline: “the recent increase in oil-on-water levels has not translated into a build-up in on-land inventories. In fact, data from the IEA indicate a decline in crude stored in tanks on land.”

Read that again. On-land inventories are declining. The increase is substantially explained by longer shipping routes rather than incremental supply, yet it is being counted barrel-for-barrel as surplus.

The strongest counterargument is that floating storage functions as deferred supply: barrels on water are barrels that will eventually arrive and displace spot purchases. If on-land inventories are declining partly because floating deliveries are meeting demand, then the waterborne barrels are economically available, just with a longer lead time.

This is a fair point, and it is why I discount only half the oil-on-water increase rather than the full amount. The portion that reflects genuine floating inventory (unsold cargoes, contango-driven storage plays) is real supply. But the portion that reflects longer voyage distances on barrels already sold to end-users (the Brazil-to-China rerouting, the Trans Mountain redirection) is not surplus in any economically meaningful sense. It is the same barrel taking a longer route to the same refinery. The distinction matters, and aggregate waterborne inventory data does not capture it.

Line 3: The IEA’s chronic demand blindness

In May 2025, the IEA quietly revised its historical demand estimates upward by 330,000 b/d across 2022-2024, erasing roughly 295 million barrels of apparent surplus. Poof. What had looked like a 220 million barrel stockbuild was retroactively turned into a ~75 million barrel drawdown.

This is not an anomaly. The IEA has underestimated demand growth for 18 consecutive years. Eighteen. At some point, a pattern this consistent stops being honest error and starts looking like institutional bias.

For context, Goehring & Rozencwajg documented the same phenomenon during 2003-2007, when the IEA projected surpluses every single year that never materialised. Oil went from $30 to $147. The demand drivers were structurally different then, with China’s industrialisation boom providing an unusually powerful tailwind, but the forecasting failure was identical: the IEA’s data pipeline systematically missed non-OECD consumption growth. The analysts projecting surpluses were wrong the entire way up.

For 2025, the IEA initially forecast US demand growth at roughly 60,000 b/d. The actual numbers came in several multiples higher. Alhajji described this on MacroVoices with visible exasperation: the IEA was ultimately “forced to upgrade its forecast” from 60,000 to 170,000 b/d, and even that revised figure remained below actual consumption. They manufactured a surplus on a spreadsheet by assuming demand that was far too low, and when reality forced a correction, they just shrugged and published a new number.

Even now, in February 2026, the IEA has cut its 2026 demand growth forecast from 930 kb/d to 850 kb/d, continuing the exact pattern of demand pessimism that it has historically reversed in every single year for nearly two decades.

Meanwhile, OPEC’s demand growth forecast sits at 1.4 mb/d for 2026. The gap between the IEA and OPEC forecasts is approximately 550 kb/d, one of the widest divergences in recent history. That 550 kb/d gap, applied over a year, is roughly 200 million barrels of disagreement about whether a surplus even exists.

A note of intellectual honesty: OPEC’s forecasting record deserves the same scrutiny I’m applying to the IEA. As the Baker Institute documented in their 2024 analysis of forecast divergences, OPEC has shifted in recent years from historically conservative demand estimates to systematically optimistic ones, particularly on China. They started 2024 forecasting demand growth of 2.25 mb/d and revised it down to 1.61 mb/d by December, a pattern of high-to-low revision that has persisted for three consecutive years. If the IEA is the institutionally bearish forecaster, OPEC is the institutionally bullish one, and both biases serve their respective constituencies. The truth almost certainly sits between the two, which still leaves a surplus meaningfully smaller than the IEA’s headline figure, but probably larger than OPEC’s implied balance.

Line 4: The ledger rebalanced

So let’s do the exercise that the sell-side refuses to do. Start with the IEA’s terrifying 477 million barrel headline build.

Subtract the 111 million barrels that went to China’s strategic reserves and will never see the open market. That gets you to 366 million barrels.

Subtract a conservative 120-150 million barrels from the oil-on-water distortion. I’m not claiming the entire 248 million barrel increase is phantom, but the evidence from UBS, from the Brazil-China rerouting data, and from the Trans Mountain Pipeline effect suggests that at minimum half of it reflects longer shipping distances rather than new supply. Call it 130 million barrels at the midpoint. That brings you to roughly 236 million barrels.

Now consider the IEA’s demand methodology. The structural reason the IEA chronically underestimates demand is well documented: non-OECD reporting lags. The IEA relies on OECD member data for approximately 55% of global consumption in near-real-time, but non-OECD data (which accounts for 100% of current demand growth, per the IEA’s own February report) arrives with a 3-6 month lag and is frequently revised upward. This is not a random error. It is the structural artefact of the data pipeline described above.

The pattern of forced upward revisions documented above applies equally to 2025-2026 figures. The IEA’s 2026 demand growth forecast has fluctuated from 690,000 b/d (April 2025 first estimate), to 930,000 b/d (January 2026), to 850,000 b/d (February 2026). This volatility in a forecast that should be stabilising as more data arrives tells you the underlying model is struggling.

The IEA’s revision of 2022-2024 data upward by 330,000 b/d, erasing roughly 295 million barrels of apparent surplus, was not an anomaly. It was the same structural lag correcting itself after the fact, as it does every cycle. I cannot quantify with precision how large the 2025-2026 revision will be. But I can bound it. The IEA’s May 2025 revision erased 330,000 b/d across three years. The 2019 revision added approximately 200,000 b/d. The 2017 cycle corrected by roughly 150,000 b/d. A conservative assumption, using the lower end of historical revision magnitudes, suggests 150,000 to 300,000 b/d of demand currently missing from the 2025-2026 figures. Annualised at the midpoint, that is roughly 80 million barrels. The structural cause of the bias (non-OECD reporting lags covering 45% of global consumption) has not changed. The 2025-2026 demand figure is almost certainly understated. The magnitude is uncertain within a quantifiable range; the direction is not.

Adding the demand revision midpoint of ~80 million barrels to the previous adjustments brings the remaining surplus to roughly 155 million barrels, or about 425 kb/d averaged over the year. Using the conservative end of each adjustment (low demand revision, low oil-on-water correction) still produces a surplus of roughly 200 million barrels, or 550 kb/d. Using the aggressive end produces roughly 110 million barrels, or 300 kb/d. The plausible range for the “real” surplus sitting in conventional commercial storage at accessible hubs is 300 to 550 kb/d.

Not trivial, but a world away from the 1.3 mb/d monster the headlines describe. The difference between a 1.3 mb/d surplus and one in the 300-550 kb/d range is the difference between a market that should be collapsing and one that’s roughly balanced with a modest buffer, which is exactly how prices are behaving.

I want to be transparent about the uncertainty here. The China SPR adjustment is high-confidence. The oil-on-water adjustment is medium-confidence, you can argue about the exact magnitude.The demand revision assumption is a directional bet informed by 18 years of consistency, not a quantified certainty. But even if I’m generous to the bears and use the low end of every adjustment, the surplus is still less than half the headline figure.

The IEA itself concedes that “deteriorating market transparency” is “further clouding the picture.” You don’t say.

The Supply Disruptions Nobody Is Pricing

Even if you take the surplus at face value, and you shouldn’t, a cascade of supply disruptions has been quietly dismantling it throughout late 2025 and early 2026.

Let me walk you through the damage.

Kazakhstan: the disruption with geopolitical fingerprints all over it

On November 29, 2025, a naval drone strike destroyed SPM-2 at the CPC terminal in Yuzhnaya Ozereevka. This wasn’t a pinprick. 21 of 45 scheduled January shipments were cancelled. CPC Blend cargo premiums surged to $1.20/bbl over Dated Brent, roughly double normal levels. Kazakhstan’s oil production fell approximately 6% month-over-month in December.

Alhajji’s analysis on this is worth paying close attention to. The CPC terminal handles roughly 1.6 million b/d of Kazakh exports. Russia also exports about 100,000 b/d from the facility. Curiously, Russia stopped exporting from the terminal about 10 days before the attack. Then the drones hit. Then a nearby gas processing facility on the Russian-Kazakh border was struck. Then empty tankers heading to load at CPC were attacked in the Black Sea.

The attribution remains unconfirmed. The Ukrainian government has not claimed the strikes, and the circumstantial timeline is suggestive but inconclusive. What I am confident about is the barrel impact, which is what matters for this analysis: we lost 700,000-900,000 b/d from Kazakhstan for over two and a half months, and full restoration has been delayed to Q2 2026 at the earliest.

The Tengiz field, Chevron’s $48 billion expansion centrepiece targeting 1% of global output, had recovered to approximately 60% of peak capacity by early February, with full output targeted for late February. Then in January, Tengiz suffered a fire that knocked out another 120,000+ b/d on top of the CPC losses. Less than half of normal production had been restored by early February.

Winter Storm Fern: 2 million barrels per day, gone in a day

On January 24, 2026, Winter Storm Fern ripped through the US energy patch, knocking out approximately 2 million b/d at peak, fully 15% of US national output. Most production was restored within a week, but the cumulative loss was roughly 6.3 million barrels per Wood Mackenzie. This happened at precisely the moment when surplus projections claimed the market was drowning in crude.

The same storm, before hitting the US, passed through the Middle East including Iran, cutting off Iranian natural gas exports to Iraq. Iraq, suddenly without gas for power generation, was forced to divert crude oil to domestic electricity production. Iraqi exports will not increase as a result, and this constraint persists until an alternative gas supply is arranged, which is to say, indefinitely.

Russia’s refining problem

Ukrainian drone strikes have hit 21 of 38 large Russian refineries since 2024. Russia’s refining throughput fell from ~5.5 to ~5.0-5.1 mb/d during peak strikes.

A gasoline export ban has been extended through March 2026. Russia’s crude output fell by a combined 170,000 b/d in the last two months of 2025 alone, per Energy Intelligence.

According to Kepler data, OPEC+ supply declined by 2.7 million barrels a day in January 2026, and that figure does not include the US losses from the winter storm.

India’s Russian crude halt: the 1 million b/d nobody has modelled

On February 2, Trump announced a trade deal with India in which New Delhi would stop importing Russian crude in exchange for tariff reductions from 50% to 18%. India has not formally confirmed the commitment, and its Foreign Secretary Vikram Misri stated that energy policy “prioritises national interest and consumer affordability.”

But the barrel impact is already materialising. Bloomberg reported on February 9 that all state-owned and private refiners except Nayara Energy have paused spot purchases. Indian Oil Corporation, Bharat Petroleum, and Reliance Industries are not accepting offers for Russian oil loading in March and April.

Kpler data shows Indian Russian crude imports fell to 1.2 mb/d in January from an average of 1.7 mb/d in 2025, though the timing of this decline (predating the formal February 2 announcement) leaves some ambiguity about whether it reflects trade deal anticipation or routine scheduling factors. Vanda Insights founder Vandana Hari estimates imports will settle around 400,000 to 500,000 b/d once the unwinding is complete.

The implications ripple in two directions. First, roughly 0.8 to 1.3 million barrels per day of Russian crude needs to find alternative buyers or gets stranded. China absorbed record volumes of Russian crude in January, but there are physical limits to how much additional volume Chinese ports and pipelines can handle. Second, Indian refiners must now source replacement barrels from the Middle East, West Africa, Brazil, and the US at higher prices. As Moody’s noted, a complete shift “could tighten supply elsewhere, raise prices and pass through to higher inflation.” The surplus narrative assumes stable trade flows. This is a structural rerouting of roughly 5% of global seaborne crude on a compressed timeline. Stable is the last word I would use.

I want to flag the uncertainty here. India has a long track record of maintaining foreign policy autonomy, and there is a credible scenario where compliance gradually erodes over six to twelve months. The Carnegie Endowment’s Evan Feigenbaum noted that “publicly rebuking Russia” is a nonstarter for Modi. But even partial compliance, say a sustained reduction to 0.8 mb/d from 1.7 mb/d, removes nearly a million barrels per day from the bearish equilibrium that surplus projections assume.

The OPEC+ Chessboard

The consensus narrative on OPEC+ has been reliably wrong for the same reason the surplus narrative is wrong: people are pattern-matching to a framework that doesn’t apply.

When the Group of Eight began unwinding 2.2 mb/d in voluntary cuts from April 2025, the market interpreted this as bearish supply flooding. It wasn’t. The critical point that the consensus missed is that OPEC+ wasn’t responding to global demand growth. They were responding to demand for their specific barrels increasing, because sanctions on Russia, Iran, and Venezuela had removed competing supply from the market. From their perspective, the conditions for unwinding were met. The market misread rebalancing as aggression.

The Q1 2026 production increase pause, confirmed at three separate meetings, was tactical restraint, not panic.

Rapidan Energy Group’s Bob McNally has made a complementary argument: OPEC+ retains what he calls “unprecedented pricing power” precisely because the spare capacity buffer gives them optionality that the market persistently undervalues. The Group of Eight can modulate output in either direction, and the market’s assumption that announced increases will translate barrel-for-barrel into incremental global supply reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of how the cartel operates.

As of mid-February, Reuters and Bloomberg report that OPEC+ is leaning toward resuming production increases from April. No formal decision has been made, and delegates stress that talks will continue in the weeks ahead of the March 1 meeting. Critically, several delegates indicated the decision may hinge on whether Trump launches military action against, or reaches a nuclear deal with, Iran. Some members believe concerns about a global glut are overblown. The market, predictably, is treating the prospect of any increase as bearish.

Here’s why it isn’t.

The seasonal demand calendar absorbs most of any announced increase. Ramadan runs from February 17 through March 20, during which energy demand in Gulf states rises materially for power generation. Then comes Hajj in late May, which alone increases Saudi oil demand by roughly 300,000 b/d as millions of pilgrims flood the Kingdom. Saudi Arabia has been aggressively expanding religious tourism as part of Vision 2030, meaning record numbers of worshippers this year.

Then the Gulf summer hits. Saudi Arabia’s direct crude burn for electricity generation peaks at around 900,000-1,000,000 b/d during summer months. Oil accounts for roughly 38% of Saudi power generation. Combined, seasonal Middle East domestic demand absorbs approximately 1 mb/d, potentially matching any announced production increase. As Alhajji put it bluntly: “This increase in production, if they start increasing production in April, the world is not going to see it. It is going to be consumed within Saudi Arabia.”

I should be honest about a gap in this argument. The April-June window, before peak summer burn hits in July, creates a period where incremental barrels could reach the export market before domestic demand absorbs them. This is real. But the magnitude is likely modest: the announced monthly increments are 137,000 b/d tranches, not million-barrel floods. The seasonal absorption argument applies most cleanly from July onward, but even in Q2, Ramadan tail effects and pre-summer cooling demand partially offset the increase.

There’s also the compensation cuts wildcard. Four producers pledged 829,000 b/d in compensation cuts by June 2026, with Kazakhstan alone owing 669,000 b/d for prior overproduction. Thanks to the CPC terminal disruptions, Kazakhstan now has the physical inability to overproduce. Sometimes geopolitics enforces quota compliance more effectively than Saudi phone calls.

Even after all announced increases, roughly 3.24 mb/d of cuts remain in place, about 3% of global demand. The buffer is substantial.

US Shale: The Multiple Peaks Problem

Here is where the long-term picture gets genuinely uncomfortable for the bears.

The EIA’s February 2026 Short-Term Energy Outlook forecasts US crude output averaging 13.5 million b/d in 2026, approximately 100,000 b/d less than 2025. This marks the first decline after four consecutive years of increases. US production hit a record ~13.87 million b/d in October 2025, then began fading.

The basin-level story tells you everything. Production is declining everywhere except New Mexico and the Gulf of Mexico. New Mexico’s Permian doubled from 0.9 mb/d in 2019 to 2.0 mb/d in 2024. Lea and Eddy counties alone accounted for 52% of all US production growth between 2020 and 2024. That’s the entire growth story concentrated in two counties in one state. If New Mexico peaks, US shale growth is finished for this cycle.

The rig count has collapsed to the lowest level in about four years, roughly 142 oil rigs per Enverus. DUC wells have risen for four consecutive months to 5,319, the longest expansion streak since early COVID. Operators are sitting on drilled but uncompleted wells rather than bringing them online, which is what rational companies do when prices don’t justify completion costs.

Trump’s “drill baby drill” rhetoric collides head-on with economic reality. Breakeven costs sit at roughly $50-80/bbl, meaning current WTI around $64 is marginal at best for new drilling. Steel and aluminium tariffs have increased drilling input costs by more than 10%, per Diamondback Energy’s CEO. The EIA sees US output staying flat in 2026 and dipping in 2027.

There’s a crude quality dimension here that almost nobody discusses but that fundamentally matters. Nearly 70% of US refining capacity is optimised for heavy sour crude, while US shale produces predominantly light sweet. The “refining wall” describes the limit at which domestic light sweet production exceeds processing capacity. OPEC+ cuts have disproportionately removed medium-heavy sour crude from the market, tightening what the Oxford Institute for Energy Studies calls an “already very tight medium/heavy sour market” while creating a light sweet surplus. Aggregate barrel counts obscure this quality mismatch entirely.

Alhajji has been beating this drum since 2017 with his #CrudeQualityMatters research, arguing that even if total production doesn’t peak, demand for light sweet crude will hit a ceiling. We may be approaching that ceiling now.

I don’t believe in calling a terminal peak for US shale, as that call has been prematurely made before and the people who made it looked foolish. What I do believe is that shale production moves in a series of peaks and plateaus driven by geology, prices, and policy cycles. The EIA projects US shale peaking at ~10 million b/d in 2027 and declining to ~9.33 mb/d by 2050. The staircase goes up, but the steps are getting shorter.

The Geopolitics That Price Is Ignoring

The market is assigning approximately zero probability to a series of geopolitical risks that, in any other environment, would command significant risk premium.

Iran: the regime nobody wants to collapse

Production sits at roughly 3.15 mb/d. The Strait of Hormuz carries ~20 million b/d of petroleum liquids, 20% of global consumption. But 95% of Iran’s own exports transit Hormuz, meaning closure would be “an act of self-sabotage” that hurts Iran’s allies (India, China) far more than its enemies (the US, Israel). So let’s dispense with the Hormuz blockade fantasy.

The real risk is different and more interesting. Alhajji described a “general realisation in the whole region” that regime collapse is the worst possible outcome. Ninety million Iranians, a potential civil war, mass migration flooding Pakistan, Turkey, Iraq, and the Gulf states. Nobody wants this. The diplomatic track has resumed in Oman, but there’s no breakthrough. Rapidan’s Bob McNally has assigned a 70% probability to selective US strikes on Iran. Fear alone adds $2-3/bbl; an actual military engagement could add $10-20.

The risk calculus has shifted materially since late 2025. Protests erupted across all 31 Iranian provinces beginning December 28, 2025, initially driven by economic deterioration. The regime responded with lethal force, killing thousands according to multiple reports. Trump warned the regime not to kill protesters and vowed help was “on the way.” The US struck Iranian nuclear facilities in June 2025, establishing precedent. In late January 2026, reports emerged that Trump was weighing targeted strikes on Iranian security forces to support regime change. By early February, the US had deployed an additional carrier strike group within striking range. On February 10, the US Transportation Department advised all American-flagged ships to avoid Iranian waters when transiting the Strait of Hormuz. The same day, the US military shot down an Iranian drone that “aggressively” approached the USS Abraham Lincoln.

Rapidan’s McNally raised his probability estimate for a US attack on Iran to 75% in the coming days to weeks, up from the 70% he had assigned to selective strikes. Columbia University’s Center on Global Energy Policy warned that if Iranian export terminals were struck, as much as 3.5 mb/d of Iranian supply could be blockaded or interdicted, with potential price spikes to $80-100 in the short term. Even without a strike, the risk premium alone is supporting Brent $4-5 above where fundamentals would place it.

Brent’s behaviour in 2026 so far is instructive. The year opened at $61, briefly tested $58.79 on peak surplus panic, then rallied 18% to $70 on the combination of supply disruptions and Iran risk premium. The double bottom at $58.79 coincided with maximum bearish positioning, and the rally occurred as the market began pricing the disruptions I’ve catalogued above. The fact that Brent cannot sustain prices below $60 even with consensus screaming “3-4 mb/d surplus” tells you everything about the quality of that consensus.

Russia-Ukraine peace: the counterintuitive bullish case

The consensus assumes peace = sanctions relief = more Russian oil = bearish. The actual sequence, as Alhajji laid out, is almost certainly: peace, then Russia spends months repairing 21 damaged refineries, then refinery utilisation rises, then domestic crude demand increases, therefore Russian crude exports decline in the medium term. That’s bullish, not bearish.

Then there’s the dark fleet. Between 1,000 and 1,900 shadow vessels currently transport roughly 3.7 mb/d of sanctioned crude. 72% of these ships are over 15 years old with no proper insurance. Sanctions relief triggers massive fleet retirement, tightening global tanker supply and increasing shipping costs for everyone.

Venezuela: the non-solution to a non-surplus

Current production sits at approximately 1 million b/d. Short-term upside is perhaps 200-300k b/d from workovers and Chevron joint venture repairs. Alhajji’s reality check on the timeline deserves emphasis: on average, it takes 3 years to increase production by 1 million b/d in any country emerging from political turmoil.

In those same 3 years, global oil demand will grow by 2-4 million b/d, while natural decline rates will remove 10-12 million b/d of existing production. The world needs roughly 15 million b/d of fresh supply over that period. Venezuela’s contribution is a rounding error. And the investment required, at least $20 billion upfront and $53 billion just to hold production flat over 15 years per Rystad, will be redirected from other basins, meaning production that doesn’t happen elsewhere.

The thesis that the US seized Venezuelan assets to flood the market and suppress prices inverts the actual economics. No operator commits $20 billion in upfront capital to a jurisdiction with this level of political risk in order to destroy the commodity price that justifies the investment.

Addressing the Reflexivity Problem

There is a critique of my thesis that I want to address directly, because it is the strongest one and I have not seen it raised in most contrarian oil commentary.

Even if the physical surplus is overstated (and I believe it is), the narrative of surplus may function as a fundamental in its own right. If every allocator believes in the glut and positions accordingly, capital withdrawal from the energy sector becomes self-reinforcing regardless of whether the phantom barrels are real. Energy’s weighting in the S&P 500 has slid back under 3%, matching COVID-panic levels, despite oil companies generating substantial free cash flow. This is not just a mispricing. It is a reflexive feedback loop in which bearish consensus reduces investment, which eventually tightens supply, which eventually corrects the mispricing, but the “eventually” can be painfully long.

The 2014-2016 period is instructive. Surpluses of 1-2 mb/d drove prices from $110 to $26, far below what fundamentals alone would justify, precisely because positioning and narrative momentum amplified the move. Sentiment can overpower supply-demand for quarters at a time.

My response is twofold. First, the 2014-2016 analogy has a critical asymmetry: US shale was growing at 1-1.5 mb/d annually during that period, providing a genuine structural supply overhang that validated the bearish narrative. Today, shale is plateauing and the EIA projects its first year-over-year decline. The physical substrate for a sustained narrative-driven collapse is weaker than it was a decade ago.

Second, the reflexivity argument cuts both ways. The crowded short positioning (hedge fund net longs at 15% of start-of-year levels) means any upside surprise triggers aggressive short covering in a market with no long positioning to absorb selling. The asymmetry is not just in fundamentals. It is in positioning. A manufactured surplus creates the conditions for a violent repricing when the manufacturing becomes apparent, and the IEA’s consistent pattern of upward revisions suggests it will.

I am not dismissing reflexivity. I am arguing that the physical reality I’ve documented constrains how far and how long the narrative can diverge from fundamentals. The 2026 market is not 2015. But the risk of a prolonged, sentiment-driven downdraft below $60, even without fundamental justification, is real and should be sized into any position.

Price Cage: Brent Rangebound in the 60s

Let me synthesise this into something actionable.

The floor (~$60 Brent): The surplus narrative collapses under scrutiny. US shale producers need $50-80/bbl to justify new drilling; below $60, rig counts collapse further, accelerating production declines in a vicious feedback loop. OPEC+ retains ~3.24 mb/d of cuts as a buffer. Supply disruptions continue to eat into any theoretical surplus.

The ceiling (~$70-72 Brent): China acts as a probable price cap, though the mechanism deserves more scrutiny than it typically gets. Alhajji’s observation that Chinese import volumes correlate negatively with prices above $70 is supported by Reuters data showing surplus crude flowing to storage dropped from 1.1 mb/d in August to 570 kb/d in September 2025, coinciding with prices approaching the low $80s. But correlation isn’t policy. China has conducted exactly one formal SPR release in its history, and the Oxford Institute for Energy Studies described it as “a test of mechanisms” rather than a serious market intervention. What we can say with more confidence is that Chinese buyers are price-sensitive, that state-directed procurement adjusts purchasing pace based on cost, and that with 1.2-1.3 billion barrels in storage, Beijing has the capacity to cap prices if it chooses to. Whether it deploys that capacity reliably at $70 specifically, or whether the threshold shifts with geopolitical circumstances, is less certain than the bears’ neat narrative assumes. Still, the combination of Chinese buying discipline and Trump administration pressure for lower prices creates real headwinds above the low $70s.

The range: $60-72 Brent for 2026. Skew to the upside if disruptions persist. Skew to the downside only in a genuine recession scenario, which JP Morgan has placed at 35% probability and Goldman at 45%. Those are not tail probabilities, and I want to be direct about the implication: if you weight the recession scenario at 40% and the range-bound scenario at 60%, expected Brent for the year sits around $58-63, closer to consensus than my base case alone would suggest. The manufactured surplus thesis is a conditional claim: if the global economy avoids recession, the market is meaningfully tighter than consensus believes. The recession scenario is a different analytical framework entirely, one driven by demand destruction rather than phantom supply, and it deserves its own assessment rather than being dismissed as an afterthought.

I want to address the obvious tension here. If the surplus is as overstated as I’ve argued, why isn’t my price target $80 or $90? Three reasons. First, the surplus isn’t zero, it’s just much smaller than advertised. A world with a 200-300 kb/d surplus still trades at a discount to a genuinely balanced market. Second, China’s price-sensitive purchasing behaviour creates real gravitational pull in the low $70s, even if it’s not the hard cap some analysts describe. Third, US shale, while plateauing, still responds to price signals with a lag, meaning a sustained move above $75 would eventually incentivise enough completion activity to add incremental supply.

The contrarian value here isn’t in calling for dramatically higher prices. It’s in recognising that the downside everyone is positioning for (Brent in the $50s) requires the phantom surplus to be real. If I’m right that it isn’t, then the crowded short trade loses money slowly and painfully in a $60-70 range, occasionally getting squeezed higher on supply disruptions, while waiting for a demand collapse that may never come.

The manufactured surplus thesis does not claim oil cannot fall. It claims the surplus narrative is the wrong reason for bearishness. If Brent drops to $50, it will be because of demand destruction from a global recession, not because the IEA’s phantom barrels materialised.

What I’m Watching

I won’t insult your intelligence with a “time will tell” conclusion. Here’s what matters from here.

The manufactured surplus narrative has created a historic valuation dislocation. Hedge fund net long positioning at 15% of start-of-year levels means the trade is asymmetrically crowded to the short side. Any upside surprise triggers aggressive short covering in a market with no long positioning to absorb selling.

The indicators I’m tracking, in order of importance: the OPEC+ March 1 meeting outcome and whether April production increases materialise; the CPC terminal restoration timeline, because every week of delay erodes another few million barrels of supposed surplus; IEA demand revisions for Q4 2025 and Q1 2026 data, where history says the revisions will be upward; China import data for any signs of stockpiling pace changes; and the US rig count response to current pricing, which tells you whether shale can stabilise or is entering a genuine decline cycle.

The risk-reward, in my view, skews long oil in the $60-65 range and cautiously short above $72 until the range breaks. What breaks it to the upside: sustained CPC disruption plus Iran escalation plus US shale declining faster than expected, in which case $75-85 is achievable. What breaks it to the downside: a global recession plus full OPEC+ unwinding plus Chinese demand collapse, which gets you to $45-55.

Consensus has Brent at $56-58 for the year. I think that figure embeds the same systematic demand underestimation that has plagued IEA forecasts for nearly two decades, compounded by phantom barrels that exist in spreadsheets but not in any storage tank with an accessible delivery point.

The surplus is manufactured. The market hasn’t figured that out yet. But it will.
