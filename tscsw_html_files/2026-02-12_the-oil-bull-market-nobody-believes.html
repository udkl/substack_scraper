Investment Thesis

Fact 1: Fossil fuels still power 81% of global energy.

Despite $6-11 trillion in alternative energy investment over the past 40 years, the market share of fossil fuels has declined from 83% to 81%. That is a 2% reduction. At this rate, the “energy transition” will be complete sometime around the year 2850.

The International Energy Agency, long the cheerleader for peak oil demand, has quietly revised its outlook. Under the “Current Policies Scenario” reintroduced in November 2025, oil demand rises to 113 million barrels per day by 2050, with no peak in sight.

China’s EV adoption is real. At 51% of new vehicle sales, it represents a structural shift. But fleet turnover takes 15-20 years. Internal combustion engines will dominate the global vehicle stock through 2040. And petrochemicals, heavy transport, and aviation have no scalable alternatives.

Fact 2: The US shale miracle is running out of miracle.

The numbers are unambiguous:

  • Tier-1 acreage drilled: 60-65% in the Permian (vs. 50% in 2022)

  • DUC inventory: 4,500 wells (79% below 2020 peak)

  • Gas-oil ratio: 40% (vs. 34% in 2014), indicating reservoir maturation

  • New well productivity: -6% year-over-year (first decline on record)

  • Rig count: Down 15% YoY despite flat production

The Midland formation’s core is nearly two-thirds drilled. The Delaware is over half exhausted. Operators are moving to Tier-2 and Tier-3 acreage where breakevens average $65/barrel and can reach $96 in marginal locations.

Historical precedent: The Eagle Ford and Bakken hit 60% Tier-1 development in 2018. Production growth stopped within twelve months. Neither basin has grown since.

Counterargument: Technology keeps extending shale’s life. Chevron expects triple-frac operations to maintain 1 million boe/d through 2040. Major operators have pushed breakevens to $30-40/barrel through efficiency gains.

Response: Technology extends the plateau, not the peak. It allows operators to produce more from existing wells, but it does not create new Tier-1 acreage. The geology is finite. The EIA now projects US crude production will decline in 2026 for the first time since COVID.

Fact 3: The industry is cannibalising itself.

Global upstream investment is running at approximately $570-600 billion annually. The International Energy Forum estimates that $640-738 billion is required by 2030 just to maintain current production levels. That is an annual deficit of $60-140 billion.

Nearly 90% of upstream investment is now dedicated to offsetting production declines rather than meeting demand growth. The industry is running as fast as it can just to stay in place.

This is the Red Queen Effect applied to hydrocarbons.

Fact 4: CNQ owns what shale operators cannot replicate.

Oil sands are not shale. The comparison matters:

No new greenfield oil sands mine has been sanctioned since 2013. The environmental permitting regime, capital requirements ($10-15 billion per project), and political climate make new entry effectively impossible. CNQ’s assets are not merely competitive advantages. They are unreplicable.

Why The IEA Is Wrong

The EIA projects WTI will average $52/barrel in 2026 and $50 in 2027. This forecast deserves scrutiny.

Problem 1: The EIA has been wrong at every inflection point.

In 2014, the EIA projected US production would peak at 9.6 million barrels/day. It reached 13.5 million. In 2020, they projected a rapid recovery. It took three years. In 2022, they projected continued growth. Production flatlined. Their models assume drilling activity responds linearly to price. It does not.

Problem 2: Operator behavior has structurally changed.

Post-2020 consolidation transformed the shale industry. Major producers now prioritize returns over volume. Executives are compensated on capital efficiency, not production growth. The historical price-to-rig correlation has broken down. Lower prices no longer trigger the supply response the EIA models expect.

Problem 3: The model ignores geology.

The EIA’s production forecasts are based on rig counts and assumed productivity. They do not incorporate Tier-1 inventory depletion. A model that ignores the finite nature of the resource base will systematically overestimate long-term supply.

The regulatory outlook has improved materially since mid-2025.

The previous concern about carbon tax escalation to C$170/tonne by 2030 is now dead policy. Operating cost risk from carbon regulation has diminished significantly. This is an unambiguous tailwind.

2. TMX Is Open

The Trans Mountain Expansion completed in May 2024, tripling pipeline capacity to Canada’s Pacific Coast from 300,000 to 890,000 barrels per day.

This provides:

  • Structural narrowing of WCS-WTI differentials

CNQ has 169,000 bbl/d of committed TMX capacity. Asian refiners configured for heavy sour crude pay premium prices. This structural improvement in netbacks is permanent and underappreciated by the market.
