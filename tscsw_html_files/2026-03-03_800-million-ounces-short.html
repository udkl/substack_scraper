The Arithmetic: Five Years of Deficits Nobody Talks About

The silver market recorded a structural supply deficit of 148.9 million ounces (Moz) in 2024. That was the fourth consecutive year of deficits, following shortfalls in 2021, 2022, and 2023. The 2025 data, now confirmed by the Silver Institute and Metals Focus at their November industry dinner, marks a fifth consecutive deficit year, bringing the cumulative shortfall for 2021 through 2025 to approximately 775 to 820 million ounces, depending on which 2025 estimate is used (the Silver Institute’s November 2025 revision put the year’s deficit at 95 Moz, down from an earlier interim estimate of 118 Moz).

The Silver Institute estimates total above-ground silver at roughly 19.3 billion ounces, but the vast majority is locked in jewelry, religious artifacts, industrial products, and forms that will never return to the market at any price. The Institute’s own February 2025 report concluded that no correlation exists between overall above-ground stocks and the silver price. Only bullion stocks matter.

After removing ETF-encumbered metal (over 1 billion ounces in ETFs globally as of mid-2025, with SLV alone holding approximately 495 Moz at peak and global silver ETP holdings reaching approximately 1.13 billion ounces), exchange inventories held for specific clients, and metal in transit or in industrial pipelines, the true “free float,” the silver actually available for purchase and delivery, is difficult to pin down precisely but likely falls in the range of 50 to 150 Moz, depending on assumptions about encumbrance and pipeline commitments. Against annual demand exceeding 1.1 billion ounces. The 800 Moz cumulative deficit came out of this thin layer of liquid, deliverable metal, not from the 19 billion ounce headline. A necessary caveat on the deficit figures: “total demand” as reported by the Silver Institute includes jewelry (approximately 200 Moz annually) and silverware (approximately 50 Moz), alongside industrial fabrication, bar and coin investment, and other categories. These are not economically equivalent. Industrial demand for semiconductor packaging or solar cells is highly price-inelastic; a chip fabricator in Hsinchu does not stop buying silver paste because the price doubled. Jewelry demand, by contrast, is significantly price-elastic, particularly in Western markets where silver jewelry is a discretionary consumer purchase. Indian jewelry demand is stickier (silver carries cultural and quasi-monetary significance), but even there, demand responds to price at the margin.

This matters for interpreting the deficit. If the reported 148.9 Moz deficit in 2024 were decomposed, some portion reflects jewelry and silverware demand that would contract at sustained high prices, partially self-correcting the imbalance. A conservative estimate: at $90+ silver sustained for two or more years, jewelry demand could decline 15% to 25% (30 to 50 Moz), which would narrow the deficit meaningfully but not eliminate it. The structural core of the deficit, the gap between industrial demand and total supply, would persist even if every discretionary ounce of jewelry demand disappeared. That is the number to focus on, and it remains firmly negative.

Now, 2025’s deficit narrowed to roughly 95 Moz according to the Silver Institute’s November 2025 revision (down from the April 2025 interim estimate of 118 Moz), and the silver permabulls will not tell you this because it complicates their narrative. Total demand pulled back approximately 4% year-on-year. Industrial demand dipped about 2% to 665 Moz (still the second-highest on record), and bar and coin demand fell to a seven-year low of 182 Moz.

The US market, in a display of almost poetic timing, saw substantial retail liquidation throughout much of 2025, even as the price was ripping higher. American retail investors collectively decided the best time to sell was during a structural repricing event.

But the deficit persisted. Even with demand softening, supply could not close the gap. The silver market is not in deficit because of a temporary demand surge. It is in deficit because supply is structurally incapable of meeting even moderately elevated demand.

The Byproduct Trap: Why Silver Cannot Fix Itself

This is the section that separates people who understand silver from people who merely own it. It is also, I believe, the single most important structural argument in commodity markets today, because it represents a first-principles violation of how commodity self-correction is supposed to work.

Approximately 70% to 75% of the world’s mined silver is produced as a byproduct of mining other metals, primarily copper, lead, zinc, and gold. Only about 25% to 28% comes from primary silver mines. In 2024, silver from lead/zinc operations remained the single largest source category at 29.4% of total production, followed by copper byproduct and gold byproduct.

Think about what this means operationally. When the silver price doubles, the rational economic response would be for miners to increase silver production. That is how commodity markets are supposed to self-correct. High prices incentivise supply. Supply meets demand. Equilibrium is restored.

Except in silver, this mechanism is broken.

If you need more silver, you need to convince copper miners, lead miners, zinc miners, and gold miners to increase their production, because that is where the silver comes from. And those miners make their capital allocation decisions based on the economics of their primary metal, not silver. A copper mine does not drill more holes because the silver price went from $25 to $90. It drills more holes because the copper price justifies the capex. Silver is a rounding error on their income statement.

This is the byproduct trap. Silver supply is, for practical purposes, price inelastic. The metal literally cannot respond to its own market signals.

And even the primary silver mines face constraints. Global silver mine production peaked at approximately 900 Moz in 2016. By 2024, it had fallen to 820 Moz, a 9% decline over eight years despite the price more than tripling during that period. Production in 2025 was essentially flat at 813 to 835 Moz depending on the source, with gains in Mexico and Russia offset by declines in Peru and Indonesia.

The project pipeline for new primary silver mines is thin. Development timelines from discovery to production run 7 to 15 years. Capital intensity has risen dramatically. Ore grades at existing operations are declining, requiring more rock to be processed for equivalent output. Exploration spending during silver’s extended bear market from 2011 to 2020 was negligible, and the projects that might have been discovered during that period simply do not exist.

New primary supply is unlikely to arrive before 2030 at the earliest, and even that assumes exploration spending that has not yet occurred.

The Demand Revolution: Solar, AI, and the Industrial Floor

Silver’s industrial demand hit a record 680.5 Moz in 2024, the fourth consecutive record year. Industrial fabrication now accounts for roughly 59% of total global silver consumption, making silver fundamentally different from gold in its demand profile. Gold is a monetary asset held in vaults. Silver is a monetary asset that gets soldered into circuit boards and laminated into solar cells.

The electrical and electronics sector has been the primary driver, increasing 51% since 2016. This should surprise nobody who understands silver’s physical properties: it is the most electrically conductive metal on earth. When you need electrons to flow with minimal resistance, silver is not optional. It is required.

Solar photovoltaics represent the fastest-growing demand segment. PV-specific silver consumption accounted for 17% of total demand in 2024, up from 5.6% in 2015, growing at an annualised rate of 12.6%. The transition from PERC to TOPCon and heterojunction (HJT) cell architectures initially increased per-cell silver loadings, though 2024 and 2025 saw aggressive “thrifting” (reduction of silver content per module) as manufacturers adapted to elevated prices. Global solar installations hit records in 2025, led by China’s 45% capacity expansion in 2024.

Here is where I need to be blunt, because most silver analysis is dishonest in one of two directions, and I have no interest in joining either camp.

The bulls dismiss thrifting. The bears dismiss deployment scale. Both are making the same intellectual error: treating a dynamic, nonlinear system as though one variable will dominate. The honest answer is that the outcome depends on the relative rates of change of two competing forces, and pretending you can forecast that interaction with precision is arrogance dressed up as analysis.

Let me lay out the data as honestly as I can.

The industry has achieved a roughly 6x reduction in silver per cell since 2010, from approximately 60 mg/W to roughly 10 mg/W. That is extraordinary engineering progress. The transition from older PERC cells to newer TOPCon architecture initially reversed some of those gains (TOPCon requires silver on both sides, consuming roughly 50% more per watt than PERC), but thrifting on the new architectures is proceeding aggressively. HJT cells, which peaked at 22 mg/W, have been driven down to 6.39 mg/W through copper substitution. Germany’s Fraunhofer ISE has demonstrated a lab record of just 1.4 mg/W.

And here is the number that silver bulls do not want to hear: PV silver demand actually declined approximately 5% year-on-year in 2025, despite record solar installations globally. That was the first time thrifting gains outpaced volume growth. Longi Green Energy has announced copper replacement for back-contact cells (mass production targeted for Q2 2026). Jinko Solar has signaled large-scale copper-based production. Shanghai Aiko Solar has launched what it describes as silver-free cells.

The timeline here deserves emphasis: Cu@Ag paste (silver-coated copper) adoption is projected by several industry analysts to reach 30% to 40% of global cell production by 2027, potentially reducing silver content by 50% to 80% per cell at scale. This is not a 2030 risk. It is a 2027 risk, and the R&D acceleration at $90+ silver is non-linear.

The 2011 precedent deserves a direct mention: after peaking at $49.51, silver crashed approximately 60% and did not recover to $30 for over a decade, driven by cascading margin increases. The CME raised margins five times during that run. Similar adjustments should be expected in 2026 if volatility persists.

If you are long silver purely on the solar thesis, this data should make you uncomfortable. It makes me uncomfortable.

But the picture is more complex than “thrifting will kill silver demand.” At current prices exceeding $90 per ounce, silver has become the single largest cost component in PV manufacturing. BNEF estimated silver at roughly 14% of total PV production cost at $42 to $46 per ounce in September 2025, rising to 17% at approximately $50. At $90 or above, silver’s cost share likely exceeds 25% of total panel cost. That is precisely the threshold at which substitution R&D budgets explode. But lab records and mass production are separated by years and billions of dollars of retooling. Copper-based pastes degrade faster, are less conductive, and require manufacturing process changes that cannot be implemented mid-production cycle. The 1.4 mg/W Fraunhofer result was achieved under laboratory conditions that bear no resemblance to a production line running 24 hours a day in Jiangsu province.

A Ghent University/Engie Laborelec study, which models this race between thrifting and deployment more rigorously than anything I have seen from the sell side, projects that PV silver demand could still reach 322 to 450 Moz by 2030, potentially representing 29% to 41% of projected global supply. Their most alarming finding: by 2030, total silver demand could reach 48,000 to 52,000 tonnes annually, but supply may cover only 62% to 70% of that need.

The AI buildout is a decade-long capital expenditure cycle, and silver is embedded in its physical infrastructure.

Silver Intensity: The Metric Nobody Is Tracking

There is a metric that, as far as I can find, no major institution has formally modeled, and it tells you more about silver’s structural position than any supply-demand table.

Silver intensity per unit of GDP appears to be rising, a claim that requires careful qualification on measurement methodology but whose direction is robust across reasonable choices. From first principles, this should not happen. The normal trajectory for any industrial commodity is declining intensity per unit of output, as engineers find ways to use less material per unit of economic value. Steel intensity peaked in the 1970s. Aluminium in the 1990s. Copper has been roughly flat. The fact that silver is moving in the opposite direction tells you something fundamental has changed about what the global economy needs to function.

Industrial silver demand grew from 491 Moz in 2016 to 680.5 Moz in 2024, a 39% increase. Over the same period, global real GDP (World Bank constant 2015 USD) grew approximately 22%. In nominal USD terms, the growth was closer to 38%, which would make the intensity increase more modest. The honest answer is that the precise magnitude depends on your deflator and denominator, and I want to be transparent about that sensitivity rather than pick the number that flatters the thesis. But the direction is robust across reasonable measurement choices: silver demand is growing faster than economic output, and the gap widens further if you adjust for purchasing power parity (where GDP growth looks larger, making the intensity increase even more pronounced).

The sector-level data corroborates the aggregate trend. The electrical and electronics sector increased silver consumption 51% between 2016 and 2024. Photovoltaic silver demand grew from approximately 60 Moz to over 200 Moz in the same period, a 230%+ increase. Semiconductor packaging, driven by advanced node chips that require silver-based thermal interface materials and die-attach pastes, has grown at roughly 8% to 12% annually since 2020 according to industry estimates from SEMI and Yole Developpement. AI-specific chip packaging (which requires higher silver loadings due to thermal management demands at smaller geometries) is accelerating this further.

Critically, this is not a single-sector phenomenon that could be dismissed as “the solar effect.” Excluding PV demand, the remaining industrial offtake still grew from approximately 431 Moz to 480+ Moz between 2016 and 2024, roughly a 12% increase against 22% real GDP growth. That ratio is closer to flat, which is normal commodity behaviour. What makes silver anomalous is the addition of entirely new demand categories (utility-scale solar, AI infrastructure, advanced semiconductor packaging) that did not exist at meaningful scale a decade ago. The economy is adding new uses faster than efficiency gains can offset them.

The Silver Institute’s December 2025 report, “Silver, The Next Generation Metal,” frames the directional thesis explicitly but does not quantify the intensity metric. For institutional readers, the analytical implication is significant: silver demand is not merely growing in absolute terms. It is growing relative to economic output. In a world where GDP growth is 2% to 3% annually but silver intensity per unit of GDP is increasing, the implied demand growth rate exceeds headline economic growth. Run that forward five to seven years against structurally capped supply, and the arithmetic of persistent deficits becomes very difficult to argue against.

This leads to a first-principles challenge for the bear case that I have not seen adequately addressed. The standard commodity demand destruction model works as follows: price rises, consumers substitute, engineers redesign, intensity declines, and the commodity gradually gets engineered out of the economy. This model has worked for steel, for aluminium, and for most industrial metals. It assumes that demand is a function of price.

But what if demand is a function of physics? Silver is the most electrically conductive element on the periodic table. In applications where electron flow efficiency is the binding constraint (solar cells, semiconductor packaging, high-frequency interconnects), you are not choosing silver because it is cheap. You are choosing it because nothing else conducts electricity as well. Copper is 5% less conductive. Aluminium is 37% less conductive. In those applications, price is secondary to performance, and the demand driver is thermodynamic, not speculative.

The Physical Squeeze: When Paper Met Reality

In October 2025, the silver market experienced something that most participants had never witnessed in their careers. One-month silver lease rates, which normally hover around 0.3% to 0.5%, spiked to nearly 40%. The London spot price broke through $50 for the first time since 1980, hitting $51.22 on October 9. The EFP spread (the difference between COMEX futures and London spot) exploded from its historical average of $0.25 to over $2.50. COMEX open interest fell by more than 22% between August and October as short sellers scrambled to unwind positions they could not cover with physical metal.

This was not a paper squeeze. It was a physical squeeze. The distinction matters enormously.

The headline LBMA figure of roughly 891 million ounces (as of January 2026) is deeply misleading. Approximately 75% of London vault silver is encumbered by ETF holdings, primarily SLV and SIVR. The remaining “free float,” the metal actually available to the market for settlement and lending, has been static at roughly 140 to 155 million ounces since spring 2025, according to both TD Securities and independent analysts. That is barely six weeks of global industrial demand. When one-year lease rates surged above 5%, it signaled that much of even this residual float is privately held and not actually lendable.

COMEX registered silver, the metal earmarked for futures delivery, tells an even more dramatic story. After a brief recovery to approximately 168 Moz in October 2025 (fed by London-to-New York transfers during the tariff arbitrage episode), registered stocks have plunged to roughly 86 to 88 Moz by late December 2025, a 48% decline in four months. On February 11, 2026, registered inventory breached the psychologically critical 100 million ounce threshold for the first time. Against approximately 528 Moz in March 2026 open interest, the paper-to-registered-physical ratio stands at roughly 6:1. COMEX delivery activity has been unprecedented: 474 Moz delivered in 2025 (more than double 2024’s 203 Moz), with January 2026 alone seeing 49.4 Moz, a 627% increase year-over-year.

Shanghai inventories have collapsed 86% from their January 2021 peak. The SHFE forward curve is in pronounced backwardation, and Shanghai physical premiums have reached $5 to $10 per ounce over Western benchmarks. Between September 30 and November 21, 2025, combined Shanghai exchange inventories dropped 56% in just 51 days.

TD Securities’ Daniel Ghali described the free-floating LBMA stockpiles as having reached a “critically low level,” noting the remaining free float represented roughly half of the metal’s daily average London trading volume. In September 2025, he warned there were “probably less than four months” until the LBMA’s entire free float would be depleted.

Meanwhile, across the Atlantic, COMEX registered inventories fell from roughly 290 million ounces at the start of 2024 to below 210 million ounces by October 2025. For the first time in the modern silver market, the three largest transparent inventory pools (LBMA, COMEX, and Shanghai) were all draining simultaneously.

The squeeze was eventually eased by emergency metal transfers and the reversal of some speculative positioning. But the aftermath revealed something more important than the spike itself: the carry trade that had underpinned silver’s market structure for decades broke, and it has not recovered.

In normal conditions, silver lease rates run 0.3% to 0.5% annually, and futures trade in contango (above spot) by 0.5% to 2%. The carry trade, borrowing physical metal at low lease rates, selling spot, buying futures at contango, and pocketing the spread, is mechanically profitable as long as contango exceeds the lease rate. When lease rates exploded to 39% on October 9, and silver simultaneously entered backwardation (spot above futures), the trade became violently unprofitable. Unwinding it requires buying back physical silver to return to lenders, which pushes spot prices higher, which triggers margin calls on remaining shorts, which forces more buying. This is a self-reinforcing squeeze, and October 2025 demonstrated the feedback loop in real time.

Here is the detail most commentary has missed: lease rates have not returned to pre-2025 levels. As of February 6, 2026, one-month silver lease rates stood at 6.3%, more than ten times the historical norm. Lease rates spiked five separate times in 2025, each spike representing a discrete liquidity crisis in the London lending market. The persistence of elevated lease rates is not noise. It is the market telling you that the structural liquidity of the silver lending pool has permanently deteriorated.

By December 2025, silver hit a fresh all-time high of $83. In January 2026, it touched $121.67 before collapsing 31% in a single session following the Warsh Fed Chair nomination. The Ratio: 800 Years of Mean Reversion

The gold-silver ratio reached 105:1 in April 2025, the second-highest reading in modern history. By early 2026, it had compressed to approximately 50 to 57:1. The historical pattern is consistent: every instance exceeding 90:1 has been followed by silver outperformance (1991: 100:1 to 40:1; 2008: 84:1 to 32:1; 2020: 125:1 to 63:1). Whether the current cycle compresses further toward the 30:1 levels seen in 2011 or the 16:1 of the 1970s remains open. But the ratio is confirmatory, not causal. The structural arguments in the preceding sections explain why silver outperforms. The ratio simply describes the statistical tendency. Do not confuse the two.

The Squeeze: What Uranium, Palladium, and Nickel Tell Us

The three most instructive supportive analogs, uranium, palladium, and nickel, do not tell you the same story. They tell you three different stories about three different failure modes, and the question for silver is which combination of those failure modes applies.

Before examining the supportive analogs, intellectual honesty requires acknowledging two that cut the other direction.

Tin experienced a deficit narrative strikingly similar to silver’s between 2020 and 2022. Supply disruptions in Myanmar and Indonesia, combined with surging semiconductor demand, produced multi-year deficits and drew widespread predictions of a structural squeeze. The price spiked from $15,000 to over $50,000 per tonne. Then demand softened, Indonesian supply recovered faster than expected, and substitution in soldering applications proved more viable than bulls had projected. By late 2023, tin had retraced over 60% of its gains. The deficit was real. The squeeze happened. And the reversal was still brutal, because the narrative overshoot on the way up created an equally violent correction on the way down. For silver, the lesson is that even genuine structural deficits can produce price spikes that overshoot the fundamentals and then mean-revert painfully.

Rhodium is the more extreme cautionary tale. A market even smaller and more illiquid than silver, rhodium surged from $2,500 to $29,800 between 2019 and 2021 on automotive catalytic converter demand and South African supply constraints. The structural arguments were sound at the time. Then the pace of EV adoption (which eliminates catalytic converters entirely) accelerated, thrifting reduced rhodium loadings per vehicle, and the price collapsed to below $5,000. Rhodium demonstrated that in very small commodity markets, structural demand can evaporate faster than the market expects when a technological shift reaches critical mass. The silver parallel is not exact (silver’s demand is far more diversified than rhodium’s), but the principle holds: in thin markets, the distance between “structural” and “obsolete” can be shorter than consensus expects.

With those caveats established, the three most instructive supportive analogs are uranium, palladium, and nickel, each of which illuminates a different mechanism currently at work in silver.

The first principle is inventory depletion. Every commodity squeeze follows the same sequence: supply falls below demand, inventories absorb the gap, and the market ignores the problem because the spot price is still “functioning.” Then inventories cross a critical threshold where marginal physical availability collapses, and the price reprices violently to ration demand. Uranium demonstrated this with textbook clarity. Mine production fell 20% to 30% below consumption for years after Fukushima.

Financial buyers accumulated substantial quantities: Sprott Physical Uranium Trust alone held 66.2 million pounds, with Yellow Cake plc holding an additional approximately 22 million pounds, physically removing material from the spot market and accelerating the depletion timeline. The price repriced from $18 to over $100 across roughly eight years. The silver parallel is direct: ETFs function identically to Sprott’s uranium vehicle. SLV alone holds approximately 495 Moz at peak. The 187 Moz of ETF inflows through November 2025 physically removed metal from the deliverable float. October 2025’s lease rate explosion to 39% was arguably silver’s “tipping point” moment, the equivalent of uranium’s spot market seizing in 2023.

The second principle is substitution. This is where palladium becomes the ghost at the feast. Palladium ran 13 consecutive years of structural deficits. Russian state stockpiles were exhausted by 2014. Prices surged from $600 to $3,429. And then automakers substituted platinum at a rate exceeding 600,000 ounces per year, and palladium collapsed 76% from its peak. The substitution risk for silver is real, and I refuse to hand-wave it away the way most silver commentary does. Solar manufacturers are already pivoting aggressively to copper-based pastes. But palladium’s vulnerability was concentration: roughly 80% of demand sat in a single application (automotive catalytic converters). One industry decision broke the market. Silver’s demand is distributed across solar (20%), electronics (25%), broader industrial applications (15%), plus jewelry, silverware, and investment. Breaking the silver market through substitution would require simultaneous displacement across multiple unrelated industries with different engineering constraints and different substitution timelines. That is a materially higher bar. Not impossible. But structurally more difficult.

The third principle is market structure fragility. The March 2022 LME nickel squeeze demonstrated what happens when opaque OTC positioning collides with physical scarcity. Tsingshan Holding’s invisible short positions created a feedback loop that sent nickel briefly above $100,000 per tonne (with trades reaching $101,365 before the LME cancelled transactions and suspended trading), forcing the LME to cancel trades and suspend trading for a week.

Silver’s OTC market dwarfs exchange volumes. The gross notional paper-to-physical ratio is frequently cited at 356:1, but that figure is misleading; it captures every swap, forward, and derivative including positions that net against each other. The operationally meaningful figure is net non-hedged speculative exposure, which TD Securities and Macquarie estimate at 30:1 to 50:1 against available registered inventories. That is still extraordinarily leveraged. With registered COMEX silver at 86 to 88 Moz, even a partial forced unwind of speculative shorts would overwhelm available physical supply.

The October 2025 episode, when the London market physically seized for 1.5 hours due to insufficient liquidity and the EFP spread exploded to $2.50 (10x its historical average, forcing traders to charter transatlantic cargo flights to move physical bars), was a preview. If physical delivery demands overwhelm registered COMEX stocks (86 Moz against 528 Moz in open interest), similar exchange interventions cannot be ruled out.

The synthesis across all three analogs is not “silver will go up.” It is more specific and more useful than that: in small, illiquid commodity markets with prolonged structural deficits, the repricing is non-linear and violent. The standard economic expectation, that high prices cure high prices through supply response, fails precisely in markets where supply is structurally constrained. Silver’s supply constraints, detailed in Section II, have no near-term remedy, and Chinese export licensing has added a new layer of political risk to an already inelastic supply chain. The analogs suggest we are in the middle innings of a multi-year repricing. But they also warn, particularly palladium, that no structural deficit is permanent, and the endgame, when it eventually arrives, will be just as violent in reverse.

The Policy Catalyst: Silver as a Critical Mineral

In November 2025, the US Department of the Interior officially added silver to the USGS 2025 List of Critical Minerals. For the first time in history, silver sits alongside lithium, cobalt, rare earths, and copper as a material deemed essential to US economic and national security.

This is not symbolic. The critical mineral designation unlocks Defence Production Act funding, accelerated permitting through Fast-41, tax incentives for domestic processing, and the legal framework for strategic stockpiling, similar to the Strategic Petroleum Reserve for oil. Today, the US holds essentially zero strategic silver, having liquidated its Cold War-era stockpile through the 1990s.

The designation reflects a straightforward reality: tthe US imports approximately 64% of its silver consumption annually (per USGS 2024 data), with Mexico as the single largest source. Meanwhile, China dominates global silver refining capacity. In a world of supply chain fragmentation and tariff uncertainty, that dependency profile qualifies as a national security vulnerability.

Whether stockpiling actually occurs is uncertain. But the framework is now in place, and the signal to the market is unambiguous: Washington has reclassified silver from a commodity to a strategic asset.

And Washington is not the only government acting. Effective January 1, 2026, China imposed new export licensing requirements that restrict silver exports to just 44 approved companies, each required to demonstrate a minimum 80-tonne production capacity, with government approval required per shipment. China’s Securities Times described the policy as elevating silver “from an ordinary commodity to a strategic material.”

This matters enormously because China dominates global silver refining, though estimates of its share vary: industry sources cite 60% to 70% of refining capacity, while Trivium China estimates China accounts for roughly 23% of globally traded silver supply and approximately 13% of extraction. The higher figure likely captures processing throughput rather than mine-to-market control, but either way, China’s position gives it significant leverage over Western supply chains. They exported over 4,600 tonnes in the first 11 months of 2025. Those exports were a critical source of Western supply. With the licensing regime in place, that supply is now subject to political discretion, not market pricing.

The obvious counterargument, and one that bullish commentary consistently ignores, is that export restrictions are a two-way door. China restricting silver exports means China is accumulating substantial inventories of refined silver domestically. If Beijing’s strategic calculus shifts (a trade deal, a recession that reduces domestic solar deployment, a diplomatic thaw that makes resource nationalism less attractive), those inventories could be released onto the global market in volume. China’s refined silver production capacity exceeds 6,000 tonnes annually. Even a partial reversal of the licensing regime could deliver 1,500 to 2,500 tonnes (48 to 80 Moz) back into Western supply chains within months, not years. That is enough to temporarily ease, though not eliminate, the structural deficit.

The probability of a full reversal is low in the near term; Beijing’s strategic direction on critical minerals has been consistently toward tighter control, not looser, across lithium, gallium, germanium, and rare earths. But treating Chinese export restrictions as a permanent, one-directional supply removal is analytically lazy. The restrictions are a policy choice, and policy choices are reversible. The structural bull case should not depend on the assumption that Chinese supply is permanently off the table. It should be robust enough (and it is) to survive a scenario where some of that supply returns.

Simultaneously, China installed 212 GW of solar capacity in the first half of 2025 alone, consuming enormous quantities of silver domestically. The strategic calculus is straightforward: China is restricting silver exports while accelerating domestic consumption of the very metal it is restricting. By late December 2025, two Chinese companies contacted Canada-based Kuya Silver offering to buy physical silver at $8 above market price. An Indian buyer offered $10 above market price. When buyers are paying double-digit premiums over spot to secure physical metal, the reported market price is no longer the real price.

The eastward drain compounds this. India became the world’s largest silver importer in 2025, with imports valued at $9.2 billion (up 44% year-over-year). Once silver enters India, absorbed into jewelry, religious artifacts, and industrial pipelines, it rarely returns to the market at any price. This is not recycling-eligible demand. It is permanent absorption. The shadow deficit, metal flowing East that will never return to Western exchange inventories, may add 100 to 200 Moz annually to the reported deficit figures.

The Contrarian Framework: Why 2026 Will Shake You Out

The hardest part of a commodity bull market is not identifying the thesis. It is surviving the path between thesis and outcome.

Silver has moved from $20 to $121 in roughly 30 months. The people buying now are buying a different risk/reward profile than the people who bought at $25. That is arithmetic, not opinion.

Zoltan Pozsar argued as early as 2022 that we had entered a new paradigm where commodity collateral would become as important as financial collateral and the physical world would reassert pricing power over the paper world. Silver is perhaps the purest expression of that thesis: a monetary metal with irreplaceable industrial applications, priced for decades as though neither attribute mattered.

The 1970s parallel is instructive: the US dollar lost 75% of its purchasing power, gold went from $35 to $850, and silver from $1.50 to $50, but the path included savage drawdowns and multi-year consolidations that shook out everyone who was not genuinely committed.

The 1970s parallel is directionally correct but structurally incomplete. The 1970s silver bull was almost entirely monetary. Industrial demand existed but was not the structural force it is today. The silver market of 2026 has a demand floor that did not exist fifty years ago: 680 Moz of industrial offtake anchored in solar manufacturing, semiconductor packaging, EV production, and AI infrastructure. These are not discretionary demand sources. A chip fabricator in Taiwan does not stop buying silver because the price doubled. A solar panel factory in Anhui province does not halt production because silver paste costs more. They absorb the cost, pass what they can to end users, and keep producing. The price elasticity of industrial silver demand in the $30 to $80 range is close to zero.

The industrial demand floor is structurally higher than in any previous cycle (Section III), but a higher floor does not mean a calm market. The people who will profit over the next five to seven years are those who have sized their positions to survive 30%+ drawdowns without forced selling, and who understand that a structurally sound thesis guarantees nothing about the next three months.

Nicky Shiels, Head of Research at MKS PAMP, who won the LBMA’s 2025 Forecast Survey silver category, captured the regime change concisely: “white metals have structurally re-rated higher” following “decisive technical breakouts in late 2025.” Her 2026 base case is $65 per ounce average silver. That may sound conservative against current spot prices, but it reflects the reality that the average price over a full year, including the drawdowns, will be materially lower than the peaks. If you are investing based on the peak, you are doing it wrong.

The range of institutional forecasts for 2026 tells its own story. JPMorgan projects $56 average, $81 year-end. UBS sees $55 to $60 with “a spike to $65 possible.” Goldman Sachs targets $85 to $100. Citigroup calls for $110 in the second half, citing “acute shortage of physical silver available for immediate industrial delivery.” Bank of America’s base case is $65, but their scenario analysis, running gold-silver ratio compression to 2011 or 1980 levels, produces $135 to $309. When the bull-bear spread among major banks runs from $56 to $309, that is not disagreement. That is genuine uncertainty about how a structurally broken market reprices in real time.

It is worth noting the bearish end of the institutional spectrum as well: HSBC projects $42 per ounce, ING targets $55, and the LBMA 2025 Forecast Survey range extends as low as $42, implying 55% downside from current levels. The full range of institutional opinion, from $42 to $309, reflects genuine uncertainty rather than consensus around any particular level.

The Bear Case: What Can Go Wrong (And Why You Should Take It Seriously)

I have spent the preceding several thousand words building a structural bull case for silver. Now I am going to try to tear it apart, and I mean that seriously. The test of whether this analysis is worth your time is not whether the bull case sounds compelling. Compelling narratives are cheap. The test is whether the bull case survives the strongest objections I can construct against it. If it cannot, you should not be positioned for it. If it can, the objections will at least tell you what to monitor and what would change your mind, which is more valuable than any price target.

Thrifting could win the race. I walked through the data in Section III, so I will not repeat it here. The summary is: if thrifting continues at its 2024-2025 pace and copper-based paste technology achieves manufacturing-grade reliability by 2027 or 2028, PV demand could plateau at 150 to 180 Moz rather than the 300+ Moz that the bull models project. That removes 50 to 100 Moz from the demand trajectory. It would not eliminate the deficit on its own (industrial demand would still need to fall 15%+ to rebalance), but it narrows the gap meaningfully and, more importantly, weakens the narrative momentum that drives speculative flows. I assign roughly 25% to 30% probability to this scenario within five years. The gap between laboratory breakthroughs and production-line reality is enormous, but the trajectory concerns me, and anyone who tells you it does not concern them is either lying or has not read the data.

Substitution beyond solar. The palladium analog haunts the silver bull case. At $3,400 palladium, automakers substituted platinum at a rate of 600,000+ ounces per year and broke the market. At $90+ silver, every manufacturer using silver in electronics, connectors, soldering, and brazing is running optimization studies. No commercially viable full substitute exists today. But “today” is not forever. The higher silver goes and the longer it stays there, the more R&D capital flows into finding alternatives. If silver sustains above $100 for two or more years, the probability of meaningful substitution in non-solar applications rises materially. I do not know where the break point is. Nobody does. But pretending it does not exist is intellectual dishonesty.

The crowded trade. Silver call option volatility hit the highest premium over puts since 2022 in late 2025. Managed money net long positioning on COMEX has been elevated. The January 2026 crash, $121 to approximately $75 in under 30 hours, was not caused by any change in fundamentals. It was caused by a Fed Chair nomination and leveraged positioning unwinding. This is the nature of silver: a market small enough that Goldman Sachs describes it as one-ninth the size of gold’s, where concentrated flows in either direction create outsized price moves. If you are not prepared for 30%+ drawdowns, multiple times, during a structural bull market, you will sell at the worst possible moment and spend the next decade explaining to yourself why you were right about the thesis but wrong about the execution.

Recession. Silver fell from $21 to $9 during the 2008 financial crisis. A severe global recession would simultaneously reduce industrial demand (cyclical component), trigger liquidation of speculative positions, and temporarily overwhelm the structural deficit with demand destruction. I model a reasonable recession scenario as follows: industrial demand declines 8% to 12% (roughly 55 to 80 Moz), investment demand collapses as risk assets are liquidated, and the deficit temporarily closes or inverts to surplus. Silver could revisit $50 to $60 in this scenario even with the structural thesis fully intact. The structural floor is higher than in 2008 (the industrial demand base is 40% larger, critical mineral designation provides a policy floor, and ETF holders in India and Europe have proven stickier than US retail). But the floor is not $90.

Policy reversal risk. Section VII detailed China’s export licensing regime and the US critical mineral designation. Both are bullish catalysts, but both are policy decisions, not physical constants. A US-China trade normalisation, a change in administration priorities, or a shift in Beijing’s resource strategy could reverse either catalyst. The critical mineral designation could be deprioritised (no strategic stockpile has been announced, and the political appetite for spending billions on silver reserves is unproven). Chinese export restrictions could loosen if domestic economic weakness forces Beijing to prioritise export revenue over strategic hoarding. These are not base-case scenarios, but they are non-trivial possibilities, and the bull case should not be constructed on the assumption that current policy trajectories are permanent.

Inventory replenishment. Scrap recycling hit a 12-year high of 194 Moz in 2024 and will continue rising at elevated prices. The Silver Institute projects recycling above 200 Moz in 2025 and 2026 for the first time since 2012, with further acceleration at sustained high prices. If silver sustains $80+ through 2026, scrap supply could reach 210 to 230 Moz, which narrows the deficit by 20 to 40 Moz. At $100+, the economics of recovering silver from electronics waste, photographic processing, and industrial scrap improve further, and recycling could become a more significant supply swing factor than most bull analyses acknowledge. Additionally, some of the metal encumbered in ETFs could return to the market if holders take profits. The SLV trust alone holds enough silver to cover roughly four years of deficits at the current run rate. If ETF outflows become sustained rather than episodic, the available supply picture changes materially.

The honest synthesis: none of these risks, individually, invalidate the structural thesis within the next three to five years. But they interact, and critically, they are positively correlated. The conditions that accelerate thrifting (sustained high prices) are the same conditions that incentivise substitution in electronics and increase scrap supply. A recession that softens demand arriving alongside these price-driven responses could collectively close the deficit by 2028 or 2029. I assign roughly 25% to 35% probability to this combined scenario, higher than most bull analyses acknowledge, because the individual risks reinforce rather than offset each other. It does not require anything exotic. It just requires several modest headwinds to arrive simultaneously.

Position sizing and time horizon should reflect this honestly. The person who puts 50% of their portfolio into silver miners at $95 because “the deficit is structural” is taking a different bet than the person who allocates 10% to 15% with a five-year horizon and accepts the volatility. Both may be right about the direction. Only one will survive the path.

Expressing the View: Physical, Miners, Streamers

There is more than one way to be long silver, and the right expression depends on where you think we are in the cycle.

Physical silver is the simplest and most conservative expression. You own the metal. No counterparty risk. No management risk. No operating cost inflation. The downside is that physical silver earns no yield, carries storage costs, and the retail premium above spot can be significant. Physical is for savers, not speculators.

Primary silver miners offer leveraged exposure to the silver price. If silver maintains $90 for 12 months, the miners whose net asset values were calculated using $40 to $45 silver could see 50%+ re-ratings even if silver moves sideways.

The logic for rotating from physical to equities at this stage of the cycle is straightforward arithmetic. If silver stays flat at $90, physical earns zero return while miners whose NAVs were calculated using $40 to $45 silver see 50%+ re-ratings as the market marks their reserves to reality. If silver declines to $50, the miners’ underlying value is largely preserved because their NAVs were already conservative. Pierre Lassonde, who co-founded Franco-Nevada and has spent four decades in precious metals, has described this exact inflection point in previous cycles: the moment when the risk/reward on equities exceeds that on the metal itself. That asymmetry favours the equities now.

However, miners come with operational risk, jurisdictional risk, management risk, and cost inflation. The same inflation driving precious metals prices is driving up mining costs: diesel, labour, explosives, steel, everything.

The all-in sustaining cost for primary silver producers varies enormously: Pan American Silver reported $13.94 per ounce in Q3 2025 (heavily subsidized by record gold by-product credits at $2,600+ gold), First Majestic reported $19.24, but Endeavour Silver guided $27 to $28, Santacruz Silver reported $35.62, and Americas Gold & Silver reported $28.13. The industry-wide average, without extraordinary gold credits, is closer to $25 to $30 per ounce. Critically, this floor is dependent on gold remaining elevated; if gold corrects, by-product credits shrink and silver AISC rises materially.

In a rising cost environment, the best operators, those in the lowest quartile of AISC, will capture disproportionate value.

Royalty and streaming companies offer high-margin exposure without operating risk. The copper industry’s $250 billion investment gap over the next decade will increasingly be filled by streaming deals on byproduct precious metals, creating substantial deal flow for Wheaton, Franco-Nevada, Osisko, and others. Smaller royalty companies (Elemental, Gold Royalty, Vox) trade at significant valuation discounts to majors, an arbitrage that resolves either through re-rating or M&A consolidation.

What Most People Will Get Wrong

Howard Marks has written that “the biggest investing errors come not from factors that are informational or analytical, but from those that are psychological.” Commodity markets, and silver in particular, are a masterclass in proving him right. The same behavioural failures recur in every cycle:

First, people do not invest in a narrative until the price action confirms it. Nobody wanted silver at $20 despite the deficit data being public knowledge. Everyone wants it at $90 because the chart validates the story. This is the definition of buying high, and the silver market is practically engineered to exploit this tendency.

Second, people cannot distinguish volatility from risk. A 15% move in a junior mining equity is not a crisis. It is the market breathing. But if you do not have an independent opinion on what a security is worth, every price move becomes a signal and every signal becomes a reason to panic. The investor without a valuation framework is, functionally, a momentum trader who does not know it.

Third, people confuse price with information. Price data is free and ubiquitous. Valuation requires work. The spread between the two is where returns are generated, and most participants pay obsessive attention to price precisely because they have never done the work to establish what something is worth.

Fourth, and most destructively, people have time horizons that are catastrophically mismatched with their theses. If your thesis is that silver will outperform over a decade because of structural supply deficits and monetary debasement, but you have trauma holding a position over a long weekend, the mismatch between your thesis duration and your pain tolerance will destroy you. This is not a discipline problem. It is a position-sizing problem, which brings us to the framework that most silver analysis conspicuously avoids.

The Survival Arithmetic: Sizing the Position to Match the Thesis

Most silver analysis ends with a directional call and a vague exhortation to “be disciplined.” That is not a framework. It is a bumper sticker. Here is the actual arithmetic.

If you accept the structural thesis (persistent deficits, inelastic supply, rising industrial demand floor), you are making a bet that resolves over five to seven years. Over that horizon, you should expect, not fear, at least three drawdowns of 25% to 35% and at least one drawdown exceeding 40%. That is not pessimism. That is the empirical base rate for volatile commodities in structural bull markets. Silver drew down 35% in 2008 before rallying to $49. It drew down 15% in March 2020 before tripling. In January 2026, it dropped 38% in 30 hours before stabilising.

The first-principles position-sizing question is therefore not “how much silver do I want to own?” It is “how large a drawdown can I absorb without being forced to sell, and what position size does that imply?”

If a 40% drawdown on your silver allocation would trigger either a margin call, a liquidity crisis, or a psychological breaking point that forces you to sell, your position is too large. Full stop. The structural thesis is irrelevant if your position size transforms a temporary drawdown into a permanent loss.

For most investors, this implies a silver allocation of 5% to 15% of a diversified portfolio, with the upper end reserved for those who (a) have no leverage, (b) have income or liquidity sources that do not depend on the silver position, and (c) have genuinely internalised, not just intellectually acknowledged, that they may be underwater for 12 to 18 months at a time.

The paradox of commodity investing is that the people who size conservatively and survive the drawdowns almost always outperform the people who size aggressively and get shaken out. A 10% allocation that compounds through five years of volatility will produce better terminal returns than a 40% allocation that gets liquidated at the first 30% drawdown. This is arithmetic, not philosophy.

The structural bull case for silver is, in my assessment, the strongest risk-adjusted opportunity in commodities today. But “strongest opportunity” and “easy money” are not synonyms. They are, in practice, almost opposites.

Conclusion: The Arithmetic Against the Narrative

Silver is trading around $93 as I write this. Three years ago it was $20. Two months ago it was $121. One month ago it was $75. It will almost certainly see both $70 and $140 again before this cycle is over, and the order in which those numbers arrive will bankrupt people who mistake a structural thesis for a trading signal.

That volatility is the defining feature of a small, illiquid market undergoing a structural repricing. Being right about the direction is necessary but not sufficient. You also need to be right about position sizing, leverage, and time horizon.

The structural case is straightforward: five consecutive years of deficits (cumulative shortfall of 800 Moz), accelerating inventory depletion, a broken supply response mechanism, record industrial demand with no sign of structural reversal, and a US government that has reclassified the metal as a critical mineral.

Against this, you have thrifting risk in solar, potential substitution at extreme prices, crowded speculative positioning, and the ever-present possibility of a global recession that temporarily crushes all risk assets.

The bull case requires patience, discipline, and a tolerance for extraordinary volatility. The bear case requires multiple structural trends to simultaneously reverse, which is possible but unlikely within the next five years.

The deficit will keep running regardless of whether the chart looks extended. The inventories will keep draining. The byproduct trap will remain structurally intact.
