# The miner setup

The average realized gold price in Q1 was roughly $4,975 an ounce, per Hickey’s calculations, which align with my own back-of-envelope work from spot price averaging. That’s 74% higher than Q1 last year. Costs are up maybe 10-15%, mostly from higher royalties, which is just a mechanical function of the gold price being higher. You pay more royalties because you’re making dramatically more money. That’s the definition of a good problem.

[![](../assets/ac8e3fdcdd812c6fdcf4250876ad3416.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!xMXx!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9a803bf1-9d02-418c-99e7-8c73673971f3_1405x693.png)

The margin math is straightforward. A year ago producers were making $2,000 an ounce. Now they’re making $3,000+. That flows straight to earnings and free cash flow. And Q1 reports start this month. Newmont April 23. Alamos April 29. Agnico April 30. These numbers are going to be the best the industry has ever printed at this scale. $3,000+ margins on millions of ounces.

The stocks trade at 3.9x to 15.3x forward earnings. These companies are running 65-73% EBITDA margins with zero debt and fortress cash balances and the market still prices them like it’s 2015 and gold is at $1,100. The discount to other commodity producers running comparable margins is historically wide.

Fifteen years of broken promises from the mining industry earned that discount. The names in this piece were selected because they break the pattern. I’ll get into the specifics. But the sector-level point is simple: the market has not caught up to what $4,672 gold means for these businesses, and Q1 earnings are about to force the conversation.

[![](../assets/a1996f492ef34de7b36ce98726e9984a.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!S9Tu!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F771aaa42-b07f-4a12-8fc3-85eb0425edc6_1405x750.png)

A note on methodology. I use P/E and EV/EBITDA throughout this piece because they’re the metrics most readers can pull up on a screen and compare across sectors. But mining is a depleting asset business. Every ounce we sell is an ounce we can never sell again. P/E doesn’t capture reserve life, sustaining capital requirements, or the difference between accounting earnings and actual free cash flow after the money you have to spend just to keep the mine running. The transparent standard for mining valuation is Price to Net Asset Value (P/NAV) derived from a discounted cash flow model over the life of mine, alongside free cash flow yield.

[![](../assets/9c831e49b9de4d212b7144e240852592.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!iOHs!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa53ebfd3-adfc-4bf7-96c0-e5fbdff359a5_2112x1176.png)

I’ve included P/NAV at both spot and a $3,500/oz base case in the table above. The base case strips out the current gold price euphoria and shows you the downside floor: what these businesses are worth if gold mean-reverts 25% from here. The spot P/NAV shows what the market is currently leaving on the table. Both numbers matter. The base case tells you whether you survive a correction. The spot number tells you how much you’re being paid to take the risk.

One disclosure on comparability: the developer NAVs (Perpetua and Skeena) are modeled at $4,500 gold. I’ve standardized to $3,500 in the table to make the discounts comparable. At $3,500, Skeena’s discount widens, which tells you something important about where the real asymmetry sits at more conservative assumptions.

For the new initiation, I’ll explain why now.

If you have read the Epic Fury series, you have the macro. If you have read “Gold Is Not Failing,” you know why the March correction was a buying opportunity, not a thesis failure. **WESDOME GOLD MINES (TSX: WDO / OTCQX: WDOFF)**

I published a deep dive on Wesdome in December at C$20. The thesis was that the market was pricing it like a disaster while the financials said it was a gold mine. Eagle River was the golden goose printing 65% EBITDA margins. Kiena was the problem child with hoist failures and contractor execution issues. Philip Yee’s appointment as CFO was the signal that a “Kirkland Lake discipline” turnaround was coming. The stock is C$26.11 today. Every measurable fundamental has improved since December. More cash, higher margins, stronger production, management openly discussing dividends for the first time. And the stock went down.

This is the kind of thing that makes you question whether markets work. Then you remember that markets work over quarters and years, not weeks, and that the inefficiency is the opportunity.

For those who weren’t around in December: Wesdome runs two underground gold mines in Canada. Eagle River in Wawa, Ontario, and Kiena in Val-d’Or, Quebec. Eagle River is mining at 13-14 grams per tonne, which is roughly triple the grade of a typical Canadian underground gold mine. At that grade, the economics are obscene. Every tonne through the mill generates outsized revenue relative to extraction cost. That’s where the margins come from.

Kiena is the problem child. I wrote extensively about this in December. Built in 1981, shut down in 2013, restarted in 2024 after Wesdome discovered a high-grade deposit hiding underneath the old workings. The history matters because it explains the market’s skepticism. This mine has broken hearts before. Passed between owners, mothballed, false-started, and the hoist failure in mid-2025 triggered the same pattern recognition investors have been conditioned to feel since the 2013 shutdown. The ramp-up has been rough. A two-week hoist shutdown in mid-2025 killed a quarter’s worth of production. Contractor execution has been poor because Agnico Eagle, which operates three mines within driving distance, hoovers up every skilled underground miner in the region. Wesdome gets the B-teams. The B-teams underdelivered.

The market looked at the Kiena problems and decided the whole company was broken. The market was wrong.

**So why is it this cheap?**

Three reasons and they’re all fixable.

Mine life perception. Wesdome’s disclosed reserves, divided by annual production, suggest limited remaining years at both mines. That’s the number screens use. That’s what the algorithms see. Size. At C$3.9 billion, Wesdome is stuck in no man’s land. Too small for the big generalist funds that buy AEM by default. Too established for the junior mining crowd chasing the next ten-bagger. Fully funded, fully permitted construction-stage projects in Tier-1 jurisdictions typically trade somewhere in the 0.7x to 1.0x range. A re-rating to 0.75x on the $6.1 billion NPV implies roughly $37 per share. At 0.9x, it’s closer to $45. Those targets use $4,500 gold, and spot is above that.

Consensus target is US$35.14, all three analysts rate it Buy. Analyst NAVPS of US$33.57 is built on gold assumptions below current spot.

As I flagged above, the per-share NAV of roughly $49, down from $59 in June despite the project getting materially better, means the upside from here is real but narrower than it was. You’re not buying the same setup I bought. You’re buying a fundamentally stronger project at a less extreme discount.

Looking further out, consensus has 2028 EPS at $2.34, the first year of real production-stage earnings. That puts the stock at 12.6x on 2028 numbers. As Perpetua transitions from developer to producer, the investor base changes. Funds that can’t hold pre-revenue names start buying. That structural shift in demand for the stock provides a re-rating catalyst that’s independent of the gold price.

**Where I could be wrong**

The capex estimate is $2.576 billion based on Q4 2024 pricing. Mining projects of this scale have a documented tendency to come in over budget. In the current inflationary environment, with energy costs surging and the Iran conflict disrupting supply chains for equipment and materials, the risk of cost escalation is real.

There’s an additional friction specific to EXIM-funded projects: federal financing typically comes with Buy America procurement requirements and Davis-Bacon prevailing wage mandates. Sourcing specialized milling equipment domestically rather than from the global supply chain, and paying union-scale wages in rural Idaho, adds cost that wouldn’t apply to a privately financed build. I haven’t been able to quantify the magnitude precisely, but it’s a source of capex inflation that sits on top of the general inflationary pressures and is worth monitoring as construction progresses.

If overruns are severe, they could require additional financing and further dilute shareholders. I wrote about this risk in June and it hasn’t diminished.

The timeline is long. “Construction-ready” in H2 2026, with first production several years beyond that. A lot can change in the gold market, the antimony market, and the political landscape between now and first ore. The administration that backed this project may not be the administration in power when it reaches production.

A reversal of China’s antimony export ban would weaken the by-product credit that makes the economics exceptional. I think this is unlikely in the current geopolitical climate, but “I think it’s unlikely” is a probability statement, not a guarantee. If U.S.-China relations somehow thaw and the ban gets lifted, the AISC climbs from $498 toward something less extraordinary.

Legal risk has diminished with the permits in hand and the federal backing, but large-scale mining projects in the western U.S. remain targets for environmental litigation. The project’s genuine environmental restoration story (reopening fish passage blocked for 80 years, remediating legacy tailings from WWII-era mining) provides real insulation. But a successful injunction could delay construction by months or years.

And the path dependency risk that I wrote about in “Gold Is Not Failing” applies here acutely. PPTA is a pre-revenue company with a $3.7 billion market cap. In a broad liquidation, this is the kind of name that gaps down 20-30% on no company-specific news. The $714 million cash balance provides fundamental support, but it doesn’t prevent the stock from overshooting in a panic. If you can’t hold through that kind of volatility, this isn’t the right position for you, and better to be honest about that now than surprised later.

I’m not trimming before the EXIM final approval. That’s the last catalyst that transforms the company’s classification from “developer” to “funded construction.” Selling in front of that makes no sense to me.

After EXIM closes, I’ll reassess sizing. The upside from 0.49x to 0.75x is real but it requires patience through a multi-year construction period. Today the project is fully permitted, the EXIM loan is effectively approved, $714 million sits in the bank, and the question is no longer “will this get built?” It’s “how much will it earn?” At $4,672 gold with antimony at multi-year highs, the answer to that question is very large numbers.

**SKEENA RESOURCES (TSX: SKE)**

This is a new name for TSCS. I am initiating coverage here because Skeena is, in my assessment, the single most compelling risk-reward in the gold developer space right now. Closer to production than Perpetua. Better near-term economics. Trading at 77% of NAV on metal price assumptions already below spot. The project ranks number one out of 612 global mining projects in Mining Journal’s Pipeline Project Index. Number one. Ahead of every other development-stage gold project on earth.

[![](../assets/1af4c4dd199496e09e24b0f8654fc502.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ALfp!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fac39224c-15d8-4bd1-bf47-99d9de620c3b_1406x618.png)

The forward earnings multiples, once production begins, imply a valuation gap the market will have to close or explain. Consensus 2028 estimates, the first full production year, put the P/E at 3.9x. I need to flag something about that number before you anchor to it. 3.9x is a 2028 estimate. Skeena has never operated a mine. The deposit is being mined with a completely different method than Barrick used. The company is 49% built. Comparing 3.9x to Wesdome at 8.4x or AEM at 15.3x is not the same comparison. Those companies are generating cash today. Skeena is generating concrete pours. The 3.9x tells you what the market thinks about construction risk. I think the market is wrong at this stage of completion. Here is why.

**Eskay Creek**

Some context for readers who don’t follow the development-stage gold names. Eskay Creek isn’t a grassroots discovery that some junior geologist stumbled onto. The original Eskay Creek mine, operated by Barrick, was the highest-grade gold mine in the world during its production life from 1994 to 2008. 3.3 million ounces of gold and 160 million ounces of silver from an underground operation mining grades that were almost embarrassingly good. Barrick shut it down when the underground reserves ran out.

The deposit didn’t disappear. It extended beyond what underground methods could economically access. Skeena acquired the property and did something genuinely clever: they designed an entirely different mine plan. Instead of chasing narrow high-grade veins underground the way Barrick did, they engineered an open-pit operation that captures the broader mineralized envelope at lower grades but with far higher throughput. Different mining method, same world-class geology.

The project is fully permitted in British Columbia. Anyone who has tried to permit a mine in BC knows what that means. The regulatory process in that province can stretch a decade or longer. The permit itself is an asset, a barrier to entry that took years of expenditure and First Nations engagement to build and that no competitor can replicate on any timeline that matters for investment decisions.

**Construction update**

Skeena released a progress update on Tuesday. 49% complete. Production on schedule for Q2 2027. Commercial production Q3 2027. That’s roughly a year from today.

CEO Reichert framed it in terms of risk reduction, and the framing is accurate. Skeena started development activities ahead of final permit receipt, which let them lock in procurement contracts before further cost inflation hit and compress the overall timeline. 88% of procurement costs are now committed. That limits the remaining exposure to supply chain disruptions and materials price increases in a way that a project at 20% procurement commitment cannot claim.

Updated capex: US$659 million, roughly 17% above the 2023 Definitive Feasibility Study. That number deserves scrutiny. 17% over a three-year-old estimate in an inflationary environment is within the range I’d expect, but mining construction cost overruns have a way of being “manageable” right up until they aren’t. The detail that gives me comfort is that the $35 million cost contingency from the original DFS is still intact despite the project being half built. Management has been absorbing variances within the revised budget rather than eating into reserves. That’s discipline.

**The refinancing**

This happened last week and it’s a bigger deal than the market seems to appreciate.

Skeena announced a $750 million refinancing package. Two components, both significant.

First, they slashed the gold stream obligation by 66.7%. Streams are the most expensive form of mining finance because they dilute the economics of the asset permanently. A stream means you’re selling a portion of every ounce you produce, forever, at a predetermined price that was set when you were desperate for capital. Cutting the stream by two-thirds means Skeena retains a dramatically larger share of Eskay Creek’s cash flows once production begins. That value accrues directly to equity holders.

Second, they cancelled the undrawn $350 million senior secured loan that was carrying an interest rate of 3-month SOFR plus 7.75%, roughly 11.4% all-in. The replacement is at 8.5%. On a facility this size, 290 basis points of rate reduction compounds into real money over the life of the loan.

Why were the lenders willing to offer better terms? Because the project is less risky than it was when the original debt was structured. When you go from 20% complete to 49% complete, the probability of reaching production increases materially. Lenders price that improvement. Skeena is capturing the value of their own execution and passing it through to shareholders. That’s how this is supposed to work.

[![](../assets/53b260c9b81b442e804cb6df1353a856.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!3bZA!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0c8ec7da-3f13-4fd2-902d-2846ac6a6e4b_1403x627.png)

One cost the market will focus on: 8.5% on $750 million is $63.75 million in annual interest expense hitting the income statement once the notes are outstanding. That debt service is real and it compresses the bottom-line earnings relative to the headline EBITDA. The consensus 2028 EPS of US$8.00 should reflect this, but given the refinancing closed days ago, verify whether analyst models have been updated.

**The economics**

Skeena’s April corporate presentation shows project economics at $4,500 gold and $70 silver. Both metals are trading above those assumptions right now.

A 102% IRR should make you suspicious. It makes me suspicious. That number assumes nameplate throughput from day one, current metal prices holding through ramp-up, and grade reconciliation matching the geological model. None of those are guaranteed. The 8-month payback comes from the same model with the same assumptions, so treat it with the same skepticism. In practice, commissioning a new mill to nameplate capacity is a 6 to 12 month process. Ramp-up delays are more common than on-schedule startups in the mining industry. If you haircut the model to 80% of nameplate throughput in Year 1 and assume 15% grade dilution from the void issues I’ll describe below, the payback stretches to roughly 14-16 months and the IRR drops to something in the 50-60% range. That is still exceptional by any standard in the sector. But it is a different number than 102%, and the difference matters for how you size the position. At current metal prices, Skeena recovers its entire $659 million capital investment within the first year to eighteen months of commercial production.

By the first half of 2028, the project is generating pure free cash flow with capex fully repaid. I’ve looked at a lot of mining project economics. This payback period at this scale is extraordinary.

[![](../assets/b96760ebc41a0df6b7ade969d309e7aa.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!7zhx!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F31e14ea6-39df-4863-89e5-7190746cf665_1405x790.png)

The driver is a combination of grade, silver credit, and the open-pit method keeping operating costs low relative to the value of ore processed. Barrick’s underground operation at Eskay Creek was mining narrower, higher-grade material at higher cost. Skeena’s open pit captures a wider envelope at somewhat lower grade but dramatically lower cost per tonne. The all-in economics end up being exceptional because the tonnage more than compensates for the grade step-down.

If gold reaches $6,000, Skeena estimates Eskay Creek's NPV climbs to approximately $10.2 billion. Current market cap is approximately C$5.2 billion. The discount is narrower than the developer names trading at 20-30% of NAV, but Skeena is also twelve months from production, not five years.

Here’s where the consensus estimates get interesting. For 2028, the first full production year, analysts project US$1.74 billion in revenue, US$1.34 billion in EBITDA at a 77% margin, and US$884 million in net income.

Consensus 2028 EPS is US$8.00 (two analysts, approximately C$11.12). At the current price, that is a 3.9x P/E. I need to flag the thinness of that consensus: two analysts covering a pre-revenue developer is not the same as a twelve-analyst consensus on a producing miner. Treat the precision of “3.9x” with appropriate caution.

The EV/EBITDA on 2028 numbers is approximately 3.3x, reflecting the additional $750 million in debt from the refinancing. Gross margins of 85%.

For comparison, Wesdome is generating record cash flows from two producing mines and trades at 8.4x forward earnings. AEM trades at 15.3x. Skeena, which is one year from production with half the project built and 88% of procurement locked in, trades at less than 4x its first full-year earnings. The discount for “it isn’t producing yet” exists and it’s rational to some degree, construction risk is real, but the magnitude of the discount looks excessive relative to the remaining execution risk.

**Silver**

Eskay Creek is technically a gold-silver deposit, and the silver component matters for the economics in a way that distinguishes Skeena from the pure gold names in this piece. The 85-87% gross margins in 2027-2028 reflect the combined value of both metals. With silver above $70 on strong industrial demand from solar and electronics, the silver credit is a meaningful margin enhancer.

For Epic Fury readers: silver has significant industrial applications and supply disruptions from the Hormuz closure affect the broader metals complex. Skeena gives you gold miner exposure with a built-in silver kicker. That’s not the primary reason to own it, but it’s not nothing.

**How it fits alongside Perpetua**

My readers are going to ask this because I own both, so let me address it directly.

Both are construction-stage developers with roughly $6 billion NPVs and similar market caps. Both are fully funded and fully permitted in Tier-1 jurisdictions. The differences are where the portfolio construction logic lives.

Skeena is closer to production by two to three years. 49% built, first ore Q2 2027. Perpetua is targeting “construction-ready” in H2 2026 with first production probably 2029 or 2030. Every additional year of construction is additional exposure to cost inflation, commodity price moves, regulatory shifts, and execution risk. Time is not a neutral variable. It’s a source of risk, and Skeena has less of it.

Perpetua has something Skeena doesn’t: the antimony moat. No government agency is funding Skeena’s construction for national security reasons. PPTA’s DoD backing and EXIM loan provide institutional support that Skeena has to earn purely through project execution and market economics.

Skeena’s payback is faster. A reality-adjusted 14-16 months versus 1.9 years. That compresses the period where your capital is at risk and accelerates the transition from cash consumer to cash generator.

The pairing makes sense to me. PPTA gives longer-dated optionality with a unique strategic asset. Skeena gives nearer-term production economics with a payback that’s hard to find anywhere in the sector. Different points on the de-risking curve. Complementary, not redundant.

**Where I could be wrong**

Skeena has never operated a mine. Full stop. Eskay Creek was operated by Barrick using completely different methods on different parts of the deposit. Skeena’s open-pit plan is based on the DFS and subsequent engineering work, but translating a mine plan into actual production is where the gap between spreadsheet and reality gets tested.

Grade reconciliation deserves more than a passing mention because it is, without exaggeration, the single most important variable in the first quarters of any new mine. The geological model says the ore body contains a certain grade. The actual ore you mine and process will differ. In open-pit operations transitioning from a deposit previously mined underground, the spatial relationship between the model and reality can surprise in ways that don’t show up in core samples.

Eskay Creek has a specific variant of this risk that generic grade reconciliation language doesn’t capture. Barrick honeycombed the deposit with underground workings over fourteen years of production. When you blast an open pit into historical stopes, you hit voids. Blast energy dissipates into empty space, rock fragmentation deteriorates, oversized boulders jam the primary crusher, and you risk processing barren backfill instead of ore. This isn’t speculative. It’s a geotechnical reality of mining into old workings, and Skeena’s mine plan has to manage it from day one.

There’s a second friction I haven’t mentioned. Eskay Creek produces a complex polymetallic concentrate, not clean doré. That means Skeena doesn’t pour bars and sell them at spot. They ship concentrate to smelters who dictate the commercial terms, impose treatment and refining charges (TC/RCs), and take months to finalize payments. The effective realized margin is lower than the headline AISC suggests, and cash flow timing is lumpier. Smelter negotiations are an ongoing commercial risk that pure gold producers like Wesdome and AEM don’t face.

If realized grades come in 15-20% below plan in the first two quarters, the 77% EBITDA margin compresses to something closer to 60-65%, the 8-month payback stretches past a year, and the 3.9x P/E headline becomes 5-6x. That’s still cheap. But it’s a different kind of cheap, the kind where the market says “told you so” and the stock sells off 20-30% before the data normalizes. This is not a low-probability scenario. Grade misses in the first year of production are closer to the norm than the exception in the gold mining industry.

The 17% capex increase from the 2023 DFS is absorbed so far, but the project is 49% complete with 12% of procurement uncommitted. Mining construction has a persistent tendency toward late-stage cost surprises. The inflationary environment, particularly with energy costs surging, adds pressure on the remaining uncommitted scope.

British Columbia is Tier-1 but it comes with complexity. The regulatory environment is intricate. Environmental activism has a long history in the province. First Nations engagement is ongoing and must be maintained through the life of the mine. None of this prevented Eskay Creek from getting permitted, but operational friction during construction and production is a different kind of risk than permitting risk.

The share count has grown through the development phase. 121.7 million shares. If additional financing is required for any reason, further dilution is possible, though the refinancing suggests the current capital structure should be sufficient to reach production.

And the gold price dependency is acute. At $4,500 gold, the IRR is 102% and the payback is 8 months. At $3,000, the project is still economic, but those numbers come way down and the “3.9x P/E” headline stops being a headline. Skeena’s leverage to gold price cuts both ways. In a rising gold environment, it’s one of the best expressions of that view. In a declining one, the re-rating I’m counting on gets delayed or doesn’t happen.

There is a counterargument I owe you. Hickey has owned Skeena since the low single digits. He has made roughly 750% on it. He is selling. Not all of it, but reducing, and rotating into Rupert Resources, which trades at roughly 20% of NPV versus Skeena at 77%. His logic is clean: the easy money has been made, and the wider discount on Rupert offers more asymmetry even though Rupert is years further from production. I disagree. I weight the de-risking at 49% completion more heavily than a wider NAV gap on a project that does not produce until 2031 at the earliest. But Hickey has been doing this since before I was born. When he disagrees with me, I put it in the piece. You decide.

Institutional ownership is at about 50%, which is reasonable for a developer at this stage. I haven’t identified an anchor investor providing the kind of conviction signal that Paulson provides for PPTA. That’s a gap in the thesis. It means there’s no committed holder whose presence signals deep due diligence and willingness to support the stock through construction-phase volatility. It doesn’t change my assessment of the project economics, but it’s a qualitative negative relative to Perpetua on the developer comparison.

I’m adding Skeena as a core position. The project is half built, a year from production, fully funded, and trading at under 4x first-year earnings on metal price assumptions that are already conservative relative to spot. The refinancing just improved the financial structure materially. And the payback period, a realistically modeled 14-16 months at current prices, means that if Skeena executes on schedule, the entire capital investment is recovered before most of the market has finished updating its models.

The risk is construction and ramp-up execution. That’s real. I’m sizing for it. But at 77% of NAV with the project half done, I think the market is overweighting the risk relative to the reward.

# What happens if I’m wrong

I put the deflationary bust scenario at roughly one in four. If it plays out, these five names do not behave the same way, and you need to know the hierarchy before you size positions.

AEM survives any environment. In a broad liquidation, its quality premium becomes a flight-to-quality bid within the gold sector. It still goes down. Everything goes down. But it goes down less and recovers first.

Alamos has the balance sheet to weather it, but 2026 is peak capex year. $900 million going out the door while gold is falling is a painful combination. The growth story delays, not dies, but the stock could take a serious hit in the interim.

Wesdome’s C$354 million cash pile provides a genuine floor, but at C$3.9 billion market cap with C$18 million in daily volume, it overshoots to the downside in a panic. The cash protects the company. It doesn’t protect the stock price in the short term.

Perpetua and Skeena, as pre-revenue names with multi-billion dollar market caps built on forward economics, take the worst of it. 30-50% drawdowns on no company-specific news are entirely possible. The projects survive because they’re fully funded, but the equity trades on sentiment, not cash flow, until production begins. Time-to-production becomes a liability rather than a countdown.

The order I listed them in is roughly the order of defensive resilience. Size accordingly.
