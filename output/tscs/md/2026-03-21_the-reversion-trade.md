## **I. The 1973 Fallacy, and Why This Is Actually Worse**

The lazy analogy is to the Arab oil embargo of 1973. It is in every op-ed, every broker note, every breathless cable news segment. The comparison is intuitive and almost entirely wrong, but not in the direction most commentators assume.

Start with the single most important structural difference, the one that makes this crisis categorically unlike anything in the modern record: spare capacity has been neutralised.

In every prior oil shock, the global market had a shock absorber. Spare production capacity, historically concentrated in Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Kuwait, could be brought online within weeks to partially offset a supply disruption. In 1973, spare capacity was the mechanism through which embargoed barrels found alternative routes to market. In 1990, Saudi spare capacity replaced the bulk of Kuwait’s lost production within months. In every disruption since, the first question on every trading desk has been: how much spare can OPEC bring?

That question is now irrelevant. Virtually all of it sits on the wrong side of the strait. It is physically inaccessible. Iran has, with one strategic move, neutralised the global oil market’s primary shock absorber. This has never happened before. Not in 1973, not in 1979, not during the tanker wars of the 1980s when the strait remained open even as Iran and Iraq attacked each other’s shipping.

Now scale. The 1973 embargo was a selective rerouting, not a physical supply destruction. Arab OPEC nations embargoed shipments to Western allies of Israel but continued producing and shipping to other buyers. Barrels found their way around.

The realised supply loss, estimated at roughly 4.3 to 5.0 million barrels per day at headline, was significantly smaller in practice because barrels found alternative routes to market.

This crisis is categorically different. Pre-crisis, roughly 20 million barrels per day of crude and products flowed through the Strait of Hormuz. That flow has effectively stopped. The IEA estimates 7.9 million barrels per day of crude, 9.9 million of total liquids, physically shut in across Gulf producers: wells capped, facilities idled, onshore storage at capacity. Bypass infrastructure, the East-West pipeline operating under intermittent Iranian targeting, the Habshan-Fujairah line, selective transits permitted by Tehran, is recovering perhaps 4-5 million barrels per day. The net loss to the globally accessible supply pool is on the order of 8-10 million barrels per day. That is roughly double the Iranian Revolution, double the Gulf War, and double any disruption in the post-war record. Nothing in the modern era comes close.

So the crisis is worse than any prior shock on scale, and the fastest self-correction mechanism has been disabled. That is the near-term reality, and it is severe.

The medium-term reality runs the other direction. The structural forces that guarantee reversion are stronger today than at any point in the oil age. Oil’s share of total primary energy demand has fallen from its 1970s peak of 46% to below 30%. US crude production runs at 13.6 million barrels per day, with the EIA forecasting 13.8 million in 2027, raised by 500,000 barrels per day in direct response to the price signal. Strategic reserves, absent entirely in 1973, are now being deployed at 3.3 million barrels per day across 32 countries. The substitution infrastructure, from LNG terminals to electric vehicles to dual-fuel industrial equipment, is orders of magnitude more developed than in any prior crisis. Every one of these forces bends the price back toward equilibrium. None of them operate fast enough to matter in the next eight weeks.

The implication is uncomfortable but must be stated plainly: the structural factors that guarantee reversion operate on a timeline of quarters to years. The physical shortage operates on a timeline of weeks. In between lies the pain.

## **II. The Physical Reckoning: What the Futures Market Is Not Telling You**

If you are watching the Brent front-month contract and concluding that the market “has this priced in,” you are watching the wrong screen. And you have plenty of company.

The consensus that $112 Brent reflects the market’s considered judgment about the severity of this crisis has become the default view across most sell-side desks. It is wrong, and the evidence is not subtle.

The $60 per barrel gap between physical Oman crude and Brent futures is not a rounding error or a temporary logistics hiccup. It is the largest physical-paper divergence in the history of the modern oil market, and it exists because the paper market is being held below physical reality by mechanisms that are quantifiable, time-bounded, and running out.

Start with what the physical market is actually saying. Front-month Brent prompt backwardation, the spread between the first and second month contract, hit an all-time record of over $9 per barrel in the second week of the crisis.

The administration has repeatedly pointed to the downward-sloping futures curve as evidence that the market expects prices to decline. This is either a misunderstanding of commodity market structure or a deliberate misrepresentation of it, and the distinction does not matter much to anyone trying to allocate capital.

In physical commodity markets, extreme backwardation signals extreme current-month scarcity. The market is paying an unprecedented premium for barrels available right now because the physical system is running dry. The forward curve is not a forecast. It is a measure of desperation.

So why is Brent at $112 instead of $170?

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!RqNS!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7351cd29-e6d6-499e-9fc0-f0b96cb845c2_1406x786.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!RqNS!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7351cd29-e6d6-499e-9fc0-f0b96cb845c2_1406x786.png)

Three mechanisms, all of which are finite.

First, the Iranian floating storage release. The US Treasury recently issued a 30-day general license permitting the sale of an estimated 140 million barrels of Iranian oil already loaded and stranded at sea. This is not jawboning. It is a concrete, one-time injection of physical supply into a market that is consuming roughly 103 million barrels per day.

At best, it provides a marginal flow of 2-3 million barrels per day for roughly 45-60 days as barrels are discharged and absorbed, with the realised flow discounted by buyer reluctance, payment channel frictions, and insurance constraints. At worst, if Asian buyers prove unwilling to touch sanctioned barrels even under a general license, it provides substantially less. The paper market is pricing this as a lasting buffer. It is a single magazine, not a resupply line.

Second, the IEA’s strategic reserve release, and specifically its composition. The 400 million barrel coordinated drawdown yields approximately 3.3 million barrels per day, but its composition is uneven and matters for pricing. The US contribution, the largest single tranche, is crude oil skewed toward sour grades.

European IEA member reserves are held substantially as refined products, gasoil, diesel, and gasoline, under statutory mandates designed precisely to bypass refinery bottlenecks during supply emergencies. This is why the Brent-Oman spread has blown out to $60: Western refiners are partially buffered by SPR crude barrels, and Western consumers are partially buffered by European product reserves.

Asian refiners have access to neither. They are fighting over the remaining physical supply, driving Oman crude into the stratosphere while Brent remains artificially insulated. The spread is not a mystery. It is the geography of the buffer made visible.

And the buffer has an expiry date: 400 million barrels against an 8-10 million barrel per day Gulf supply hole provides 40-50 days of coverage. If the conflict extends into May, the SPR buffer is exhausted entirely, and Brent reprices to meet physical reality.

Third, the expectation of a “Trump Taco.” The market is pricing a significant probability that Trump unilaterally declares victory and withdraws. This has precedent: the June 2025 conventional strikes against Iran’s nuclear enrichment facilities lasted twelve days before a ceasefire declaration, and the Venezuela operation was similarly brief. The market is betting this follows the same pattern. If that bet is wrong, if this drags beyond another two to three weeks, that probability reprices violently.

Now the air pocket.

The last laden crude tankers departed the Gulf approximately three weeks ago. Transit times vary significantly by destination: roughly two weeks to Singapore and Southeast Asia, three to four weeks to North Asian destinations (Japan, Korea, northern China). The air pocket is likely already arriving in Southeast Asian markets, with the full impact on North Asian refiners still one to two weeks away.

The physical scarcity has not yet fully materialised in the world’s largest consuming region. Asian refiners have preemptively cut throughput from approximately 90% to 65% of capacity to extend their runway before forced shutdowns, which is itself creating acute product shortages even before the crude scarcity arrives. When it does, when Singapore inventory data begins showing the drawdowns (expected to appear in weekly data imminently), the $60 physical-paper divergence compresses upward. The $112 Brent print becomes untenable.

Total the available offsets optimistically, the SPR, pipeline bypasses, Russian redirection, Iranian floating storage, and you get 8-10 million barrels per day of realistic coverage against a 9 million barrel per day hole in the first month, when all buffers are at maximum strength. The arithmetic of how each offset depletes over time, and why the gap widens rather than narrows, is the subject of the reversion waterfall in Section VI.

The price required to balance the residual gap deserves explicit treatment. If offsets partially fail, the residual supply gap is 3-6 million barrels per day. If they fail entirely, it approaches 8-10 million. Against pre-crisis demand of roughly 103 million barrels per day, that requires destroying 3-10% of global consumption through price alone.

The academic literature on short-run oil demand elasticity spans a wide range, from estimates as low as -0.02 in traditional structural models (Hamilton 2009) to substantially higher figures in more recent specifications that account for inventory and speculative dynamics (Kilian and Murphy 2012, 2014). We cite the literature for completeness. We do not build on it. Those elasticities were estimated from shocks that removed 2-4 million barrels per day from a less complex, less substitution-rich global economy. Extrapolating them to an 8-10 million barrel per day disruption is not analysis. It is arithmetic wearing a lab coat.

What we can do is look at what actually happened the last time the oil market had to destroy demand at remotely comparable scale.

The 1979-1980 shock removed roughly 4-5 million barrels per day of Iranian and Iraqi supply from the global market over the course of the crisis. Brent-equivalent prices approximately tripled in real terms, from roughly $50 per barrel (2026 dollars) to $150 per barrel equivalent at the peak. OECD demand fell approximately 6 million barrels per day over four years, with the first 2-3 million arriving within twelve months. Critically, that destruction occurred in an economy where oil represented 46% of primary energy demand, substitution options were limited, and strategic reserves did not exist in meaningful form. The demand side was far more inelastic than it is today.

The current environment is structurally more elastic on every dimension that matters. Oil’s share of primary energy is below 30%. The oil-gas substitution spread is at historically extreme levels. Strategic reserves are deploying at 3.3 million barrels per day. The installed base of alternatives, from electric vehicles to dual-fuel industrial equipment, is orders of magnitude larger than in 1980. The price required to destroy a given volume of demand is lower than the 1979 benchmark on every axis.

If the 1979 crisis required a tripling in real prices to eventually destroy 4-5 million barrels per day of demand over several years, and the current environment is structurally more responsive to price signals, then the clearing level for a 3-6 million barrel per day residual gap sits in the $130-170 range, not the $200+ that a naive elasticity extrapolation would suggest. For the full 8-10 million barrel per day gap (the scenario where offsets fail entirely), the answer is less knowable, because no modern analogue exists. The honest statement is that we are in uncharted territory above 6 million barrels per day of required destruction, and the price path in that region depends on how fast non-linear responses (government rationing, emergency industrial shutdowns, forced fuel-switching) arrive relative to the inventory drawdown.

We do not think the full gap scenario is the base case. We think offsets cover 60-80% of the hole in Month 1, and we think Trump capitulates before the residual gap widens enough to require $200 oil. But the base case is not the only case, and intellectual honesty demands that the tail be stated clearly: if offsets fail and the strait remains closed beyond Month 2, the market enters a demand-destruction regime with no modern precedent, and the clearing price is genuinely unknowable in advance.

## **III. The Substitution Arbitrage**

Henry Hub natural gas closed near $3.20 per MMBtu. On an energy-equivalent basis, that translates to roughly $18.50 per barrel of oil equivalent. Brent crude trades at $112. Physical Oman trades at $170.

Two ways to measure the dislocation, and both are screaming. The nominal price ratio (Brent divided by Henry Hub per MMBtu) is running above 35x, against a long-run average of roughly 15-25x. The energy-equivalent ratio (Brent at $112 divided by gas at $18.50/boe) is running above 6x, against a long-run average of roughly 3-4x. Both are at or near historical extremes.

And here is the paradox that makes this directly relevant to the reversion thesis: higher oil prices are structurally bearish for US natural gas. The EIA’s March 2026 STEO projects Henry Hub averaging $3.80 for 2026, 13% lower than the February forecast. Why? Because higher oil prices incentivise aggressive drilling in the Permian, and Permian wells co-produce natural gas. The EIA expects marketed natural gas production to average 121 billion cubic feet per day in 2026, rising to 124 Bcf/d in 2027, with growth originating primarily from the Haynesville, Permian, and Appalachia regions. War with Iran is, paradoxically, deeply bearish for the price of US natural gas.

A caveat is warranted. If US LNG export terminals run at maximum capacity to backfill the Qatar hole, and the new trains at Corpus Christi and Golden Pass ramp on schedule, that feedgas demand absorbs a significant share of the incremental associated gas before it reaches the domestic market.

The net effect on Henry Hub depends on the sequencing: LNG feedgas demand increases immediately as existing terminals maximise throughput, while the associated gas supply from accelerated Permian drilling builds over 6-12 months. In the near term, the LNG pull likely dominates, and Henry Hub is less bearish than a naive associated-gas model would suggest.

In the medium term, if Permian drilling responds as aggressively as the EIA projects, the associated gas supply eventually overwhelms the incremental LNG demand. The structural bearish case holds on an 18-month horizon. It may not hold on a 6-month one.

This is the substitution trade in its purest form. Oil and natural gas do the same thing at the molecular level: they are burned to produce heat to do work. The engines that burn them are currently specialised, which is precisely why the spread exists. But the crisis is compressing substitution timelines that would normally take years into quarters. Dual-fuel engine retrofits for heavy transport. Accelerated LNG bunkering infrastructure for shipping. Gas-to-liquids investment. Power generation fuel-switching, already happening in real time across Southeast Asian grids. Industrial boiler conversions. Every incremental unit of demand that migrates from oil to gas narrows the spread, destroys oil demand, and builds structural gas demand.

The kicker is the LNG export capacity coming online into this environment. Cheniere’s Corpus Christi Stage 3 Train 5 loaded its first cargo in February. Golden Pass Train 1 is expected to commission this month. The Hormuz closure has sent European and Asian LNG prices sharply higher while simultaneously disrupting Qatar’s massive North Field gas exports following the strikes on the shared South Pars geological structure. The US is becoming the marginal energy supplier to the world through the back door of the gas market while everyone stares at the crude oil price.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!tvBl!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1a41071b-e6ca-44ef-beaf-0bd029239028_1406x786.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!tvBl!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1a41071b-e6ca-44ef-beaf-0bd029239028_1406x786.png)

The sceptic’s objection is obvious: substitution at scale takes years, not months. Correct. But markets price the margin, not the installed base. The forward curve knows that every additional month of $100+ oil pulls investment into gas substitution that will never come back. The demand lost to substitution during a crisis does not return when prices normalise. It is structurally destroyed. This is the ratchet effect, and it is why every major oil spike in history has been followed by a glut that lasts longer than anyone expected.

The historical record is unambiguous on this point. After the 1979 oil shock, OECD oil demand fell by roughly 6 million barrels per day over four years as power generators switched to coal and gas, industrial users reconfigured boilers, and efficiency standards tightened. That demand never returned.

When oil prices collapsed in 1986, OECD consumption was still 15% below its 1979 peak. The 2011-2014 period of sustained $100+ crude drove a second, quieter wave of gas-for-oil substitution in Middle Eastern and Southeast Asian power generation.

The current crisis compresses that same substitution logic into a more violent timeframe, with a wider starting spread (oil-gas ratios above 30x versus historical norms of 6-10x) and more mature LNG infrastructure to absorb the shift. The question is not whether substitution happens. It is how many million barrels per day of demand it permanently removes, and whether the answer is 1-2 (as in 2011-2014) or 4-6 (as in 1979-1983).

This matters for the reversion thesis because substitution is the mechanism that converts a temporary price spike into a permanent demand impairment. The physical reckoning we described in Section II is real and imminent. But every week it persists, it is building the conditions for its own reversal, one industrial boiler conversion, one LNG bunkering contract, one dual-fuel retrofit at a time. The reversion is not an external event that rescues the market. It is endogenous to the crisis itself.

## **IV. The Second-Order Map**

If the reversion thesis is correct on a 12-18 month horizon (and we believe it is), the obvious trade is to fade the spike in crude futures. But the obvious trade is rarely the best trade, because it requires precise timing, significant margin capacity, and the willingness to sit through a potential move to $240+ before your thesis materialises. The traders who were right about the direction of oil two Mondays ago and lost their positions on the $35 intraday swing can speak to the lethality of being right on destination and wrong on path.

The more interesting question is: where are the second-order dislocations that offer asymmetric exposure to the same thesis with less path dependency?

**Tanker equities.** VLCC spot rates have exploded to over $400,000 per day, with reported fixtures approaching $700,000. Theoretical freight costs on key Middle East Gulf routes have risen from approximately $3 per barrel to $11, pushing transport to roughly 14% of delivered crude cost versus 4% in January. Tanker equities have responded, but the market is underpricing the structural persistence of elevated rates across every resolution scenario.

Even if the strait reopens next month, the logistical unwind is itself a rate-supportive event.

The IEA estimates 238 laden oil tankers are currently inside the Gulf, holding approximately 186 million barrels of crude awaiting transit.

When the strait reopens, every one of those vessels attempts to unload into the same destination ports simultaneously, creating bottleneck congestion reminiscent of the COVID-era port queues that sustained elevated freight rates for quarters, not weeks. Add the structural shift toward Atlantic Basin loading (US Gulf to Asia, US Gulf to Europe) that the crisis has accelerated: these are longer ton-mile voyages on routes that were already growing pre-crisis. The demand for vessel capacity does not revert to the pre-crisis baseline even in the most benign resolution.

There is a further mechanism the market is underpricing: the insurance lag. War risk premiums in the Gulf are currently running at 1-3% of hull value per transit depending on the quoting window, up from basis points in peacetime. When the strait reopens, the mines do not clear themselves overnight, and the insurance markets do not normalise the morning after a ceasefire announcement.

Historical precedent from the Tanker War of the 1980s and the 2019 Fujairah limpet mine attacks suggests war risk premiums remain elevated for 4-8 weeks after hostilities formally cease, and comprehensive hull coverage takes longer still to reprice. During that window, effective shipping costs remain at multiples of peacetime rates even though physical transit is theoretically possible.

Shipowners who can offer insured tonnage in the immediate post-ceasefire window capture a scarcity premium that the spot rate headline will understate.

This extends the elevated-rate environment for tanker equities by 1-2 months beyond whatever the market is pricing as the ‘end of the crisis,’ which in the Taco scenario means rates remain structurally elevated through Q3 2026 at minimum. This is not a bet on the war. It is a bet on the geography of the barrel, which has shifted structurally regardless of how the war ends.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!tA88!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe41ef2b9-c662-479f-8924-61e5973a996b_1403x642.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!tA88!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe41ef2b9-c662-479f-8924-61e5973a996b_1403x642.png)

**LNG equities and infrastructure.** If the natural gas substitution thesis plays out even partially, the beneficiaries are the companies that own the infrastructure connecting cheap US gas to expensive international markets. LNG export terminal operators, floating storage and regasification units, and gas pipeline operators serving the Gulf Coast export corridor are positioned at the intersection of two structural trends: the crisis-driven demand for non-oil energy and the medium-term growth in global gas demand that was already in the forecast before the war began.

The scale of the Ras Laffan damage has become clearer in the days since the strikes. QatarEnergy CEO al-Kaabi confirmed that two of Qatar’s 14 LNG trains and one of two gas-to-liquids facilities sustained damage severe enough to require 3-5 years of repair, removing 12.8 million tonnes per year of LNG from the global market. The critical constraint is the destruction of the cryogenic cold boxes, bespoke manufactured components with multi-year procurement and fabrication lead times. QatarEnergy has declared force majeure on long-term contracts covering deliveries to Italy, Belgium, South Korea, and China for up to five years.

Replacement costs run into the multi-billion dollar range, with procurement timelines for bespoke cryogenic components measured in years rather than months. Even if hostilities ceased tomorrow, al-Kaabi stated that resuming LNG loading operations would take a minimum of three to four months.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!S8Fb!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fca6c75c2-a662-435e-b3e6-ce1cbf7c3c7a_1403x788.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!S8Fb!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fca6c75c2-a662-435e-b3e6-ce1cbf7c3c7a_1403x788.png)

**Further second-order effects.** Two additional transmission channels deserve flagging, though we will develop both at full length in dedicated pieces rather than give them the abbreviated treatment they would get here.

The first is industrial metals. The logic runs on two tracks simultaneously: accelerated energy infrastructure buildout (LNG terminals, pipelines, grid capacity, transport electrification) front-loads copper and aluminium procurement into a market already drawing down exchange-visible inventories, while the global diesel price spike driven by the Hormuz closure blows out operating costs for mining operations in Chile, Peru, the DRC, and Zambia. The demand accelerant and the supply disruptor arrive at the same time. We developed the structural inventory fragmentation thesis across COMEX, LME, and SHFE in “The Copper Inventory Paradox”; the crisis is stress-testing every vulnerability we identified.

The second is fertiliser, and it may be the second-order effect with the longest tail and the least market attention.

A third channel, the liquid helium supply shock and its implications for semiconductor fabrication in Taiwan and South Korea, our helium research can be found here: [LINK]

**Refined product spreads.** This may be the most immediate and tradeable dislocation. Iraq’s force majeure and the Kuwaiti refinery strikes have created acute tightness in middle distillates that will outlast the crude disruption itself. Diesel crack spreads have blown from $30-40 (already elevated) to $50-65 per barrel depending on region, with European gasoil leading and the 2022 peak of $80-100 well within reach. European IEA product reserves provide a partial buffer, but the released volumes are sized for peacetime shortfalls, not for replacing the Middle East’s role as Europe’s primary post-sanctions diesel supplier.

Asian refiners, having cut throughput to 65% to avoid forced shutdowns, are simultaneously reducing product supply at the exact moment physical crude scarcity is about to arrive.

The timeline is asymmetric in a way the market is not pricing. A refinery cutting from 90% to 65% throughput can do so in days. Restarting from reduced throughput to full capacity takes 2-4 weeks under normal conditions; a cold restart from full shutdown, which becomes likely if crude supply is physically unavailable for more than 4-6 weeks, takes 6-12 weeks and involves significant maintenance risk. Product tightness will outlast crude tightness in every scenario, including the Taco, because the refineries that cut throughput today will not be running at full capacity the day the strait reopens. The Middle East had become Europe’s primary diesel supplier after the Russian diesel import ban; that supply chain has been obliterated.

**Fertiliser and the food inflation transmission.**

The Strait of Hormuz handles roughly 30-35% of global seaborne urea trade. The blockade, compounded by China’s pre-emptive ban on fertiliser exports, has driven urea prices up approximately 40% since the crisis began. The timing is brutal: Northern Hemisphere spring planting runs March through May, and farmers are making input purchasing decisions right now. Unlike oil, where demand destruction can operate in real time through price, fertiliser application is binary and seasonal.

A farmer who cannot afford or source urea in April does not apply half as much. They plant fewer acres or accept lower yields. That decision, made in the next 4-6 weeks, locks in a grain supply outcome for the entire 2026-2027 crop year. The transmission to consumer prices operates on a lag that could produce an 18-month inflation problem from a 3-month strait closure. We will develop the trade implications, including nitrogen producers, grain futures, and agricultural equipment names, in a forthcoming dedicated piece.

## **V. The Fog Premium**

There is one more component to the current pricing environment that deserves separate treatment, because it may be the least understood and most consequential: the information premium.

Markets price uncertainty. This is axiomatic. But the Iran conflict has introduced a dimension of informational asymmetry that is genuinely novel. We are watching a major military conflict play out in an environment where the information supply itself is being actively manipulated in real time by all participants.

Consider what the average institutional energy trader is working with. US military briefings that have been inconsistent and occasionally contradictory, with the gap between Hegseth’s declarations of imminent total victory and more measured Pentagon assessments wide enough to drive a VLCC through. Iranian state media broadcasting damage assessments and strategic messaging calibrated to erode US domestic political support. Israeli military censorship that has made it illegal to film incoming missiles or infrastructure damage. And a social media ecosystem that has demonstrably altered its algorithmic distribution of war-related content mid-conflict, suppressing raw footage that was circulating freely in the first 48 hours.

The fog is not accidental. It is strategic on the part of every belligerent. And it carries a measurable market cost, one that manifests not as a premium on crude prices (which are arguably suppressed by the same fog) but as a liquidity vacuum. When institutional capital cannot accurately assess the state of play, it steps aside. Volumes thin. And in thin markets, the suppression mechanisms we identified in Section II, the floating storage release, the SPR drawdown, the political jawboning, hit harder. A 140 million barrel floating storage release into a liquid, well-informed market is a speed bump. The same release into a market where half the institutional flow is sitting on its hands is a wall.

But the fog and the suppression are not independent forces. They are coupled. The fog amplifies policy interventions that are themselves finite. As the offset waterfall depletes (Section VI), so does the fog’s ability to hold the paper market below physical reality. They expire together. This is the mechanism the market has not priced: not just that the buffers run out, but that the information environment that made them disproportionately effective runs out at the same time.

The fog will clear in stages. Two of those stages, Iran’s sustained strike capability and US political signalling, are the structural gates that determine which scenario we are in. We formalise those in Section VI. But two nearer-term signals deserve attention first, not because they shift the scenario distribution, but because they will kill the complacent narrative that $112 Brent reflects reality.

The first is Asian inventory data. Singapore onshore storage figures, published weekly by Enterprise Singapore, will be the first official dataset to register the arrival of the supply air pocket we described in Section II. Given transit times of roughly two weeks from the Gulf to Singapore, the drawdowns may already be underway. This is not a prediction. It is arithmetic.

When the numbers print, every sell-side note arguing that the futures market “has this priced in” becomes toilet paper. The physical-paper divergence compresses upward.

The second is China’s posture. Beijing banned refined product exports in the first days of the crisis. That is a hoarding signal. A reversal of that ban would tell you that Chinese intelligence assessments, which are almost certainly better-informed on Iranian and Gulf-state conditions than anything available through Western channels, have concluded the disruption is approaching resolution. Watch the policy, not the satellite imagery.

The most probable sequencing: the confirmatory signals arrive first (Asian inventory within days, China’s posture over weeks), while the structural gates that determine which world we are in take longer to resolve. The fog narrows as each signal arrives and each gate opens. It will not vanish in a single session.

## **VI. Positioning the Reversion**

The analytical framework we have built across the Epic Fury series was constructed precisely for this moment. Let us use it honestly, which means assigning probabilities, explaining why we weight them as we do, and building the supply-side arithmetic that shows how the gap actually closes.

A note on what ‘closed’ means. The strait is not sealed with a padlock. The IEA reports transits have collapsed to less than 10% of pre-crisis flows, which implies some selective movement, likely Iranian-permitted, is occurring. For the purposes of our offset arithmetic, we treat sub-10% as functionally closed because the volumes are too small and too unreliable to build a supply plan around. But the distinction matters strategically: a strait that Iran can selectively open is a coercive instrument, not just a blockade. It gives Tehran leverage over individual buyers and transit requests that a full closure would not. The market has not yet priced the difference.

### **The Reversion Waterfall**

The reversion thesis is not a prayer. It is an arithmetic process with identifiable inputs, measurable flow rates, and hard time constraints. Here is the month-by-month supply-side picture as we see it.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!yIAG!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0685c719-843b-4810-8589-dfa599178313_2115x1183.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!yIAG!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0685c719-843b-4810-8589-dfa599178313_2115x1183.png)

*Month 1 (now through mid-April).* Available offsets: SPR coordinated release at 3.3 million bpd, Russian oil-on-water redirection at roughly 2 million bpd (decaying as floating inventory is absorbed), Iranian floating storage at roughly 2-3 million bpd realized over 30 days (140 million barrels under the Treasury general license, discounted for buyer reluctance and logistical friction), East-West pipeline flows at partial capacity (perhaps 3-4 million bpd net, under intermittent Iranian targeting), Fujairah pipeline at approximately 500,000 bpd incremental.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!l6JY!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7efea730-db8d-489a-ba0d-184dc1b9f0ce_1405x788.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!l6JY!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7efea730-db8d-489a-ba0d-184dc1b9f0ce_1405x788.png)

Note: the March 18-19 strikes require careful disaggregation. The SAMREF refinery at Yanbu is a 400,000 bpd downstream facility. Its destruction is devastating for refined product exports (and directly bullish for the crack spread thesis in Section IV) but does not necessarily impair the East-West pipeline’s crude bypass to the adjacent Yanbu crude terminal at Muajjiz, unless loading infrastructure or pipeline pump stations sustained collateral damage. Ras Tanura, on the Persian Gulf coast, is irrelevant to the Red Sea bypass; its suspension removes refining capacity on the Gulf side.

The net effect on crude bypass flows depends on damage assessments that are not yet public. We conservatively reduce the realistic upper bound of combined pipeline offset flows from 3-4 million bpd toward 2.5-3.5 million bpd pending confirmation. Gross offset: approximately 10-12 million bpd on paper.

But the Russian redirection is a one-time stock draw, the Iranian floating barrels are finite, and the pipeline flows are under active military threat. Realistic net offset: 7-9 million bpd, reverting toward the lower end if subsequent damage assessments confirm impairment to crude loading infrastructure at Yanbu.

One additional near-term supply increment: drilled-but-uncompleted wells. At $130+ oil, every available DUC in the Permian, Eagle Ford, and Bakken gets a frac crew. First oil from DUC completions arrives in 30-90 days, significantly faster than the 6-month minimum for new drill-to-complete cycles. The available inventory is smaller than in prior cycles, the DUC count has drawn down substantially since 2020, but the incremental contribution is real: perhaps 100-200,000 bpd within 60-90 days. This does not meaningfully close the 1-3 million bpd residual gap, but it is analytically dishonest to omit it.

Against a net supply loss of roughly 9 million bpd (the IEA’s total liquids shut-in estimate, after accounting for the overlap between production curtailments and transit disruption), the residual gap in Month 1 is 1-3 million bpd, widening toward the upper end of that range following the confirmed Yanbu and Ras Tanura strikes.

This is the period where the offsets are at maximum strength. It is also the period where the Asia air pocket lands. Brent likely tests $130-145.

*Months 2-3 (mid-April through June).* The one-time buffers are depleted: Russian floating stock is absorbed, Iranian floating barrels are sold or stranded. The SPR continues at 3.3 million bpd but is now 40-60% drawn down and approaching exhaustion. If the strait remains closed, the offset waterfall drops to approximately 5-7 million bpd (SPR plus pipeline flows). Against the same 9 million bpd hole, the residual gap widens to 2-4 million bpd.

Beyond the DUC completions already underway, the lag from price signal to new drill-to-complete first oil is a minimum of 6 months; we see perhaps 100-150,000 bpd of incremental tight oil by June, with Canadian oil sands maintenance deferrals adding another 150,000 bpd. These are rounding errors. Demand destruction at prevailing prices ($130-170 Brent) is the primary balancing mechanism, running at perhaps 2-4 million bpd based on the elasticity estimates in Section II. This is the grind. Product spreads are at their widest. Asian refinery shutdowns peak.

*Months 4-6 (July through September).* If the strait is still closed (our Unthinkable scenario), the SPR is exhausted or rationed to a trickle. The offset waterfall collapses to pipeline flows plus whatever incremental non-OPEC production has materialised (perhaps 300-500,000 bpd from US shale, plus marginal contributions from Brazil, Guyana, and Argentina). The residual gap is 4-6+ million bpd, and demand destruction by price is doing essentially all of the balancing. Brent in this scenario is $180-220, with the risk of an overshoot to $250 if rationing and emergency fuel-switching responses arrive slower than the elasticity math assumes.

*Months 6-12 (Q4 2026 through Q1 2027).* If the strait reopens at any point in this window, shut-in production begins returning, but not all of it. Petroleum engineers warn that forced, uncontrolled shut-ins of the duration we are discussing risk permanent reservoir damage through asphaltene precipitation and pressure depletion. The shut-ins in Kuwait and Iraq were sudden, driven by storage maximisation rather than controlled maintenance protocols. Long-term recovery rates for these fields could be permanently reduced by an as-yet-unquantifiable margin, meaning the eventual supply recovery may fall 5-15% short of the pre-crisis baseline. Meanwhile, US shale response is now fully in the pipeline: the EIA’s 500,000 bpd upward revision for 2027 production begins arriving. Substitution effects (Section III) are registering in gas demand data. The demand destroyed at $130+ does not return. The market transitions from deficit to balance, and then, as every post-crisis commodity market in the modern record has done, overshoots into surplus.

*Months 12-18 (Q1-Q2 2027).* The glut builds. US production at 13.8 million bpd. Substitution-driven demand impairment is structural. SPR refill buying provides a floor but not a ceiling. Brent settles in the $80-95 range, above the pre-crisis $75 baseline because the 400 million barrel SPR drawdown has erased the 2025 surplus build, OPEC spare capacity has been partially impaired, and the market permanently re-embeds a strait risk premium. This is the reversion. Not to the prior equilibrium, but to a new one, scarred by the crisis but fundamentally governed by the same forces that have resolved every supply shock in the modern era.

### **Scenario Architecture and Update Triggers**

Readers who have followed the prior three installments will note a methodological shift here. Parts I ([Operation Epic Fury](https://open.substack.com/pub/tscsw/p/operation-epic-fury?r=203zi2&utm_campaign=post&utm_medium=web)) through III ([Oil Isn’t The Trade](https://open.substack.com/pub/tscsw/p/oil-isnt-the-trade?r=203zi2&utm_campaign=post&utm_medium=web)) assigned fixed scenario probabilities (and updated them between installments as events warranted).

We are moving away from that approach, not because the prior estimates were wrong at the time of publication, but because the crisis has entered a phase where the governing variable, whether Iran can sustain precision strikes against Gulf export alternatives, is genuinely unobservable on any timescale shorter than 2-4 weeks.

Assigning a fixed probability to that capability would require us to know things about Iranian munitions inventories, targeting infrastructure survivability, and command-and-control resilience that we do not know and that Western intelligence assessments have been inconsistent about.

What we can do, and what is more useful to readers allocating capital in real time, is define the observable gates that will resolve the uncertainty, specify how our scenario weights update when each gate opens, and build trade expressions that perform across the full conditional distribution rather than requiring a bet on a single branch. The scenarios themselves are unchanged. The architecture around them has matured.

We see three paths from here. We are not going to pretend we know which one we are on.

The primary source of uncertainty is not physical or economic but political: whether a single decision-maker chooses to escalate or withdraw, on a timeline driven by domestic political calculus that is opaque to external observers. Individual leadership decisions are the lowest-confidence forecast class in all of macro, and we do not pretend to have edge on this question. What we do have edge on is the physical market mechanics that play out conditional on that decision, which is why the framework below is built around observable gates rather than fixed probability assignments.

The resolution of this crisis is governed by two variables that are currently unobservable but will become legible within defined windows. They are sequential: the first informs the second.

**Gate 1: Iran’s sustained strike capability against Gulf export alternatives.** This was the single most important variable in the crisis, and as of this week, it has resolved.

The initial strikes on Yanbu and Kuwaiti refineries in the first days of the conflict demonstrated reach. We wrote in Section V that reach and sustained operational capability are different things, and that the question was whether Iran could maintain a tempo of precision strikes against bypass infrastructure or whether the initial wave represented the bulk of its available precision munitions. We said: watch Yanbu.

On March 18, Israel struck Iran’s South Pars gas field, the first direct hit on core fossil fuel production infrastructure in the conflict. Within 24 hours, Iran launched a coordinated retaliatory wave against the exact bypass infrastructure we identified: the SAMREF refinery at Yanbu (the Saudi Aramco/ExxonMobil joint venture, a 400,000 bpd downstream facility adjacent to the Kingdom’s Red Sea crude export terminal) and the Ras Tanura refinery on the Persian Gulf coast, forcing suspension of operations at both. Maritime logistics advisories from Inchcape Shipping Services confirmed the strikes and estimated Saudi output reductions of approximately 2 million bpd from the second wave. The critical question for crude bypass flows is whether the strikes damaged only downstream refining capacity or also impaired the crude loading infrastructure at the Yanbu terminal itself; damage assessments are ongoing.

Gate 1 has resolved toward sustained Iranian capability. The “mission accomplished” off-ramp is no longer politically viable when Iran is visibly destroying allied export infrastructure faster than it can be repaired. The scenario space has compressed. The Taco is no longer the modal outcome.

**Gate 2: US political signalling (now the governing variable).** With Gate 1 resolved, Gate 2 takes the form we described for the adverse branch: the political question is no longer “does Trump withdraw?” but “does he escalate further or accept a grinding stalemate?” The indicators are no longer domestic rhetoric but military posture: carrier group redeployments, mobilisation of additional air assets, and whether US or Israeli targeting extends to Iranian oil export infrastructure on Kharg Island.

The benign branch of Gate 2, the rhetorical migration from “total victory” to “mission accomplished,” is not fully closed, but it requires a sustained absence of further Iranian strikes over the coming weeks to become politically viable again. Congressional War Powers invocations, bipartisan pressure, and betting market compression on conflict duration remain secondary indicators worth tracking. But the burden of proof has shifted: the default path from here is the Grind, and it takes active de-escalation to move probability mass back toward the Taco.

**Three scenarios, conditionally weighted.**

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!w2iJ!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4f8b49be-864d-403f-87ca-a1d7ad18c3b6_2117x1179.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!w2iJ!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4f8b49be-864d-403f-87ca-a1d7ad18c3b6_2117x1179.png)

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!dNzE!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F10f8e58e-0044-41f9-9af0-13b5cb1d61f9_2117x1179.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!dNzE!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F10f8e58e-0044-41f9-9af0-13b5cb1d61f9_2117x1179.png)

Scenario 1: The Taco (20%). Trump declares victory, citing the destruction of Iran’s nuclear enrichment and ballistic missile capabilities, and initiates a withdrawal within 3-5 weeks. This scenario requires a sustained absence of further Iranian strikes and a political willingness to absorb the optics of withdrawal after Ras Tanura and Yanbu. The strait functionally reopens over a further 2-3 weeks as mines are cleared and insurance is reinstated. Shut-in production returns over 4-8 weeks.

Brent path: spikes to $130-145 as the air pocket lands, then grinds lower to $85-95 by Q3 2026.

Post-crisis floor sits around $85-90, above the pre-crisis $75 baseline, because the 400 million barrel SPR drawdown has erased the 2025 surplus build and the market permanently re-embeds a strait risk premium.

Scenario 2: The Grind (50%, now the base case). The strait remains closed for 2-4 months. The March 18-19 strikes on Ras Tanura and Yanbu SAMREF have already compromised the primary Red Sea bypass route, consistent with this scenario’s core mechanic. Iran sustains harassment of Gulf export alternatives without destroying them at scale. Offsets cover 40-60% of the supply gap per the waterfall above, leaving a residual hole of 2-4 million bpd by Months 2-3. Asian refiners enter rolling shutdowns. Product spreads blow out further, with diesel cracks testing the 2022 highs of $80-100 per barrel. Brent path: $140-170 over the next 4-8 weeks, sustained above $120 for a full quarter, with reversion to $90-105 by Q1 2027 as substitution, demand destruction, and the US shale response begin to bite.

Scenario 3: The Unthinkable (30%). Escalation continues beyond the March 18-19 wave. Iranian strikes reach Abqaiq or the Habshan-Fujairah pipeline. US or Israeli targeting extends to Kharg Island, Iran’s primary crude export terminal. The strait remains closed for 6+ months. The SPR is exhausted. Demand destruction arithmetic from Section II applies in full: Brent prints $200+. Global recession. Demand destruction becomes structural rather than cyclical. This is the scenario where the reversion overshoots to the downside, where the eventual glut is deep and prolonged, because the demand destroyed at $200 does not return when prices normalise.

**How the gates update the weights.**

In our initial framework, we specified two conditional branches. If no second-wave strikes materialised within three weeks, the Taco would become the strong prior at 50-60%, with the Grind at 30-35% and the Unthinkable at 10-15%. If a second wave landed on major export infrastructure, the Taco would compress to 15-20%, the Grind would rise to 45-55%, and the Unthinkable would move to 25-35%. The second wave landed. On Ras Tanura and on Yanbu, within 24 hours of the South Pars strike, against the exact facilities we told readers to watch. The adverse branch is now the operating reality. Our current working distribution: Taco 20%, Grind 50%, Unthinkable 30%. These are not static. A sustained absence of further Iranian strikes over the next 2-3 weeks would allow some probability mass to migrate back toward the Taco, perhaps to 30-35%. A further escalation, particularly strikes on Abqaiq or on the Habshan-Fujairah pipeline, shifts mass toward the Unthinkable, potentially to 35-40%. But the centre of gravity has moved. The Grind is now the base case. We said we would publish a decision tree that reprices as information arrives. It just repriced.

One observation spans all three paths. The near-term physical reckoning is not a scenario. It is a constant. It arrives before any political resolution translates into physical barrels, before any ceasefire clears a single mine, before any shut-in well reopens. The question that separates the three scenarios is not whether the pain arrives. It is how long it persists, and what it permanently destroys on the way through.

### **The Trade Matrix**

**Tanker equities** work in all three scenarios. In the Taco, the logistical unwind (238 laden tankers transiting out simultaneously, port congestion, the structural shift toward longer Atlantic Basin routes) sustains elevated rates for quarters after the strait reopens. In the Grind and the Unthinkable, spot rates remain at multiples of historical norms. This is the highest-conviction cross-scenario position in our framework.

**LNG infrastructure** is positive in all three but asymmetrically so. In the Taco, the structural case for US LNG export capacity is reinforced but not transformed. In the Grind and the Unthinkable, where the oil-gas substitution arbitrage begins closing in earnest and Qatar’s North Field impairment persists for years regardless of the strait’s status, US Gulf Coast LNG becomes the marginal energy supply source for the world. The duration of the crisis determines whether this is a 20% re-rating or a 50% one.

**Refined product spreads** are the most immediate dislocation. Diesel and jet fuel cracks are already at extreme levels and worsen in every scenario. Even in the Taco, the Asian refinery throughput cuts have created a product supply deficit that takes weeks to unwind. In the Grind and the Unthinkable, product spreads are the primary transmission mechanism through which the crisis reaches consumers in advanced economies.

**Industrial metals**, particularly copper, are a Grind and Unthinkable overlay on the same infrastructure thesis, developed separately in our forthcoming work.

**Naked long crude** is the highest-reward, highest-risk expression. It works spectacularly in the Grind and the Unthinkable and is ruinous on path in the Taco if the withdrawal announcement arrives while you are leveraged. We are not in the business of recommending positions that require you to survive a $35 adverse move to collect on your thesis.

The expected value across this distribution points clearly: position for the physical reckoning that arrives in every scenario, express the reversion thesis through second-order assets with less path dependency, and respect the fact that the market will break everything that is fragile before it clears.
