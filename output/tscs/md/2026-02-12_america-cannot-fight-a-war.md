## **What Is “Reverse Perestroika”?**

In 1985, Mikhail Gorbachev inherited an empire that was militarily powerful but economically broken. The Soviet Union could project force anywhere on the globe, but it could not feed its own people or manufacture a decent pair of shoes. Gorbachev’s solution was perestroika (restructuring) and glasnost (openness): loosen political controls, liberalize the economy, integrate with the West, convert swords into ploughshares. Trade guns for butter, as the economists say.

It did not end well for the Soviet Union.

Michael Every’s argument is that the United States in 2025 faces the mirror image of Gorbachev’s problem. America is economically dominant (still the world’s largest economy, still home to the reserve currency, still the center of global capital markets) but militarily hollowed out, industrially atrophied, and strategically dependent on supply chains it does not control. The U.S. has plenty of butter. What it lacks is guns, and the industrial base to make them.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!G-L_!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5ac7a501-aa2b-4161-977f-db4c9ad6ddad_1405x774.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!G-L_!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5ac7a501-aa2b-4161-977f-db4c9ad6ddad_1405x774.png)

Reverse Perestroika, then, is the deliberate inversion of Gorbachev’s project: tighten economic controls, reshore industrial capacity, rebuild the defence-industrial base, accept short-term economic pain for long-term strategic resilience. Trade butter for guns.

Whether you think this is visionary or delusional (I genuinely cannot decide, and I suspect that ambiguity is the correct analytical posture), the data increasingly suggest it is what is actually happening.

Not as a coherent master plan, mind you. More like a series of policy choices that, when you squint, form a recognizable pattern. But I want to be disciplined about this, because pattern-matching is the most dangerous drug in macro analysis. So let me state upfront what would falsify this thesis: if the administration cuts tariffs back below 5% weighted average, abandons defence procurement expansion, and the Fed returns to large-scale asset purchases under Warsh, then Reverse Perestroika is dead and I was telling you a good story, not doing good analysis.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!DFXR!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9ff65d57-e745-48ad-86f2-21cb93f29429_1405x776.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!DFXR!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9ff65d57-e745-48ad-86f2-21cb93f29429_1405x776.png)

If the share of C&I lending as a percentage of total bank credit has not moved above 25% by the end of 2027, the restructuring is not happening regardless of what anyone in Washington says. I will revisit these markers explicitly. Hold me to it.

## **The 80/20 Problem: Where American Credit Actually Goes**

Every’s starting point is a deceptively simple observation about the structure of U.S. bank credit. He argues that roughly 80% of American bank lending goes to “non-productive” purposes (real estate, financial assets, consumption) while only 20% flows to commercial and industrial activity.

This is one of those claims that sounds like polemical exaggeration until you pull the Federal Reserve’s H.8 data and discover it is almost exactly right.

Here is what the December 2025 H.8 release actually shows:

Commercial and industrial loans: $2,710 billion, or 14.3% of total bank credit. Real estate loans: $5,743 billion (30.4%). Securities holdings: $5,623 billion (30.1%). Consumer loans: $1,858 billion (9.8%). Everything else: $2,909 billion (15.5%).

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!INwH!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1f630203-3541-40f5-82f7-b6d4c0e1526d_1405x786.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!INwH!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1f630203-3541-40f5-82f7-b6d4c0e1526d_1405x786.png)

If you strip out securities and look only at loans and leases (which is arguably the more relevant denominator for credit allocation purposes), C&I comes to 20.4%. Exactly what Every claims.

Now, you can quibble with the “non-productive” label. Commercial real estate supports business operations. Consumer credit facilitates demand. Securities holdings include Treasuries that fund government spending. Fair enough. But the directional point is devastating: the American financial system is overwhelmingly configured to inflate asset prices, not to build things.

Now, the obvious rebuttal, and it is a serious one: the United States is a services economy. It has been one for decades. Credit composition reflects economic structure, not a distortion of it. A country that generates a quarter of global GDP through finance, technology, healthcare, and professional services is not ‘broken’ simply because its banks lend accordingly. I take this point. But here is where it gets interesting: even within the C&I category, manufacturing’s share of commercial and industrial lending has been declining relative to services-oriented borrowers.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!f9tc!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F790863b8-4c79-4ce7-9ba1-08729bf2f6a3_1405x785.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!f9tc!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F790863b8-4c79-4ce7-9ba1-08729bf2f6a3_1405x785.png)

The H.8 data does not disaggregate this cleanly, but the Federal Reserve’s Senior Loan Officer Survey consistently shows tighter lending standards for manufacturing and heavy industry than for services and technology firms. The problem is not just that C&I is 20% of total credit. It is that within that 20%, the industrial core, the firms that actually bend metal and pour concrete, are competing for capital against software companies with 80% gross margins. The financial system is not just tilted toward asset inflation over production. It is tilted, within the productive economy itself, toward the capital-light over the capital-heavy. That is the structural problem Reverse Perestroika needs to solve, and it is considerably harder than the top-line 80/20 split suggests.

This is not a recent development. This composition has been remarkably stable for over a decade. It is structural. And it means that any serious attempt to reindustrialize America, to rebuild manufacturing capacity, to reshore supply chains, requires either a fundamental reallocation of credit (which means someone loses) or a massive parallel financing mechanism outside the banking system.

I want to be specific about who loses, because this is the part of the Reverse Perestroika thesis that keeps me up at night. Real estate is not just 30% of bank credit. It is the primary collateral underpinning the solvency of American regional banks. Nearly half of U.S. regional banks currently exceed the 300% CRE-to-Tier 1 capital threshold that regulators consider “excessive.” Office CMBS delinquencies hit 11.8% in late 2025, a record that exceeds 2008 crisis levels.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Ah5s!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F392a0ac9-35a1-40f3-8625-b9bee07fd7c7_1405x783.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Ah5s!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F392a0ac9-35a1-40f3-8625-b9bee07fd7c7_1405x783.png)

There is a $1.5 to 1.8 trillion wall of CRE loans maturing in 2025 and 2026, much of it originated at 3 to 4% and now refinancing at 6 to 7% or higher. You cannot aggressively reallocate credit away from real estate without crushing the asset values that underpin regional bank solvency. The sequencing matters enormously: you need to build the new industrial lending channels before withdrawing from the old ones, or you get something resembling the S&L crisis of the 1980s, where over a thousand institutions failed. The administration may understand this. I have seen no evidence that they do.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!GOx8!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F05ae20cd-b559-400c-bc3d-8f3a36bfc3d7_1405x786.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!GOx8!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F05ae20cd-b559-400c-bc3d-8f3a36bfc3d7_1405x786.png)

Keep that thought. We will come back to it.

## **Ammunition Problem: Three Days of War**

If the credit allocation data tells you the economy is not configured for production, the munitions data tells you the consequences are existential.

Every argues the U.S. cannot fight a single major war for more than a few weeks. The data says he is being generous.

The U.S. has theoretical capacity to produce 40,000 155mm projectile casings per month. That sounds like a lot until you learn two things. First, Ukraine was consuming 6,000 to 10,000 shells per day at peak intensity. Second, propellant charge shortages mean actual complete round production was roughly 18,000 per month as of September 2024, less than half of casing capacity. Do the arithmetic: at actual production rates, the United States can sustain Ukraine-intensity combat for approximately two to three days.

But I want to be precise here, because the artillery comparison, while viscerally effective, can mislead if you let it. The United States does not plan to fight Russia in a land war across the Donbas. The Pentagon’s operational concept for a Taiwan contingency is primarily air and naval: long-range anti-ship missiles, submarine-launched cruise missiles, air superiority platforms, and electronic warfare. The relevant production gap is not just in 155mm shells but in precision-guided munitions across the entire kill chain.

And that gap is, if anything, worse. The CSIS ran a series of war games in January 2023 simulating a Taiwan Strait conflict. Their finding: the U.S. would likely exhaust its long-range precision-guided munitions in less than one week. LRASM production rates remain classified but are widely estimated in the low hundreds per year. JASSM production is ramping but from a base that was designed for counterinsurgency tempo, not peer conflict. The Harpoon inventory is aging. The Maritime Strike Tomahawk is only now entering fleet service.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!gYpz!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2a8dfeee-92bd-4e68-87a5-f4de69a87d54_1405x786.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!gYpz!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2a8dfeee-92bd-4e68-87a5-f4de69a87d54_1405x786.png)

One week. For the defence of Taiwan. Against the country that manufactures most of the world’s advanced semiconductors.

The bottlenecks are almost comically specific, and they span both the artillery and precision munitions supply chains. All U.S. artillery propellant comes from a single General Dynamics facility in Valleyfield, Canada. Not in America. In Canada, the country we just slapped with 35% tariffs on non-USMCA goods. Now, to be precise: defense goods almost certainly remain exempt from these tariffs under the Defence Production Sharing Agreement (1956) and DFARS regulation 252.225-7013, which provide duty-free entry for Canadian defense products on a separate legal track from USMCA or IEEPA-based levies.

The propellant will keep flowing. But that is not the point. The point is that the sole source of a critical military input sits in a foreign jurisdiction at a moment of active trade hostility with that jurisdiction. Tariff exemptions are policy decisions, and policy decisions can be reversed by either side. The vulnerability is structural, not tariff-dependent.

The United States has not produced domestic TNT since the 1980s. Solid rocket motor production, which underpins virtually every guided missile in the inventory, depends on two facilities and a handful of specialty chemical suppliers with no redundancy.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!r6j9!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc56f296d-7cc2-4967-b006-141705071cee_1405x720.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!r6j9!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc56f296d-7cc2-4967-b006-141705071cee_1405x720.png)

This distinction matters for your portfolio, not just your worldview. If the binding constraint is artillery shells, the investment opportunity is in forging, casting, and energetics companies. If it is precision munitions, you are looking at guidance systems, solid rocket motor producers, seekerhead manufacturers, and the rare earth supply chain that feeds all of them. The answer, of course, is both. But the capital expenditure profile and the competitive dynamics are very different, and I intend to cover them separately in coming weeks.

House Appropriations Chairman Tom Cole put it bluntly: if the U.S. were in a short-term conflict, it would be short-term because there are not enough munitions to sustain a long-term fight.

This is not a talking point. This is a strategic vulnerability of the first order, and it is the kind of problem that does not get solved by financial engineering or monetary policy. It requires physical production capacity, skilled labour, secure supply chains, and years of investment. It requires, in Every’s framing, a reversal of forty years of policy that prioritized efficiency over resilience, consumption over production, finance over industry.

## **National Security Strategy**

In December 2025, the White House published its National Security Strategy. It is 29 pages long. Most of Washington treated it as boilerplate. It is not.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5EJy!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F52a1b468-6cce-4a72-806a-eefd7db24393_1405x752.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5EJy!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F52a1b468-6cce-4a72-806a-eefd7db24393_1405x752.png)

On Latin America, the document lays out what it calls “The Trump Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine,” stating that the goal is for partner nations to build up their domestic economies while a stronger Western hemisphere becomes an increasingly attractive market for American commerce and investment.

That is, on its face, a reasonable articulation of hemispheric economic integration. It is also, if you read it closely, a declaration that the U.S. intends to build a regional economic bloc capable of competing with (and excluding) Chinese supply chains. The commercial diplomacy framing is the velvet glove. The reshoring imperative is the iron fist.

On Europe, the document drops all pretence. It states that continental Europe has been losing share of global GDP, down from 25% in 1990 to 14% today.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!k2Sk!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdc7a956d-5015-4b8a-8e28-ed1907421b2d_1405x775.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!k2Sk!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdc7a956d-5015-4b8a-8e28-ed1907421b2d_1405x775.png)

But then it goes further, arguing that this economic decline is eclipsed by the real and more stark prospect of civilizational erasure. It lists migration, censorship, cratering birthrates, and loss of national identity, concluding that should present trends continue, the continent will be unrecognizable in twenty years or less.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!BrZm!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2b477b13-8870-4430-b5f3-f5ef1f19439d_1405x770.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!BrZm!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2b477b13-8870-4430-b5f3-f5ef1f19439d_1405x770.png)

## **The Dollar’s New Clothes: Stablecoins as Strategic Infrastructure**

What follows is not a pillar of the Reverse Perestroika thesis in the way that tariffs and defence spending are.

Nobody in the NSC sat down and designed stablecoin regulation as a tool of dollar hegemony. What happened is more interesting than that: a regulatory framework built primarily because the crypto lobby spent enormous sums and Congress likes bipartisan wins has produced, almost as a side effect, a piece of financial infrastructure that happens to serve the strategic objectives of Reverse Perestroika remarkably well.

The best macro trades are often found in these accidental alignments, where policy designed for one purpose creates structural consequences that nobody intended but that the market has not yet priced.

The total stablecoin market has grown from approximately $134 billion in January 2024 to over $306 billion in January 2026, a 130% increase in 24 months. USDT (Tether) holds $186 billion, USDC (Circle) holds $75 billion, and a growing tail of smaller issuers fills in the rest.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5AP5!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe6ca49df-e03e-4858-9f11-84a643cfcd6b_1405x771.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5AP5!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe6ca49df-e03e-4858-9f11-84a643cfcd6b_1405x771.png)

Here is the part that should make you sit up. Approximately 75 to 80% of major stablecoin reserves are now held in U.S. Treasury bills and Treasury-collateralized repos. Tether alone holds over $141 billion in Treasury exposure as of Q4 2025, making it the 17th largest holder of U.S. Treasuries globally, larger than South Korea. Combined USDT and USDC Treasury holdings represent roughly 3.5 to 4.5% of the entire T-bill market (approximately $6.5 trillion outstanding), depending on reserve composition assumptions.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Vwor!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1aacf468-7427-4107-ae41-ee119ac7208d_1405x785.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Vwor!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1aacf468-7427-4107-ae41-ee119ac7208d_1405x785.png)

The GENIUS Act (Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins) was signed into law on July 18, 2025, with overwhelming bipartisan support: 68-30 in the Senate, 308-122 in the House. It requires 100% reserves in dollars, short-term T-bills of 93 days or less, or repos. Monthly attestations. Annual audits. Explicit exclusion from SEC and CFTC oversight.

Think about what this means. The U.S. government has created a regulated framework that incentivizes the creation of dollar-denominated digital payment instruments, backed by short-term Treasury debt, operating outside the traditional banking system. Every stablecoin issued is a synthetic dollar that creates demand for T-bills. Every transaction settled in USDT or USDC extends the dollar’s reach into corners of the global economy that traditional banking cannot or will not serve.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!jy0r!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2ea941f1-c1d6-4cce-ad45-3409649bd020_1405x789.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!jy0r!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2ea941f1-c1d6-4cce-ad45-3409649bd020_1405x789.png)

Let me preempt the obvious objection: $306 billion against $36 trillion in national debt is, today, a rounding error. Stablecoins cannot replace the Fed or foreign central banks as primary buyers of Treasuries. That is not the argument. The argument is trajectory. Standard Chartered projects the stablecoin market reaching $2 trillion by end-2028. Citi’s base case is $1.9 trillion by 2030, with a $4 trillion bull case. At $2 trillion with a legal mandate to hold 100% reserves in short-duration Treasuries, stablecoins would absorb a meaningful share of net new T-bill issuance at precisely the moment the Fed is stepping back as a marginal buyer.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!qvPs!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa452a75f-ffa6-4f68-b282-22dd110df5fc_1405x794.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!qvPs!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa452a75f-ffa6-4f68-b282-22dd110df5fc_1405x794.png)

The mechanism is not replacement. It is a structurally growing, legally mandated bid for the short end of the curve that did not exist five years ago.

This is not crypto enthusiasm. I have no interest in whether Bitcoin goes to $200,000 or $20,000. This is about the architecture of dollar hegemony in a world where the traditional channels are fraying.

And it is, whether by design or by accident, entirely consistent with Reverse Perestroika: extend the dollar’s reach while restructuring the economy behind it.

## **Panama: Securing the Chokepoints**

On January 29, 2026, Panama’s Supreme Court ruled that concession contracts held by Hong Kong-based CK Hutchison Holdings to operate ports at both ends of the Panama Canal were unconstitutional. Maersk/APM Terminals (Danish) was announced as temporary administrator the following day. CK Hutchison commenced international arbitration. China’s Hong Kong and Macau Affairs Office called the ruling “absurd” and “shameful,” warning Panama would pay heavy prices.

The backstory matters. Trump’s inaugural address had specifically threatened to “reclaim” the canal. Panama launched an audit of CK Hutchison’s operations in early 2025, finding $1.2 billion in revenue losses since 1997. Panama also exited China’s Belt and Road Initiative in 2025. CK Hutchison had tried to sell its port interests (as part of a $22.8 billion global deal with BlackRock/MSC) in March 2025, but the deal stalled after Chinese government objections.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!oxNj!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3e0602fe-5187-4e01-9a3b-e4f9e232f39f_1402x552.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!oxNj!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3e0602fe-5187-4e01-9a3b-e4f9e232f39f_1402x552.png)

The ports affected, Balboa on the Pacific side and Cristobal on the Atlantic side, are the two strategic chokepoints flanking the canal. Control of these ports by a Hong Kong-based entity was, from a U.S. strategic perspective, exactly the kind of vulnerability that Reverse Perestroika is designed to eliminate.

I want to resist the temptation to draw a straight causal line from Trump’s inaugural address to the Panamanian court ruling. The evidentiary chain is circumstantial: the timing is suggestive, the U.S. had obvious motive, and Panama’s exit from the Belt and Road Initiative in 2025 suggests a broader diplomatic realignment. But I have no direct evidence of American pressure on the court, and asserting it without evidence would be the kind of lazy analysis I spend most of this newsletter criticising.

## **Central Banks Are Telling You Something**

If the policy framework is Reverse Perestroika, the market signal is gold.

Central banks purchased 3,220 tonnes of gold between 2022 and 2024, more than double the 1,576 tonnes purchased during the prior three-year period. The historical average from 2010 to 2021 was 473 tonnes per year. The 2022 to 2024 period averaged over 1,070.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!x-y0!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4b5d2fb5-227e-4b04-b6e2-079e608eb338_1402x785.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!x-y0!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4b5d2fb5-227e-4b04-b6e2-079e608eb338_1402x785.png)

2025 came in at 863 tonnes, below the thousand-tonne mark but still 82% above the historical average. And here is the detail that should concern you: roughly 57% of 2025 purchases were not publicly disclosed. Opaque buying. Central banks accumulating gold and choosing not to tell anyone about it.

The top buyers tell their own story. Poland led in both 2024 and 2025 (90 and 102 tonnes respectively), now holding 550 tonnes and targeting 700. Poland, the NATO front-line state bordering Russia, Kaliningrad, and Belarus, is building a gold reserve that would be extraordinary for a country of its size. Kazakhstan bought a record 57 tonnes in 2025. Brazil re-entered the market after a four-year absence. India and China continue to accumulate.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!w-0E!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0de5d9fe-3a39-41eb-82ee-10235262402f_1402x788.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!w-0E!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0de5d9fe-3a39-41eb-82ee-10235262402f_1402x788.png)

A World Gold Council survey found that 95% of central banks expect global gold reserves to increase over the next twelve months, the highest reading in the survey’s eight-year history. 43% plan to increase their own holdings, up from 29% in 2024.

These are not speculators. These are the institutions that manage national monetary reserves. And they are telling you, with their actual purchasing behaviour, that the monetary architecture of the post-Bretton Woods era is shifting. Gold is not going up because retail investors are excited. Gold is going up because the people who run the world’s monetary systems are hedging against the very restructuring that Reverse Perestroika implies.

If you think this is all just talk, look at where foreign capital is actually flowing.

BEA data shows manufacturing’s share of new foreign direct investment into the United States (FIDUS) jumped from 28.8% in 2023 to 44.9% in 2024. The absolute numbers: $67.7 billion into manufacturing in 2024, up from $42.9 billion the prior year.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!uNU8!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F84edd9f8-0cf7-44f5-99b8-19354e70b894_1403x790.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!uNU8!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F84edd9f8-0cf7-44f5-99b8-19354e70b894_1403x790.png)

The longer-term trend is equally telling. In 2022, 66% of capacity-expanding greenfield FDI went to manufacturing, roughly double the historical average. Computer and electronics investment surged from $15 million in 2021 to $1.8 billion in 2022, a 120-fold increase driven by CHIPS Act momentum. In the first half of 2025, manufacturing led all FDI flows at $71 billion.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!3UDd!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8d59e55e-d003-4581-b889-090b08d0db4a_1405x785.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!3UDd!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8d59e55e-d003-4581-b889-090b08d0db4a_1405x785.png)

Foreign capital is not stupid. It does not flow into U.S. manufacturing because of sentiment or patriotism. It flows because the combination of tariffs (which make importing less attractive), subsidies (IRA, CHIPS Act), and geopolitical risk (which makes non-U.S. manufacturing less secure) has fundamentally altered the investment calculus. The reshoring incentive structure is working, at least by this measure.

## **The Tariff Wall: Highest Since 1946**

The mechanism through which much of this restructuring is being forced is tariffs. And the scale is remarkable.

The average weighted applied tariff rate has gone from roughly 2.3% pre-Trump to 14.0%, the highest since 1934. The effective tariff rate is approximately 10.1%. Customs duties collected in 2025 totalled $264 billion, a 192% increase from 2024.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!U4tj!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb6d56ad0-5f30-4aa6-a26d-12d5ceb8124c_1405x785.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!U4tj!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb6d56ad0-5f30-4aa6-a26d-12d5ceb8124c_1405x785.png)

The current rate structure is complex and evolving. China faces an effective rate of 37 to 51% depending on the product category, with stacked Section 301 tariffs driving headline rates considerably higher on targeted goods. Canada faces 35% on non-USMCA goods (with USMCA-compliant imports, roughly 87% of the total, exempt). The EU is at 15% (negotiated down from a proposed 30 to 50%). Steel and aluminium face 50% globally, with the UK at a preferential 25%. Autos face 25%, with 15% for the EU, Japan, and South Korea.

January 2026 alone brought tariffs on countries buying Iranian oil (25%), a Greenland-related tariff on eight European countries (announced then cancelled within four days after a framework deal), a reduction in Taiwan tariffs from 20% to 15%, and a threatened 100% tariff on Canada if it proceeds with a China trade deal.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!lkOC!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ffceb9e3d-2bc2-435b-8dba-cb3078eba3ee_1405x785.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!lkOC!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ffceb9e3d-2bc2-435b-8dba-cb3078eba3ee_1405x785.png)

The legal status remains unresolved. Courts ruled IEEPA-based tariffs illegal in May and August 2025. The tariffs remain in effect pending Supreme Court review; oral arguments were heard November 5, 2025, with a ruling expected imminently.

I will not pretend to know whether this tariff regime is optimal, sustainable, or wise. What I will tell you is that it is historically unprecedented in the modern era, it is consistent with the Reverse Perestroika framework (trade economic efficiency for industrial resilience), and the market is not pricing the possibility that it persists and deepens.

If Reverse Perestroika fails or is abandoned, if the administration pivots back to conventional globalization, gold’s bid weakens. Central bank buying could decelerate. The miners, levered to the gold price, would get crushed.

I do not think this is the base case. But I would be lying if I told you I could assign it a zero probability.

**Theme 2: Defence and Munitions**

The production gap documented above is not closing. It is barely narrowing. And the political consensus behind increased defence spending is one of the few genuinely bipartisan realities in Washington.

The investment opportunity here is not in the Lockheed Martins and Raytheons of the world (though they will benefit). It is in the supply chain beneath them. The specialty chemical producers, the propellant manufacturers, the precision machining companies, the rare earth processors, the small and mid-cap companies that make the components without which a 155mm shell is just an expensive paperweight.

This is a multi-year, multi-hundred-billion-dollar capital expenditure cycle. The U.S. needs to go from 40,000 shells per month to something approaching 200,000 or more if it wants to be credible against a peer adversary. That requires new facilities, new supply chains, new workforce training, and new capacity across dozens of sub-industries.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!X5KH!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdc46388a-e2d6-46a5-a551-abac0d34f18b_1403x728.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!X5KH!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdc46388a-e2d6-46a5-a551-abac0d34f18b_1403x728.png)

The risk profile here is different from gold. Defence spending is sticky (governments rarely cut it once they start) but the individual companies are operationally complex, often single-customer dependent, and subject to procurement cycles that can be brutal. Position sizing matters. Diversification across the supply chain matters more.

I will name one area specifically, because it illustrates the kind of opportunity I mean. Solid rocket motor production in the United States is dominated by two companies: Aerojet Rocketdyne (now part of L3Harris) and Northrop Grumman. Below them sits a supply chain of specialty chemical producers, composite case manufacturers, and propellant blenders that are, in many cases, single points of failure for multiple weapons programmes. Several of these companies are publicly traded, subscale, and priced as though peacetime procurement tempos will persist indefinitely. They will not.

The DoD’s own industrial base assessments have identified these bottlenecks explicitly. When the prime contractors receive the multibillion-dollar production contracts that are coming, the capital will cascade down the supply chain. The question is whether the sub-tier suppliers will be acquired at premiums or re-rate independently. Either way, the current valuations are wrong. I will be publishing a detailed supply chain map in the coming weeks.

## **The Big Question**

I have presented you with a framework and the data that supports it. I have shown you where the investment opportunities sit. Now let me be honest about what I do not know.

I do not know if Reverse Perestroika will work.

The historical precedents are not encouraging. Economic restructuring on this scale has, more often than not, produced disruption, dislocation, and unintended consequences that dwarfed the original problem. The Smoot-Hawley comparison is tired but not entirely wrong. The idea that you can raise tariffs to 14%, restructure global supply chains, pick fights with allies and adversaries simultaneously, and emerge with a stronger industrial base and a more resilient economy, that is an extraordinarily ambitious bet.

On the other hand, the status quo is also unsustainable. An economy where 80% of bank credit inflates assets while the country cannot produce enough ammunition to fight for a week is not a stable equilibrium. It is a system waiting for a shock. The question is not whether restructuring happens, but whether it happens deliberately (however messily) or is forced upon us by crisis.

That is the Reverse Perestroika trade in a nutshell: positioning for a world where the restructuring is real, persistent, and investable, while acknowledging that the path from here to there will be anything but smooth.

I know which side I am leaning toward. The data, I think, speaks for itself.
