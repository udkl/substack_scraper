In the golden age of Periclean Athens, amidst the flourishing of democracy and philosophy, lived a merchant named Demetrios. He was not a man of idle speculation; he was a master of logistics, a student of the science of economics.

For years, he watched the city’s lifeblood, the grain shipments from the fertile colonies of the Black Sea, fluctuate with the seasons. He saw inefficiency, risk, and opportunity.

Demetrios dedicated himself to a singular, grand calculation.

He charted the harvest cycles in the Cimmerian Bosporus, modeled the insatiable demand of Athens’ large populace, and quantified the variables of spoilage, piracy, and the fickle Mediterranean weather.

His conclusion, arrived at through meticulous logic, was revolutionary.

A massive, purpose-built fleet of advanced grain ships, larger and faster than any before, could streamline the entire process.

By achieving an unprecedented economy of scale, he could undercut all rivals, guarantee Athens a stable food supply, and in the process, amass a fortune that would rival the city’s own treasury. His models were perfect, his logic unassailable.

He staked his family’s wealth and convinced the city’s most astute financiers to back his venture. The shipyards of Piraeus buzzed with activity, constructing what came to be known as Demetrios’s Folly, or his Armada, depending on one’s optimism. The ships were marvels of engineering, designed according to the purest principles of economic rationality.

Yet, for all his brilliance, Demetrios’s calculations contained a fatal flaw.

They were built entirely on the assumption of a rational world governed by supply, demand, and profit.

He had accounted for every variable except the one that mattered most: The irrational, non-linear force of a great power’s desperation.

As his fleet prepared for its maiden voyage, the Peloponnesian War raged on. Sparta, cornered and on the verge of collapse, made a move that no rational economic actor would ever predict.

They made a pact with the devil.

In exchange for a vast infusion of Persian gold, the very gold of their sworn, ancestral enemy, the Spartans ceded control of the Greek cities of Ionia and key islands in the Aegean. It was a strategically vital act of survival that was, on its face, economically and culturally insane.

Overnight, the geopolitical map was redrawn.

The sea lanes that Demetrios’s models had marked as safe and profitable were now patrolled by a Persian-funded Spartan navy. New alliances shifted the currents of trade. The very foundation of his perfect calculation, the predictable order of the world, crumbled. His magnificent fleet, a testament to economic logic, became a collection of stranded assets, too specialized to be repurposed, too expensive to maintain.

Demetrios was ruined not by a flaw in his math, but by the gravitational force of a rival state’s will to survive, a force that bends all market logic to its own ends.

### **When Desperation Bends Market Logic**

Demetrios’s story, though fictional, reveals an important lesson, one with no expiration date.

It is this: standard market models, built on assumptions of rational economic behavior and commercial profitability, are inherently fragile.

They are fatally vulnerable to the non-linear, often irrational, forces of geopolitical desperation.

When a major state actor believes its survival is at stake, it will subordinate commercial logic to strategic necessity. It will make deals that are value-destructive on paper to secure a political or strategic victory, creating price distortions and systemic risks that conventional forecasting, with its elegant curves and logical progressions, cannot possibly capture.

This idea, that the most potent forces often lie in the negative space, in the avoidance of ruin rather than the pursuit of profit, echoes through disciplines. It is the core of Socratic wisdom.

The Oracle declared Socrates the wisest man in Athens not because of what he knew, but because he alone was acutely aware of what he didn’t know. He understood the profound danger of assuming his knowledge was complete.

Similarly, market strategists who focus only on their quantifiable economic models, ignoring the vast, unquantifiable realm of geopolitical desperation, are falling into the same trap as the overconfident Athenian poets and politicians. They believe they have the answer to everything, but their ignorance of the unknown is the very source of their greatest risk.

The investor Charlie Munger championed the same concept with his principle of “inversion.”

“Invert, always invert.” — Charlie Munger

To solve a problem, one should first invert it. Instead of asking how to achieve success, one should ask how to guarantee failure, and then avoid those paths.

For a multi-billion dollar energy project, the question is not, “How will this project be profitable?” The inverted, and far more useful, question is, “What could cause this project to catastrophically fail?”

The answer is rarely a minor miscalculation in demand forecasts. It is far more likely to be a geopolitical event, like a desperate state making a “sweetheart” deal that fundamentally rewrites the rules of the market.

We see this principle in action today.

The United States’ CHIPS Act, which subsidizes the construction of semiconductor fabrication plants in Arizona, is not an act of pure economic rationality. It is cheaper and more efficient to build these plants in Taiwan.

The policy is a strategic imperative, driven by the geopolitical fear of being dependent on an island that exists under a constant threat of invasion. Washington is deliberately choosing a less profitable path to avoid a potentially catastrophic failure in its supply chain.

This is a modern state bending market logic to the will of national security. And it is this same dynamic that is now poised to reshape the global natural gas market.

### **U.S. Calculations vs. Russian Realities**

For the past decade, the United States has been assembling its own version of Demetrios’s armada. It is a fleet not of grain ships, but of massive Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) export terminals, a tangible expression of a grand strategic and economic calculation.

This calculation, however, is now on a collision course with the desperate, market-bending actions of a cornered Russian state. And a critical policy shift in Washington has just thrown gasoline on the fire.

#### **The Athenian Fleet – America’s LNG Armada, Unleashed**

The scale of the American LNG expansion is staggering.

The United States is already the world’s largest LNG exporter, and the next wave of investment is set to cement that dominance. The International Energy Agency (IEA) projects that projects already under construction will add more than 300 billion cubic meters (bcm) of annual LNG capacity to the global system by 2030.

As the chart below illustrates, a colossal wave of that new supply is coming from the U.S.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Xfwp!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2d05ad99-acb1-4f59-9eb4-a98f96110907_1905x1064.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Xfwp!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2d05ad99-acb1-4f59-9eb4-a98f96110907_1905x1064.png)

This buildout is the result of a seemingly flawless economic calculation. It is underpinned by the vast, cheap reserves of shale gas, priced against the domestic Henry Hub benchmark. This gives U.S. LNG a powerful competitive advantage.

The strategic logic became even clearer after 2022, when Europe, in its urgent pivot away from Russian pipeline gas, created a massive new source of demand.

To mitigate the immense financial risk of constructing these multi-billion-dollar liquefaction facilities, developers have relied on a specific business model: they only begin construction after securing long-term, 20-year Sale and Purchase Agreements (SPAs) for the majority of their capacity.

This is the modern equivalent of Demetrios pre-selling his grain shipments; a rational, logical approach to de-risking a massive capital investment.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!eF9_!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8961fcb3-8097-42b3-b711-8a639801176f_1903x1053.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!eF9_!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8961fcb3-8097-42b3-b711-8a639801176f_1903x1053.png)

On paper, the model is sound, the logic is perfect, and the profits seem assured.

Then, in January 2025, the equation changed dramatically.

The new Trump administration, in a day-one executive order to “unleash American Energy Dominance,” lifted the Biden-era pause on new LNG export permits. That decision, which had cast a cloud of uncertainty over a “second wave” of U.S. projects, was reversed overnight.

The move effectively signaled to the world that the American gas armada was not only coming but was now fully unleashed, with regulatory floodgates opened for decades of supply.

#### **Bargain – Russia’s Desperate Pivot to the East**

While America has been executing its calculated expansion, Russia has been grappling with a strategic catastrophe.

The full-scale invasion of Ukraine led to the near-total loss of its most lucrative energy market Europe. Annual pipeline gas exports to the continent, which once peaked at around 175 bcm, are projected to fall below 17 bcm in 2025, a level not seen since the early 1970s.

The financial strain is immense; while Gazprom reported a net profit in 2024, it was a fiscal illusion propped up by its oil subsidiary and accounting maneuvers. The core gas business recorded a substantial loss.

Unlike oil, which can be rerouted by sea, Russia’s vast West Siberian gas fields are connected by a web of pipelines pointing west. To monetize that gas, Russia must build new infrastructure eastward.

This existential predicament has made the proposed Power of Siberia 2 (PoS2) pipeline not just another commercial project, but a geopolitical lifeline. It is the centerpiece of Moscow’s “pivot to Asia,” a desperate attempt to prove it can replace its lost European customers and secure long-term revenue streams.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ildj!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8e09c45f-d4b2-46b8-8974-8f226bf5e75d_1600x897.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ildj!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8e09c45f-d4b2-46b8-8974-8f226bf5e75d_1600x897.png)

This is not a project driven by profit maximization; it is driven by national survival.

The desperation creates a profound asymmetry in negotiations with Beijing. Russia *needs* this deal.

China, with its diverse energy sources, merely *wants* the deal, but only on its own terms. This power imbalance is evident in the diplomatic language surrounding the project. In September 2025, Russian officials triumphantly announced a “legally binding memorandum”.

Yet, Chinese statements have been far more circumspect, often silent, and it is clear that critical commercial details like price, financing, volume, and timeline remain entirely unresolved despite years of talks.

The market’s judgment is clear: PoS2 is not a business deal. It is the price of geopolitical alignment, a Spartan bargain where strategic necessity trumps commercial sense.

### **China’s Unmatched Leverage**

Sitting across the negotiating table, China is in a position of unparalleled strength and is using it to extract a “Persian price” for Russia’s gas. Beijing is well aware of Moscow’s desperation and has a clear set of demands that would make PoS2 gas astonishingly cheap.

Reports suggest China is pushing for a pricing formula linked not to international benchmarks, but to Russia’s own heavily subsidized domestic gas prices.

This could result in a price as low as $120 per 1,000 cubic meters, or roughly $3.50 per million British thermal units (/MMBtu). To put this in perspective, spot LNG prices in Asia have recently hovered around $11-$12/MMBtu.

Furthermore, China is demanding unprecedented flexibility, seeking to lower the “take-or-pay” commitment from the industry standard of over 80% to as low as 50%. This would mean China commits to buying only half the pipeline’s volume, leaving Gazprom to bear the financial risk for the massive, underutilized infrastructure.

If Russia agrees, it would effectively be subsidizing China’s energy security. It is a deal that makes little commercial sense for Gazprom but fulfills the Kremlin’s strategic imperative.

This dynamic reveals a more subtle, but equally powerful, aspect of the PoS2 project. The pipeline is a strategic weapon for China even if it’s never built.

The mere credible *possibility* of this massive volume of ultra-cheap pipeline gas gives Chinese negotiators immense leverage in *all* of their other energy negotiations.

When a Chinese utility negotiates a new 20-year LNG contract with a U.S. exporter, it can now point to the PoS2 agreement as its alternative. The message is clear: “We have access to a non-market, geopolitically priced alternative. Your market-based price must compete with that.”

### **Storm Signal: Glut Meets a Black Swan**

The stage is now set for a dramatic collision.

The massive, predictable wave of new U.S. LNG supply, now supercharged by Washington’s policy reversal is sailing directly into a global market that is about to be fundamentally warped by two new realities: China’s slowing demand and the non-market force of the Russia-China gas deal.

The numbers are significant.

The 50 bcm of annual gas flow from PoS2 is a colossal volume. But since the initial agreement was announced, new data has emerged that strengthens China’s hand even further.

According to the IEA, China’s natural gas demand actually *declined* by 1% in the first half of 2025, with its LNG imports plummeting by over 20%.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!k6Sc!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd405df01-2e8d-4781-a90f-f887a7707a17_1901x1064.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!k6Sc!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd405df01-2e8d-4781-a90f-f887a7707a17_1901x1064.png)

This is not just a cyclical downturn. It’s a structural shift driven by China’s astonishing renewables revolution.

In the first half of 2025, for the first time ever, China’s wind and solar farms generated more electricity than its coal plants. The falling cost of this domestic, clean energy is “severely hindering” the economic case for gas. Russia isn’t just competing with American LNG; it’s competing with Chinese-made solar panels, a battle it is destined to lose.

When the world’s most important energy importer suddenly has less need for gas and gains access to a massive new source at a politically dictated, below-market price, the ripple effects will be global. This injection of cheap gas will deepen and prolong the anticipated LNG supply glut, suppressing global spot prices for everyone, for longer.

It is crucial to understand who bears the ultimate risk of this collision.

In the short term, the U.S. LNG developers themselves are largely insulated. Their business model, based on long-term, fixed-price contracts, protects them.

But the risk does not disappear; it is simply transferred to their customers.

The entities most exposed are the major portfolio players (energy giants like Shell and TotalEnergies) and commodity traders like Vitol and Trafigura. These firms have signed the binding 20-year contracts to buy the U.S. LNG.

They will be contractually obligated to take delivery of this gas, priced at a premium to Henry Hub, while the global spot market is potentially flooded with cheaper alternatives, squeezed by the price-depressing effect of PoS2.

The longer-term consequences for the U.S. are even more severe. Beyond the wave of projects already under construction, there is another 250 bcm of potential U.S. LNG projects on the drawing board.

The lifting of the permit pause means they *can* be built, but in a world where China is absorbing ever-more Russian gas at sweetheart prices, and global spot prices are chronically depressed, these projects will face a catastrophic challenge. Finding buyers willing to commit to 20-year contracts at prices that can justify the multi-billion-dollar investment will become nearly impossible.

America’s ambition to be the undisputed energy supplier for the 21st century could see its grand armada stranded at the docks before it is even fully built.

The critical question for investors and strategists is no longer just, “What do the supply and demand curves say?” It must now be, “What price is Vladimir Putin willing to pay for his regime’s survival, and how will Xi Jinping leverage that desperation to China’s advantage?”

In the answer to that question lies the true risk to America’s energy future.
