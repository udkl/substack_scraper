# **Operation Epic Fury: The Oil Market Just Broke at 2:30 AM**

I need to start with a confession.

In my last oil piece, “The Manufactured Surplus,” I laid out a framework: $60-72 Brent for 2026, with skew to the upside. The IEA’s 3.7 million barrel per day surplus was built on phantom barrels, misclassified Chinese strategic reserves, shipping route distortions, and a demand model that has systematically underestimated global consumption, with upward revisions in nearly every year of the past two decades. The real surplus, once you stripped the accounting fictions, was a quarter of the headline figure. Maybe less.

I assigned probabilities to Iran scenarios: 55% managed crackdown, 25% Artesh pivot, 20% fragmentation. I flagged Rapidan’s McNally at 75% probability of a US strike. I noted the risk premium was already adding $4-5 to Brent. I said the ceiling of the range was where the action would be.

That framework was correct in every particular except one: I underestimated how fast the ceiling would break.

At approximately 1:15 AM Eastern on February 28, 2026, US and Israeli forces launched coordinated strikes against Iran. The Pentagon designated the campaign "Operation Epic Fury." At approximately 2:30 AM Eastern, Donald Trump released a prerecorded eight-minute video on Truth Social declaring "major combat operations" in Iran. Israel’s parallel effort was codenamed “Operation Roaring Lion.” Dozens of sorties from carrier-based aircraft and regional installations. Seven missiles in the vicinity of Khamenei’s compound in Tehran.

Multiple sources, including Israeli military officials cited by Reuters and regional intelligence contacts, initially indicated that Supreme Leader Ayatollah Khamenei was killed in the strikes on his Tehran compound. Iranian state media (IRNA, IRIB, Fars News Agency) confirmed Khamenei's death early on March 1, declaring 40 days of national mourning. He was 86. His daughter, son-in-law, and grandchild were also killed in the strike.

Israeli military and regional sources, corroborated by US officials via CBS News, confirmed that defence minister Aziz Nasirzadeh and IRGC commander Mohammad Pakpour were killed. Additional confirmed casualties include Ali Shamkhani, chief of staff Mohammad Bagheri, and army commander Abdolrahim Mousavi.

Brent closed at $72.87 on Friday, pressing the exact ceiling of my range before a single bomb fell. It is widely expected to gap above $80 at Monday’s open.

The manufactured surplus thesis didn’t die. But the geopolitical overlay just swallowed it whole.

This piece is the update.

## **What Happened, and Why It Matters for Every Barrel on the Planet**

Let me be precise about the targeting, because this is where the oil thesis lives or dies.

The strikes hit military and nuclear targets across Iran: Tehran (IRGC headquarters area, the National Security Council, the presidential palace vicinity, Khamenei's compound), Isfahan, Kermanshah, Qom, Tabriz, Karaj, Ilam, and Lorestan province.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth described the operation as "the most lethal, most complex, and most-precision aerial operation in history." Communication services across Tehran were severely disrupted, with internet connectivity dropping to approximately 4% according to monitoring service Netblocks.

Iran's Foreign Minister Araghchi initially confirmed two military commanders died and stated Khamenei was alive "as far as I know." Within hours, Iranian state media confirmed the Supreme Leader had been killed. A three-member Provisional Leadership Council has formed under Article 111 of Iran's constitution: President Pezeshkian, Supreme Court Chief Justice Mohseni-Ejei, and newly appointed Guardian Council cleric Ayatollah Arafi.

But here is what matters for your portfolio.

**Kharg Island, which handles 90% of Iran’s crude exports, has reportedly been shut down.** Iran’s semi-official Mehr news agency confirmed an explosion on the island Saturday, though it provided no detail on the oil terminal itself. Geopolitical Futures assessed the facility as non-operational.

Bloomberg’s reporting as of Saturday provided no initial confirmation that the strikes specifically targeted energy assets. Robin Mills of Qamar Energy noted unconfirmed reports of explosions at Kharg and Dezful. The extent of damage to loading berths, storage, and the subsea pipelines feeding the terminal from the onshore Ghurreh booster station and Ganaveh manifold (identified by CSIS as critical choke points) remains unknown pending satellite imagery.

I am treating Kharg as likely non-operational based on the balance of evidence, but readers should understand this is the single most important variable in the entire analysis and the one most likely to be revised. If the loading infrastructure is intact, the disruption is measured in days. If the subsea pipelines or booster stations were struck, it could extend for months. If the non-operational assessment holds, Iranian crude exports have collapsed from approximately 1.7 million barrels per day to just over 100,000 b/d, the largest supply disruption since the Libyan civil war.

It is important to distinguish the confirmed February 28 targeting from the June 2025 "Twelve-Day War" between Israel and Iran. During that earlier conflict, Israel struck one unit of Phase 14 of the South Pars gas field (which produces a significant share of Iran's natural gas and is shared with Qatar), Iran partially suspended gas production, and Tehran fuel depots and refineries were hit.

Those strikes established a precedent for energy infrastructure targeting but are not confirmed to have been repeated on February 28. The February 28 operation's confirmed target set was a decapitation and military degradation campaign, not an energy infrastructure campaign. Whether energy infrastructure was deliberately struck, or only suffered collateral damage (as at Kharg Island), remains unclear pending satellite imagery. Power infrastructure may have taken collateral damage across the industrial base.

Iran Open Data Center analysis underscores why the geography matters: Khuzestan alone produces 70% of Iran’s crude, with major refineries at Abadan and Bandar Abbas, and the Lavan export terminal forming a narrow, highly exposed industrial belt adjacent to Kharg Island and South Pars. The result is a brittle network where one sustained air campaign could disable extraction, refining, and export operations in a single stroke.

## **The Strait of Hormuz: Where $80 Becomes $100**

The Strait of Hormuz remains in legal limbo. Iran's IRGC broadcast VHF closure warnings to all merchant shipping, confirmed by EU naval mission Aspides and UKMTO, though UKMTO noted these "do not constitute a legally recognised suspension of transit passage."

On March 1, Iranian General Mohsen Rezai announced the strait is "open to tankers until further notice" while warning US warships are "legitimate targets." In practice, commercial shipping has largely halted, with at least 150 tankers at anchor beyond the strait.

Tankers are piling up at both entrances. Some oil majors and top trading houses have suspended crude and fuel shipments through the strait. Greek authorities advised all Greek-flagged vessels to avoid the Persian Gulf, the Gulf of Oman, and the Strait of Hormuz entirely.

The escalation ladder at Hormuz had been climbing all month. On February 3, two IRGC gunboats attempted to seize a US-flagged tanker; a Marine F-35C from USS Abraham Lincoln shot down an approaching Iranian drone. On February 5, Iran seized two foreign tankers near Farsi Island. On February 16, the IRGC conducted “Smart Control of Hormuz Strait” live-fire drills, temporarily closing sections of the waterway for the first time. Now, post-strikes, tankers are actively self-deterring from transit.

And here is the compounding variable that almost nobody is pricing: the Houthis have announced they will resume Red Sea attacks, including strikes on Israel and on shipping routes. This creates an unprecedented dual-chokepoint scenario. Hormuz handles roughly 20% of global oil trade. The Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb handles another significant chunk of global shipping. If both become contested simultaneously, the logistical rerouting costs and insurance premiums alone could add $5-10 per barrel before you even discuss physical supply losses.

CSIS’s February 19 disruption scenario analysis is the most detailed public framework for modelling this. Their Scenario 2 (Hormuz harassment) projects that several million barrels per day could be disrupted for a period of weeks until allied naval forces neutralise sea- and shore-based threats, with crude prices climbing past $90 and US retail gasoline exceeding $3 per gallon nationally.

Their assessment that this scenario is reversible (Tehran could call off disruption, or allied forces could suppress it) is correct, but the duration uncertainty is the variable that separates a spike from a regime change in price.

McNally’s January assessment reinforces this: the disruption risk at Hormuz is measured in weeks, not hours or days. The Iranians have better weaponry and a better coastline to harass the strait than the Houthis, and the US failed to fully pacify the Red Sea over a sustained campaign.

VLCC rates from the Middle East to China have surged past $200,000 per day on the MEG-China benchmark route, with LSEG data showing the TD3 benchmark at $206,141 per day and individual fixtures such as DHT Jaguar reaching $208,000 per day as of the week ending February 25. This represents a tripling since the start of 2026 and the highest level since the 2020 oil price war. Splash247 reports that owners “are ever more convinced that this time the supercycle is real,” with 12-month time charter rates reaching approximately $93,000-$105,000 per day and February averaging as the best on record for VLCCs.

Clarksons made the critical observation that VLCC rates do not need barrels to disappear to move; they reprice on perceived risk through war-risk premiums, owners demanding compensation to call the region, and charterers accelerating bookings to reduce schedule uncertainty.

A structural change is compounding the rate surge that almost nobody outside shipping circles is discussing. South Korea’s Sinokor has been on a massive VLCC acquisition spree, now controlling roughly 78 vessels in the active spot market (projected to reach 88+ this quarter), representing approximately 24% of the spot-trading fleet. Signal Group, a shipping analytics firm, calls this “a fundamental change in the competitive dynamics of the VLCC spot segment,” an unprecedented level of concentration for a single commercial operator. Over 15% of the global VLCC fleet has also migrated into the shadow fleet for sanctioned trades, further reducing mainstream availability. This fleet tightening was already driving rates higher; the geopolitical overlay is now multiplying a structural supply squeeze in available tonnage.

This is a reversal from late 2025, when backwardation had been at its shallowest since January 2024 and the entire WTI 2026 curve was below $60. The Iran-driven rally re-steepened the backwardation, particularly at the front of the curve. A backwardated curve in the face of the most bearish fundamental outlook in years (IEA’s 3.7 mb/d surplus) is the market telling you something the models are not.

There is a critical nuance in the inventory data. The IEA's February 2026 Oil Market Report noted that OECD industry stocks surpassed their five-year average for the first time since 2021, following a counter-seasonal 3.9 mb December build. However, implied builds have been concentrated in non-OECD and floating storage rather than at pricing hubs. This distinction matters: the barrels may exist on spreadsheets without existing at Cushing.

## **Revising the Iran Probability Matrix**

My previous 55/25/20 split (managed crackdown, Artesh pivot, fragmentation) was built for a world where the regime was under domestic pressure but externally intact. That world no longer exists. The revised probabilities below reflect a Bayesian update: the prior was the pre-strike assessment, and the update is conditioned on confirmed infrastructure destruction, reported leadership decapitation, and the IRGC taking direct hits for the first time since 1988. Reasonable analysts could place these numbers 10-15 percentage points differently and still be within the range of defensible judgment.

**Managed crackdown: down to 15-25% (central estimate 20%).** The regime's ability to "manage" anything has been degraded by infrastructure destruction, the confirmed killing of the Supreme Leader and multiple senior military commanders, and the IRGC taking direct fire. This scenario requires the Provisional Leadership Council to establish effective authority rapidly, which is currently being tested for the first time since 1988.

**Artesh pivot / institutional transition: up to 30-40% (central estimate 35%).** Bokhari’s analysis of IRGC-Artesh power-sharing dynamics (published February 13, remarkably prescient) is now the most relevant analytical frame. If the IRGC is weakened as an institution, the regular armed forces may emerge as the entity capable of negotiating a ceasefire, a political transition, or both. This is not a pro-Western outcome. It is a pragmatic military outcome, and those are historically the most durable.

**Fragmentation / prolonged instability: up to 40-50% (central estimate 45%).** The reference class here is instructive: of major-power military strikes against authoritarian states with institutional security services since 1990, the fragmentation rate is high. Iraq 2003 and Libya 2011 both produced prolonged institutional collapse despite (or because of) external military intervention. Serbia 1999 and Syria 2018 are counterexamples where the regime survived, but in both cases the strikes were far more limited in scope than what Iran is absorbing. Bokhari’s “iron triangle” framework explains why even the optimistic version of this scenario is messy: the Russia-China-Iran alignment is structural, not opportunistic, and it guarantees that even regime change does not produce a clean, market-friendly outcome. Instead, you get protracted uncertainty with competing power centres, fragmented institutional authority, and an oil supply question mark that persists for quarters, not weeks.

Here is how the political scenarios map onto the oil price tiers. This cross-mapping is the critical analytical step that connects the two frameworks, and readers should internalise it before positioning.

Managed crackdown (25%) maps primarily to Tier 1. If the regime holds institutional coherence, it retains the capacity to authorise a ceasefire, reopen Kharg Island, and de-escalate at Hormuz. This is the cleanest pathway to a contained outcome. However, even a managed crackdown could produce Tier 2 pricing if infrastructure damage is more severe than initial reports suggest (provisional), creating a weeks-long restoration timeline despite political willingness to de-escalate.

The Artesh pivot (35%) is the most ambiguous, and this ambiguity matters enormously for positioning. A rapid, orderly military-led transition that prioritises economic normalisation could produce a Tier 1 outcome within weeks, potentially even faster than the managed crackdown if pragmatic military leaders move quickly to reopen export infrastructure. A messy, contested transition with factional infighting over who controls export terminals and naval posture lands squarely in Tier 2. The split is roughly 40/60 toward Tier 2 within this scenario, based on the historical base rate of post-conflict military transitions achieving rapid economic normalisation (low) versus protracted institutional negotiation (high).

Fragmentation (40%) maps to Tier 2 at minimum and is the primary feeder into Tier 3. Without clear institutional authority over Kharg Island, pipeline operations, and naval posture at Hormuz, the supply disruption extends by default rather than by design.

The key implication for sizing positions: the 30% I assign to Tier 1 (contained conflict) requires one of the first two political outcomes to resolve favourably and quickly. The probability of achieving both conditions simultaneously is lower than the standalone probability of either political outcome. The cross-mapping math confirms this: even with a combined 60% probability for managed crackdown and Artesh pivot, only about half of those pathways produce a rapid, clean market resolution.

## **The Supply Picture: Compounding Disruptions Nobody Is Pricing**

The Iran disruption does not exist in isolation. It compounds onto a supply picture that was already tighter than consensus acknowledged.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ohYe!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb71d2205-43be-4237-80c2-cceb8dc08d8e_1884x592.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ohYe!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb71d2205-43be-4237-80c2-cceb8dc08d8e_1884x592.png)

**Kazakhstan’s CPC terminal** is still not fully restored from the Ukrainian naval drone strike on November 29, 2025. Tengiz field production reached 790,000 bpd by February 24, still ramping from 660,000 bpd after a January power outage. Kazakhstan revised 2026 shipment estimates down to 68 million metric tons from 72 million. Full functionality delayed until Q2 at the earliest.

**Russia is bleeding.** Russian supply declined approximately 350,000 bpd in January (IEA). Treasury sanctioned Rosneft and Lukoil, together producing 5.3 mb/d and exporting 3.5 mb/d, representing roughly 50% of Russia’s total oil exports. Over 75% of Russian exports now fall under US sanctions. January oil and gas tax revenues hit 393 billion rubles, a severe year-on-year collapse (January 2025 oil/gas revenues were approximately 789 billion rubles per Moscow Times reporting). Indian Russian crude imports fell to approximately 1.2 mb/d in January (per Kpler and CREA data), the lowest since late 2022 and down from a 1.7 mb/d average. Reliance Industries received zero Russian seaborne oil in January.

**Venezuela.** Following Maduro’s capture on January 3, exports were paralysed. Fifteen-plus million barrels shifted to floating storage. January production cratered to roughly 780,000 bpd, down 210,000 bpd month on month.

**US production is plateauing.** Crude output at approximately 13.6 mb/d, with the EIA forecasting 13.5 mb/d average for 2026, the first decline after four years of increases. Baker Hughes oil rigs at approximately 407-409, with a roughly 20% decline in Permian rigs year on year. ConocoPhillips announced a $1 billion cost-cutting initiative on lower price expectations.

Add these together. Kazakhstan is minus several hundred thousand barrels. Russia is minus 350,000 bpd and falling. Venezuela is minus 210,000 bpd. Iran just went from 1.7 mb/d of exports to approximately 100,000 bpd (provisional, contingent on Kharg Island damage assessments). US shale is flat at best, declining at worst.

This is where the two frameworks converge.

The IEA’s headline surplus of 3.7 mb/d is the number the market prices against. I argued in “The Manufactured Surplus” that the real, physically deliverable surplus is roughly a quarter of that figure, perhaps 0.9 mb/d, once you strip phantom barrels from misclassified Chinese strategic reserves, shipping route distortions, and Goldman’s observation that approximately 600,000 bpd of Russian and Iranian crude is “stuck at sea” and never reaches pricing hubs. I stand by that assessment.

The distinction matters enormously for how you interpret the disruption math. If you accept the IEA’s 3.7 mb/d headline, the current disruptions (well over 2 mb/d across all theatres) erode the surplus but do not eliminate it. The market remains oversupplied, and the price spike is temporary. If you accept my estimate of approximately 0.9 mb/d of real, deliverable surplus, then the current disruptions do not merely erode the cushion; they push the physical market into outright deficit for Q2 2026 under any scenario where Iranian exports remain offline for more than two to three weeks.

This is the crux of the analytical disagreement between the bear case (temporary geopolitical premium over a fundamentally oversupplied market) and the bull case (geopolitical shock exposing a market that was never as oversupplied as the headline suggested). I sit on the bull side of this divide, but I want readers to understand exactly where the disagreement lives so they can calibrate their own positioning.

## **The Bear Case: Why I Could Be Wrong**

Here is the counterargument.

**China’s buffer is enormous.** 1.47 billion barrels in commercial and strategic storage, equivalent to roughly 130 days of imports. Beijing is adding 730,000 bpd to inventories in 2026, with 169 million barrels of new capacity under construction. Chinese refiners can weather a multi-month Iranian export disruption without panic-buying in the spot market. Amena Bakr at Kpler confirmed this assessment: China “has a healthy level of stocks so it would not face any short-term shortage of supplies.” This caps the upside.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!bIOi!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3fde9c39-093f-4848-b744-2b1844c181f3_1886x830.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!bIOi!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3fde9c39-093f-4848-b744-2b1844c181f3_1886x830.png)

**The floating Iranian barrels provide a bridge.** That approximately 200 million barrels on water gives Chinese refiners four months of supply continuity. If the conflict is short (Tier 1), much of this crude reaches its destination and the physical market never tightens as dramatically as the headlines suggest.

**OPEC+ spare capacity, if real, is significant.** If Saudi Arabia genuinely holds 3.1 mb/d of spare capacity (a number I am sceptical of, but let us take the IEA at face value), then OPEC+ can replace the lost Iranian barrels. OPEC+ had already frozen production increases for January through March 2026 at the November meeting, citing seasonally weak demand. The March 1 meeting is the first test. Rystad’s Jorge Leon, a former OPEC official, noted that OPEC’s own data show demand for OPEC+ crude declining in Q2, which could limit the scope for further increases, but the geopolitical context may override this consideration. Amro Zakaria at Kyoto Network offered the key conditional: “Should the strikes end quickly, I expect the prices to come back down as the market is oversupplied.”

The administration’s price-suppression toolkit, which I detail below, reinforces this ceiling.

**Trump does not want high oil prices.** This is Sankey’s argument, and it is structurally sound. Every dollar increase in oil is a political liability heading into midterms.

**The demand picture remains weak.** China’s NBS manufacturing PMI fell to 49.3 in January (contraction for nine of the past ten months). Property sales are down 60% from peak, starts down 80%. EVs displaced over 1.3 mb/d of oil demand in 2024, and by 2030 the IEA projects over 5 mb/d displaced. The IEA notes that petrochemical feedstocks will become the dominant source of oil demand growth from 2026 onwards, as transport fuels face structural decline. The structural demand headwinds have not changed because bombs fell on Tehran.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!RBwE!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa4382c59-bf8a-4d8f-badb-b9ecf06d8cf3_1886x918.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!RBwE!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa4382c59-bf8a-4d8f-badb-b9ecf06d8cf3_1886x918.png)

There is also a data quality point that Alhajji flagged and which deserves attention: China’s record 11.6 mb/d crude imports in 2025 were not what they seemed. Rystad Energy decomposed the figure and found that 430,000 bpd, fully 83% of the import increase, was strategic stockpiling rather than consumption. Beijing directed national oil companies to buy 140 million barrels for strategic reserves between July 2025 and March 2026. Strip out the stockpiling, and Chinese demand growth was marginal at best. FGE expects 2026 inventories to rise by 266 million barrels (730,000 bpd). The Chinese import data, like the IEA’s surplus headline, flatters the bull case when read carelessly and undermines it when decomposed properly.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!XmaR!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F10a2c46d-dba6-456c-9a71-cb6729d4ee16_1884x1056.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!XmaR!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F10a2c46d-dba6-456c-9a71-cb6729d4ee16_1884x1056.png)

Sankey maintains a $40 oil target if peace and new regimes allow sidelined supply to return. His logic: “Every 100 million barrels that comes back into the market is $4 off the oil price on a short-term basis.” He views the geopolitical premium as a temporary veil over a market that would otherwise be in a glut.

**The administration’s price-suppression toolkit is deeper than most analysts acknowledge.** The White House initiated a military operation that it knew would raise oil prices, which means it has almost certainly pre-planned mitigation measures. The available levers include: SPR releases. The reserve sits at 415.4 million barrels (58.2% of capacity).

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!RpiO!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc0d11491-7b5e-47e3-a136-624eccdb1a15_1884x1060.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!RpiO!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc0d11491-7b5e-47e3-a136-624eccdb1a15_1884x1060.png)

Energy Secretary Wright’s stated acquisition target of at or below $79 per barrel creates a natural ceiling: the administration can sell into spikes above $80, dampen the price, and repurchase when the conflict de-escalates. This is not unlimited (Congress constrains release volumes, and the reserve is already depleted from 2022 releases), but it provides a short-term buffer of 30-60 million barrels without legislative approval.

**OPEC+ pressure.** Trump has a demonstrated track record of direct engagement with Riyadh and Abu Dhabi on production levels. The March 1 OPEC+ meeting is the first test of whether the administration pre-negotiated a supply response. Reuters' reporting that delegates are discussing a 411,000 bpd April increase suggests coordination may already be underway. If the Saudis agree to a 500,000+ bpd increase (versus the originally planned 137,000 bpd), that would signal explicit US-Saudi cooperation on price management.

**Sanctions flexibility.** The administration could quietly ease enforcement on Russian crude (allowing Indian and Turkish buyers to resume purchases without secondary sanctions risk) or issue waivers for specific Iranian trading partners. This would be politically contradictory (striking Iran while easing oil sanctions), but the administration has demonstrated comfort with policy contradictions when the economic incentive is large enough.

**Expedited domestic permitting.** Executive orders on federal lands leasing, LNG export approvals, and pipeline permitting have limited near-term impact on supply (12-18 month lag at minimum), but the announcement effect can shift sentiment and dampen speculative positioning.

The key question is whether these tools can offset a 1.5+ mb/d supply loss.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!TLJI!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F66ad86c6-556a-426f-b4d0-c3986b9df669_1884x1054.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!TLJI!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F66ad86c6-556a-426f-b4d0-c3986b9df669_1884x1054.png)

**My assessment:** they can cap the upside in a Tier 1 scenario (contained conflict) and moderate it in Tier 2, but they are insufficient for Tier 3 (dual chokepoint). SPR releases buy weeks, not months. OPEC+ spare capacity, even if the Saudis are willing, takes 60-90 days to reach the market. And no amount of domestic permitting replaces barrels that are not flowing through Hormuz.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!gC15!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fcbb995f8-f56f-4974-b78a-3a9415f64eec_1600x720.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!gC15!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fcbb995f8-f56f-4974-b78a-3a9415f64eec_1600x720.png)

I take these counterarguments seriously, and the administration’s toolkit in particular represents the most underpriced variable in the bear case. But I disagree with the conclusion, for reasons I laid out in “The Manufactured Surplus.”

## **What I Am Doing (Positioning Framework)**

I am not issuing specific trade recommendations with entry prices and stop losses, because anyone who does that within 24 hours of a live military conflict is prioritising the appearance of conviction over the reality of uncertainty. What I can do is map the three tiers to positioning logic so you can calibrate to your own risk tolerance and time horizon

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!UwXM!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5d1c2413-1303-417a-bc88-085777c745db_1888x1060.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!UwXM!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5d1c2413-1303-417a-bc88-085777c745db_1888x1060.png)

If you believe Tier 1 is the base case (contained conflict, Kharg restores within days, rapid ceasefire), then the current positioning data, 320,952 lots net-long in Brent, OVX at nearly double historical averages, is a crowded trade that unwinds violently once resolution becomes clear. You want to be reducing long energy exposure into any Monday gap above $80, taking profits on defence names that have already run 6-10% on pre-market flows, and looking at airlines and consumer discretionary as mean-reversion candidates on a two to four week horizon. The risk to this positioning: you are betting that the same parties who just bombed each other sit down for productive conversations within days. The historical base rate on that is poor.

If you believe Tier 2 is the base case (sustained disruption, months of reduced Iranian exports, Hormuz navigable but frictional), then you want exposure to the physical tightening. That means upstream E&Ps with light hedge books and stable-jurisdiction assets that capture higher realisations directly, tanker companies with spot-market exposure where the earnings catalyst is already materialising at $200,000+ per day VLCC rates, and defence names where supplemental appropriations provide a fundamental catalyst rather than a sentiment-driven pop. Detailed equity analysis across these sectors is coming this week. The risk to this positioning: OPEC+ deploys spare capacity faster than expected, the administration’s price-suppression toolkit (SPR releases, sanctions flexibility, OPEC+ pressure) proves more effective than I anticipate, and the $10 per barrel risk premium identified by ING compresses rather than expands.

If you are hedging for Tier 3 (dual chokepoint, $100+ Brent), options are the instrument, not outright positions. The OVX at 58.82 tells you vol is expensive, but if Tier 3 materialises, the current vol surface dramatically underprices the move. Out-of-the-money Brent calls for Q2 expiry are the cleanest expression.

My own probability-weighted view centres on Tier 2 as the most likely landing zone, which implies Brent averaging $85-88 for Q2 2026, roughly $23-26 above where the EIA STEO places it and $21-24 above Goldman’s stale $64 target.

The core analytical tension, and the most important thing to internalise, is the collision between the most bearish fundamental outlook in years and the most bullish geopolitical shock since 1990. These two forces are not additive; they are multiplicative in their uncertainty.

But geopolitics does not care about your demand model.

The reflexivity I described in “The Manufactured Surplus” now cuts in the opposite direction. The same mechanism that kept prices suppressed on a phantom surplus will amplify the upside as every allocator, CTA, and macro fund reads the same Kharg Island headline and reaches for the same exposure.

Monday’s positioning flow could be historic.

But reflexivity is symmetric, and intellectual honesty requires flagging the downside. If the market gaps to $85 on Monday and satellite imagery on Tuesday shows Kharg Island’s loading infrastructure largely intact, the same reflexive mechanism unwinds the entire premium in hours. The net-long becomes a crowded exit.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!3WqH!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd2a204d3-bcec-4407-9349-2ba6885bb158_1884x682.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!3WqH!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd2a204d3-bcec-4407-9349-2ba6885bb158_1884x682.png)

The narrative flips from “largest supply disruption in a decade” to “temporary scare in an oversupplied market,” and the unwind is violent precisely because the buildup was conviction-driven rather than hedging-driven. This is not a minor tail risk; it is the primary risk to Tier 2 and Tier 3 positioning, and readers should size accordingly.

The market was positioned for this. Net-long positions at 22-month highs. OVX nearly double historical averages. Forward curve in backwardation despite a supposed glut. The smart money was not reading the IEA’s surplus headline. It was reading the Strait of Hormuz.
