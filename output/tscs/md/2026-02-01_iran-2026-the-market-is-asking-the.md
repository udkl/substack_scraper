# **2. The Thesis: Three Legs**

A revolutionary regime, much like a poorly constructed derivative portfolio, requires three legs to remain solvent:

1. **Coercive Capacity** (The ability to force compliance)
2. **Economic Resources** (The liquidity to pay the enforcers)
3. **Ideological Legitimacy** (The “story” that keeps the investors/citizens from redeeming their capital)

For forty years, the Islamic Republic maintained this stool through a complex system of internal volatility suppression and external volatility exportation. They exported chaos to Lebanon, Syria, and Iraq via the IRGC, keeping the war far from Iranian borders.

This is not a prediction of immediate collapse. Iran has entered a phase of Managed Decay. The critical variable is not the Supreme Leader. It is not the “kids on the street.” It is the institutional friction between the IRGC and the Artesh.

## **3.2 The Bull Case for an Artesh Intervention**

The argument for an Artesh-led transition is structural.

**Professionalism vs. Ideology:** Artesh officers are nationalists. They swear loyalty to the Supreme Leader, but their institutional identity predates the revolution. They view themselves as guardians of Iran (the nation), not the Velayat-e Faqih (the theological system).

**The “Competence” Arbitrage:** The IRGC has been delegitimized. Their “Forward Defense” failed. Their missiles failed. Their commanders are dead. Why should the Artesh, a professional military, continue to defer to a discredited, incompetent, and corrupt militia?

**The Hedge:** An Artesh general now heads the Joint Staff. As Bokhari notes, this is unprecedented. The Joint Staff has historically been IRGC-dominated. This suggests that even Khamenei, the ultimate survivalist, is hedging his bets. He knows the IRGC is a broken shield.

**The US Signal:** The US bombed IRGC facilities in June 2025 but left Artesh bases largely alone. This is not subtle. It is a direct signal to the Artesh officers: You are not the target. Stay in your barracks, and you survive.

## **3.3 Why it might not happen**

However, we must respect the “Market Structure” of the dictatorship. The IRGC was created specifically to prevent a military coup.

**Constitutional Subordination:** Article 150 of the Constitution authorizes IRGC political activity. The Artesh is legally bound to be apolitical. Breaking this requires breaking the Constitution.

**The Intelligence Moat:** The IRGC controls the intelligence apparatus. Any coordination among Artesh generals would likely be detected by the IRGC Intelligence Organization (SAS) before the first tank rolled out of the barracks.

**The Complexity Risk:** Egypt is a homogeneous society. Iran is an empire. 25% Azeri, 7% Kurdish, 3% Arab. An Artesh coup could trigger fragmentation. If the Artesh moves, do the Kurdish units defect? Do the Baloch units mutiny? The “State” might dissolve in the attempt to save it.

# **5. Scenarios: Managed Decay vs. Abyss**

Based on what I’ve said thus far, we can probability-weight three scenarios. Note that “Status Quo” is not an option. The “Forward Defense” is dead. The “Economic Leg” is broken. The system must move to a new equilibrium.

Before presenting these scenarios, I want to offer a caveat to Bokhari’s framework. The Egypt-Syria binary is analytically useful but potentially misleading. Real transitions rarely conform to ideal types.

Iran could equally follow a Venezuela model: authoritarian retrenchment with managed economic collapse but no regime change, where a hollowed-out state limps along for years, maintained by oil revenue trickles and Chinese support. It could follow an Algeria 2019-2020 model: mass protests leading to cosmetic elite reshuffling (the Hirak movement forced out Bouteflika but left the military-backed system intact). Or it could be something entirely sui generis.

The Egypt comparison assumes military institutional cohesion; Egypt had a unitary military with a shared identity forged over decades. Iran’s dual structure was designed precisely to prevent this. The Syria comparison assumes ethnic fragmentation as the primary driver; Iran’s ethnic minorities are significant (25% Azeri, 7% Kurdish, 3% Arab) but have shown limited appetite for separatism outside of specific border regions like Baluchistan and Kurdistan.

# **6. Why the Regime May Be More Resilient Than It Appears**

Everyone loves a revolution. The videos are compelling. The narrative is clean. But we must be skeptical of “clean” narratives. The Islamic Republic has survived 46 years not by being loved, but by building institutional depth that most observers underestimate.

Here are the strongest arguments against the “collapse” thesis:

### **The Multilayered Elite**

Ray Takeyh, the Hasib J. Sabbagh Senior Fellow for Middle East Studies at the Council on Foreign Relations, argues that “at this point, Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei appears to maintain sufficient control. The country’s ruling elite is too multilayered for regime decapitation, with influential members among high-ranking Shia clergy and the merchant class as well as official government circles.” This is not a top-heavy regime that collapses when the leader dies.

### **The Defense Council: Institutional Adaptation**

The Clingendael Institute (Netherlands) notes that the creation of a Defense Council in August 2025 and Ali Larijani’s appointment as secretary of the Supreme National Security Council are “part of a deliberate effort to embed leadership and command more strongly in institutions that can function without Supreme Leader Khamenei.” The upshot for investors: “the Islamic Republic is improving its resilience in a bid to endure. This makes it less likely to fracture under pressure of protests, sanctions or war.”

Crucially, the Defense Council excludes the most hardline factions (the “Stability Front”), suggesting a pragmatic rebalancing of power within the regime, not collapse.

### **Protests Lack Organization**

Crisis24, the risk consultancy, assesses that “sustained economics-driven unrest is eroding regime legitimacy but has not yet coalesced into an organized movement capable of threatening regime survival. Despite domestic vulnerability, Iran retains strong coercive and military capabilities.” The protests are broad but leaderless. That is a weakness, not a strength.

### **The “Survival = Vindication” Mindset**

Shahram Kholdi, a historian interviewed by Iran International, argues that Tehran is not reading this moment as a loss. Instead, the leadership is internalizing 2025 through a survivalist lens: “If something that can kill you doesn’t destroy you, it makes you stronger.” This belief, he argued, helps explain why executions have continued and why the Islamic Republic is signaling resolve despite suffering unprecedented blows.

### **The Sunk Cost of the Elite**

The IRGC is a corporation with 190,000 employees. It has pension funds. It has construction contracts. The New Lines Institute notes that the IRGC “controls an estimated 50% of Iran’s oil wealth through subsidiary companies and operates vast construction, telecommunications, and export networks worth billions.” This economic empire gives military commanders material stakes in regime survival that transcend ideological loyalty. The entire ruling class knows that if the regime falls, they hang. This creates a powerful incentive for cohesion.

### **The Soviet Parallel**

The Soviet Union did not collapse because of protests. It collapsed because the elite decided to liquidate the asset. We are not there yet. The IRGC commanders are still fighting for their shares. The more likely trajectory is Managed Decay: a long, ugly share of declining legitimacy, periodic crackdowns, and gradual institutional fragmentation. Think the Soviet Union from 1985-1991, not Romania in December 1989.

# **10. Regional Radius**

*Strategist’s Note: The following analysis draws heavily from the “Hundred Year Pivot” transcript with Kamran Bokhari, who provides the historical depth necessary to understand why this is not just about “Democracy vs. Theocracy.”*

The brilliance of Bokhari’s analysis is his focus on the historical imperatives. He notes that Iran’s strategic culture is driven by a 2,500-year-old trauma: the threat from the Western Flank (Iraq/Mesopotamia).

**The Persian Anxiety:** From the Achaemenids to the Sassanids to the Safavids, the threat always comes from the West. The Arabs. The Ottomans. Saddam. The US.

**The Strategy:** The “Forward Defense” was not just Islamist expansionism; it was a Persian imperial necessity. Push the defensive line to the Mediterranean.

**The Failure:** By losing Syria and Hezbollah, Iran has lost its strategic depth. The “Western Flank” is now wide open. This existential panic is what drives the regime’s current volatility.

## **The Regional Contagion**

**Turkey:** A weakened Iran is a double-edged sword. It removes a rival, but it potentially unleashes a Kurdish statelet that threatens Turkey’s own territorial integrity.

**Azerbaijan:** The 25% Azeri population in Iran is the wildcard. If the center collapses, Baku (backed by Israel and Turkey) might make a play for “Southern Azerbaijan” (Northwest Iran). This would trigger a major regional war.

**The Taliban:** They are watching. If a 46-year-old theocracy with oil cannot survive, what chance does a fledgling theocracy without resources have?

## The China Variable

One significant gap in most Western analysis, including my primary source material, is China’s role. Beijing is Iran’s primary economic lifeline, purchasing approximately 90% of Iranian oil exports through a barter system that floods Iranian markets with Chinese goods. How China responds to regime instability matters enormously for all three scenarios.

The evidence suggests Beijing is regime-agnostic. Jonathan Fulton, an expert on China in the Middle East at Zayed University, put it bluntly: “There is utility [for China] in a large anti-Western government in the Gulf, but whether that’s run by the Ayatollah or the military or a council of elders, I think Beijing is largely agnostic. As long as the energy flows, they’re fine with it.”

During the June 2025 war, China’s response was notably passive. Despite joint naval exercises, SCO membership, and the 2021 “25-year strategic partnership,” Beijing did little beyond diplomatic statements urging restraint. Some Chinese think tanks described this posture as “sitting atop a mountain watching tigers fight,” a deliberate neutrality in crises deemed too costly or lacking immediate benefit. When the US struck Iranian nuclear facilities, China confined itself to rhetoric.

Joseph Webster at the Atlantic Council’s Global Energy Center notes that “Chinese refiners have import cover and could replace Iranian oil” given their 1.2-1.4 billion barrel strategic stockpile. However, “Iran losing access to imported goods from China could add to the economic crisis and cause an even greater inflationary spiral.” This is the asymmetry: China can survive without Iran; Iran cannot survive without China.

The investment implication: do not assume Chinese support will prop up the regime. Beijing’s interests are transactional, not ideological. In the “Fragmentation” scenario, China would likely accelerate efforts to secure alternative supply. In the “Artesh Pivot” scenario, Beijing would move quickly to establish relations with any successor regime. China is not a stabilizing force for the Islamic Republic; it is a hedged buyer of Iranian oil who will work with whoever controls the spigot.
