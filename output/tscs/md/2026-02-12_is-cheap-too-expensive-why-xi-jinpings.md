# 2024: The Year Everything Broke (But GDP Didn’t)

To understand where China is going, we need to understand where it just came from.

## The Dollar-for-Dollar Swap

As real estate investment collapsed, something remarkable happened: high-tech manufacturing investment rose by almost exactly the same amount. Naughton describes it as “almost a dollar-for-dollar shift when you look at the credit balances.”

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!DsDX!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3e3f6ff5-2bfd-4992-9170-8a66759d7635_1600x898.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!DsDX!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3e3f6ff5-2bfd-4992-9170-8a66759d7635_1600x898.png)

Less money for housing. More money for factories. The GDP math worked out. Growth stayed near 5%.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5u_-!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F460f1bcf-b10a-4c75-87e0-58db270bae14_1600x895.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5u_-!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F460f1bcf-b10a-4c75-87e0-58db270bae14_1600x895.png)

But think about what this means. All that capital flooding into manufacturing “wasn’t driven by market forces but rather driven by government policy mandates.” The result: virtually every sector has overcapacity, has intense competition and falling prices.  
  
The swap saved GDP growth. It also locked in structural deflation.

**The Fiscal Collapse**

Local government land sales revenue: down 44% from peak

* 2021 (peak): 8.7 trillion yuan
* 2022: 6.7 trillion yuan (-23%)
* 2023: 5.8 trillion yuan (-13%)
* 2024: 4.87 trillion yuan (-16%)

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!1rIT!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd12a3b1b-1439-4715-a6f5-9e408675d473_1600x871.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!1rIT!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd12a3b1b-1439-4715-a6f5-9e408675d473_1600x871.png)

Land revenue used to fund 38% of local government budgets. It’s now down to 27%. The Rhodium Group’s analysis: “Land sales revenues dropped from a peak of 8.7 trillion yuan in 2021 to only 4.9 trillion yuan in 2024.” Three consecutive years of double-digit decline.

For 2025, the Ministry of Finance is projecting fiscal revenue growth of 0.1%. That’s not a typo. Zero point one percent. Even with 5% GDP growth, Beijing expects flat tax receipts because deflation is crushing nominal GDP.

Xi’s question echoes here. If the factories are running and prices are low, isn’t that good? By this measure, yes. The GDP math worked. But the GDP math hides the margin compression that makes the factories unsustainable without perpetual state support.

## The Property Collapse (Worse Than You Think)

The official numbers say home prices fell 9.8% for new homes, 16.9% for existing homes. These are national averages that obscure the disaster in lower-tier cities.  
  
Kenneth Rogoff of Harvard built a database of 298 Chinese cities. His findings:

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Fvym!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7490dc74-32a7-4250-b88d-165bb5025f35_1600x889.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Fvym!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7490dc74-32a7-4250-b88d-165bb5025f35_1600x889.png)

Housing is 59% of Chinese household wealth. When 60% of the country sees their primary asset fall 40%, consumption collapses. Hence: “We’re not taking a vacation because of the economy.”

## Confidence Collapse

Naughton observed something on his return to China that doesn’t show up in any statistic:

“For so many years, people even when they were poor were always optimistic about the future. You had this built-in stabilizer which was the willingness of households to believe that things were going to get better anytime there was a shock, and all of a sudden that disappeared.”

Consumer confidence has been below 100 for 40 consecutive months. The psychological foundation of China’s growth model has cracked.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!T9H5!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe60d83cd-1404-4c6a-8905-2b573fc6bb62_1600x877.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!T9H5!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe60d83cd-1404-4c6a-8905-2b573fc6bb62_1600x877.png)

### **The Numbers Behind the Psychology**

* Consumer Confidence Index: Below 100 for 40 consecutive months (below 100 = pessimistic)
* Household debt to GDP: 60.4% (Q3 2025), down from peak of 62.3% (Q1 2024), people are deleveraging
* Household debt to disposable income: 139% (vs. ~100% in US)
* Property income growth: +2.2% in 2024 (vs. +5.8% wage growth), rental yields collapsing
* Local Government Financing Vehicle debt: 65.9 trillion yuan ($9.26 trillion), about 50% of GDP (IMF estimate)

UBS analysis: Only 65% of LGFVs can service interest on their debts outright. Another 23% need local government subsidies. The remaining 12% are effectively insolvent.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Vc0y!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F461d147d-5816-4b50-aefb-29c3efd16a5c_1600x814.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Vc0y!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F461d147d-5816-4b50-aefb-29c3efd16a5c_1600x814.png)

This is where Xi’s framing breaks down. Low prices may be “good” in the abstract. But when households stop believing in the future, when 40 consecutive months of pessimism become the new baseline, low prices become a symptom of something darker. The price is low because no one wants to buy.

## Why This Matters for the Deflation Thesis

DeepSeek didn’t disprove Chinese weakness. It demonstrated it.  
  
When you have engineers willing to optimize around constraints (export controls, limited chips, no H100 access), you get efficiency gains that compress costs and margins for everyone else. DeepSeek is China exporting deflation to the AI industry, the same way China exports deflation to steel, solar, and batteries.

But here’s the problem: Beijing will interpret DeepSeek as vindication.  
  
The “scaling laws” paradigm said you needed massive compute to achieve frontier AI. American labs spent accordingly. DeepSeek proved you could do it cheap with clever engineering and policy-induced necessity.  
  
From Xi’s perspective, this proves industrial policy works. Constraints breed innovation. The strategy is correct.

“Every time they reach one of these crunch points, they end up doubling down on their technology and industry policy.”

DeepSeek makes the doubling-down more likely, not less.

From Xi’s chair, the answer to his own question just got clearer. Low prices forced innovation. Constraints bred efficiency. DeepSeek is proof that the model works. The reinforcement is complete.

## The November 2025 Policy Response

Naughton’s assessment of AI competition:

The AI race is real. China is competitive. And the competitive strategy is to outproduce, underprice, and accept compressed margins. Sound familiar?

## The Implication

When you produce everything at 30-60% lower cost, you export deflation. You gain market share. But you also compress margins for everyone, including yourself.

Chinese factories are winning the production game and losing the profit game simultaneously. This is sustainable only if the government keeps directing capital into investment regardless of returns.  
  
Which is exactly what’s happening.  
Is this sustainable? Xi would say yes: low costs mean market share, market share means strategic dominance, strategic dominance means security. The Western economist would say no: margin compression eventually starves investment, and state-directed capital misallocates. Both are correct. The question is which timeline matters for your portfolio.

## 2027: The Political Wildcard

The 21st Party Congress will likely occur in fall 2027. Xi Jinping will be 74.  
  
Three scenarios:  
  
**Scenario A: Xi Continues (60% probability)**  
Xi abolished presidential term limits in 2018 and has systematically removed potential rivals from the Politburo Standing Committee. No succession mechanism exists, no heir apparent has emerged, and the Party Congress format gives him complete control over delegate selection. At 74, he would be younger than Biden was when inaugurated.  
  
Investment implication: More of the same. Deflation persists. Critical minerals premium persists. Avoid direct China equity exposure.  
  
**Scenario B: Managed Transition (25% probability)**  
This would require Xi to voluntarily cede operational control while retaining influence, following Deng Xiaoping’s model in the 1980s. Li Qiang or another technocrat could gain space for incremental reform. The probability is nonzero because health, factional pressure, or economic deterioration could shift calculus, but there are no visible signs of preparation for transition.

Investment implication: Marginally positive for China demand. Copper and industrial commodities could catch a bid. But structural issues remain.  
  
**Scenario C: Policy Pivot (15% probability, though we would assign lower)**  
A genuine shift toward consumption-led growth would require Xi to admit the current model has failed. Naughton’s observation that Beijing “doubles down” at every crunch point suggests this is ideologically foreclosed. We include it because regime-level policy reversals, while rare, do occur (Deng’s market reforms, Gorbachev’s glasnost), and assigning zero probability to tail events is analytically reckless.

This is what Naughton says should happen. It’s also what he says won’t happen:

“Every time they reach one of these crunch points, they end up doubling down on their technology and industry policy.”

The 21st Party Congress will be, in effect, a referendum on Xi’s question. If he secures a fourth term with no pivot toward consumption, Beijing is answering definitively: low prices are good, the model is correct, the path continues. The Japanification scenario locks in for a decade.  
  
Investment implication: If this happens, Chinese consumer discretionary and property would rally hard. But don’t bet on it.

## 2028-2030: The Three Paths

By the late 2020s, one of three macro outcomes crystallizes:  
  
**Path 1: Japanification (50% probability)**  
  
China follows Japan’s post-1990 trajectory. Slow grinding deflation. Zombie firms kept alive. Incremental policy adjustments that never quite resolve the underlying imbalances. GDP growth fades to 2-3%. No crisis, but no dynamism.  
  
Duration: 15-20 years of stagnation.

The INSEAD analysis of Japan’s experience:  
  
“The biggest lesson from Japan is ‘take the pain and do so quickly.’ For years, even decades, fiscal and monetary policy were misaligned in Japan. The government also did not let unproductive firms fail, leading to ‘zombie firms’ and continued misallocation of resources.”  
  
China is replicating these mistakes in real time.  
  
**Path 2: Managed Soft Landing (30% probability)**  
Beijing threads the needle. Technology investments pay off. Export competitiveness is maintained while domestic consumption gradually rises. The property sector slowly heals. Growth stabilizes at 4%.  
  
This requires policy changes that show no signs of happening. But China has surprised before.

**Path 3: Disorderly Adjustment (20% probability)**  
  
Something breaks. A major developer actually defaults with contagion. A regional bank crisis. Capital flight that overwhelms controls. The $3 trillion in foreign reserves starts depleting rapidly.  
  
In this scenario, Beijing would likely respond with aggressive stimulus, currency depreciation, and financial repression. Commodities would initially crash on China demand fears, then rally as the stimulus hits.

### **Criticism & The TSCS Pushback**

We’ve laid out a bearish case on Chinese domestic demand and a bullish case on the deflation-export machine. But macro investing is humbling. Here’s where this thesis could be wrong:

**1. “Deflation is bad” is Western orthodoxy, not universal law**

Xi’s question (”isn’t low prices good?”) sounds naive, but consider: Japan had 30 years of deflation and remained a wealthy, stable, technologically advanced society. Real wages in Japan held up better than in many “growing” economies. If China’s deflation is accompanied by rising real incomes and stable employment, the “crisis” framing may be overblown. The households saying “we’re not taking a vacation” might just be being prudent, not panicking.

**2. The cost curve advantage is a moat, not a weakness**

We framed China’s 30-60% cost advantage as “exporting deflation.” But from a competitive strategy perspective, being the low-cost producer of everything is an extraordinarily strong position. CATL, BYD, and the robotics giants aren’t margin-compressed victims; they’re consolidating global market share. When the shakeout ends, survivors will have pricing power. We may be conflating a painful transition with a structural collapse.

**3. The $1.2 trillion surplus shows strength, not weakness**

We interpreted the trade surplus as evidence that China can’t consume its own production. But Chatham House compared it to America’s WWII surpluses. That wasn’t weakness; that was industrial dominance during a global crisis. If the world is de-globalizing and re-arming, being the factory to everyone (except America) is a strategic asset. China’s “Global South” pivot (Africa +26%, ASEAN +13.7%) may be the early stages of a parallel economic order, not a desperate search for buyers.

**4. DeepSeek proves the innovation engine still works**

We argued DeepSeek is “China exporting deflation to AI.” But it’s also a genuine frontier breakthrough that American labs didn’t see coming. If Chinese engineers can match OpenAI at 1/100th the cost under export controls, what happens when constraints ease? The West’s “chip ban” strategy assumed Chinese AI would hit a wall. It didn’t. Betting against Chinese innovation has a poor track record.

**5. We’ve predicted China’s collapse before**

In 2015, everyone was sure the Chinese stock market crash would bring down the economy. In 2016, capital flight was going to drain the reserves. In 2021, Evergrande was going to be Lehman Brothers. Each time, Beijing muddled through. The system is ugly, opaque, and inefficient, but it’s also resilient in ways Western analysts consistently underestimate. The “Japanification” scenario isn’t a crisis; it’s a managed decline that could last decades while China remains the world’s manufacturing hegemon.

**The Honest Position**

If the bearish thesis is right, you want: critical minerals with security premiums, gold, and zero direct China exposure.

If the bullish counterarguments are right, you want: Chinese industrial champions (CATL, BYD, maybe Naura), copper for global electrification, and patience.

We lean bearish on Chinese consumption and bullish on deflation-export dynamics. But we hold that view loosely. The best positioning works in both scenarios: critical minerals that benefit from Western security premiums AND from Chinese industrial demand. Copper is the overlap.

We are not permabears. Specific data would shift our probabilities:

If consumer confidence returns above 100 for six consecutive months without a major stimulus announcement, the “psychological break is permanent” thesis weakens. If Tier 2 and Tier 3 property prices stabilize for two consecutive quarters without direct government purchasing, the wealth destruction feedback loop may be less severe than we model. If PPI turns positive while export volumes remain strong, China may have found a way to maintain production without margin destruction.

Conversely, if LGFV defaults spread beyond isolated cases, or if household savings rates spike above the current 33%, the disorderly adjustment scenario deserves higher probability.

We will revisit these markers in Q3 2026.

**The Question That Remains**

In late 2024, Xi Jinping asked his advisers a question: “Wait, if prices are low, isn’t that a good thing?”

Thirty-nine months of producer deflation. Forty months of consumer pessimism. Forty-one months of falling home prices. The data answers his question one way. Xi answers it another.

The Western framework says deflation is a disease: falling prices discourage spending, compress margins, increase real debt burdens, and spiral into stagnation. Japan is the case study. The treatment is stimulus, currency depreciation, and demand support.

Xi’s framework says deflation is a symptom of success: low prices mean efficient production, efficient production means market share, market share means strategic dominance. DeepSeek trained a frontier model for $300,000. BYD sold 4 million cars. The trade surplus hit $1.2 trillion. By these metrics, the model is working.

Both frameworks are internally consistent. Both have evidence. The question is which one describes reality.

We lean toward the Western framework, not because it’s Western, but because confidence data is harder to fake than production data. You can mandate factory output. You cannot mandate households to believe in the future. And 40 consecutive months of pessimism suggests something is broken that low prices cannot fix.

But we hold that view loosely. China has surprised before. The system is ugly and opaque, but it’s also resilient in ways Western analysts consistently underestimate.

The investment implication is to own assets that work in both scenarios: critical minerals that benefit from Western security premiums regardless of Chinese consumption, copper that benefits from electrification regardless of who builds the grid, and gold that benefits if the Japanification path eventually forces monetary response.

Until Xi asks a different question, the deflation continues. Position accordingly.
