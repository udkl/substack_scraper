On September 8th, a mudslide sent 800,000 tonnes of wet material into the Grasberg Block Cave in Indonesia, killing seven workers and crippling the world’s second-largest copper mine. Freeport-McMoRan declared force majeure. At a stroke, roughly 2.5% of global mine supply went offline, with a full restart not expected until 2027.

The market shrugged.

Copper fell on the day. Analysts published notes about “temporary disruption.” And then everyone went back to arguing about Nvidia’s gross margins.

This is the problem with copper. The market has decided that “Artificial Intelligence” is a weightless phenomenon. It lives in the cloud. It is software, an abstraction, a large language model that exists in the ether. Wall Street cheers for the algorithms and the multi-trillion-dollar valuations attached to them.

They are missing the plot.

AI is not software. It is a greedy, metal-hungry industrial buildout. Every gigawatt of AI compute requires substations, transformers, switchgear, busbars, and tens of thousands of tonnes of cable. That infrastructure runs on copper, not because copper is traditional, but because the laws of thermodynamics leave no alternative at the densities these facilities demand.

And the supply chain that is supposed to deliver that copper is, as of this writing, absolutely fucked.

We are witnessing the collapse of treatment and refining charges (TC/RCs) into negative territory. This means the global supply of copper concentrate has tightened so severely that smelters, the critical link between raw ore and usable metal, are now paying miners for the privilege of processing their material. The 2025 benchmark was set at $21.25 per tonne, down from $80 the year prior. Spot charges have gone negative. The China Smelter Purchase Team has failed to set quarterly guidance for consecutive quarters because there is no price at which the math works.

This is not a cyclical downturn. This is a structural break. The concentrate market was already in deficit before Grasberg. It was already in deficit before the IEA published projections showing data center power demand more than doubling to 945 TWh by 2030. The upstream supply chain is choking at the exact moment that a new, non-substitutable, physics-mandated source of demand is arriving.

The market is celebrating the magic trick. We are focused on the plumbing. And the bill for that plumbing is now due.

## **A World of Two Choices**

Start with first principles. When an engineer is tasked with moving electrons at scale, there is no “market.” There is only a binary, physics-based choice.

There are only two metals available at global scale that make economic and technical sense: copper and aluminum. Yes, silver conducts better, but it is not remotely economical for power hardware. The entire practical A-B comparison for the modern world is copper versus aluminum.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!U_a3!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fccbbebb7-895f-4b42-ad35-4b05bad4cd74_1403x690.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!U_a3!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fccbbebb7-895f-4b42-ad35-4b05bad4cd74_1403x690.png)

This choice is not based on the “price per pound” nonsense you hear from financial publications. It is based on the primary constraint of the design. This is the “Design Filter”.

**Constraint 1: Span, Weight, and Cost.** If the engineer’s problem is stringing a wire 300 meters between two towers, weight is the enemy. Aluminum, being ~70% lighter by density (a fact we’ll return to), is the obvious, non-negotiable winner. This is why the world’s overhead power lines are aluminum.

**Constraint 2: Space, Thermal, and Density.** If the engineer’s problem is stuffing a 10,000-amp busbar into a 24-inch panel, winding a motor, or designing a server rack, space and heat are the enemies. This is the world of modern technology.

The entire “Green Energy Transition” (GET) and AI revolution are stories of electrification density. An EV motor is a bet on power density. A solar inverter is a bet on power density. A data center is the ultimate bet on power density.

The world’s newest and fastest-growing sources of demand (AI, EVs) all fall into the “Constraint 2” category. They are all applications where space and heat are the primary enemies. Therefore, they are all applications that will be forced, by the laws of physics, to default to copper. The old-world applications (overhead grid) are Al; the new-world applications (AI, EVs) are Cu.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!CO1O!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5fc06b6b-6f5a-45a4-a232-bce518bb4dab_1405x780.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!CO1O!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5fc06b6b-6f5a-45a4-a232-bce518bb4dab_1405x780.png)

## **Copper as the Physical Baseline**

Let’s be fundamentalists. What is 100%?

The International Annealed Copper Standard (IACS) sets high-purity annealed copper as the definition of 100% conductivity. It is the yardstick against which all other conductors are measured.

Now, let’s introduce aluminum’s “original sin.” Electrical-grade aluminum (like the 1350 alloy) clocks in at just **~61% IACS**.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!bHbi!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa83cd465-46d7-42e9-bf26-e52a1266828c_1403x708.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!bHbi!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa83cd465-46d7-42e9-bf26-e52a1266828c_1403x708.png)

In practical, engineering terms, this means that to get the same ampacity (move the same amount of current), the aluminum conductor must have roughly **1.6 times the cross-sectional area**.

This is where the “aluminum is lighter” fallacy falls apart. Yes, aluminum is lighter by density (~2.70 g/cm³) compared to copper’s ~8.9 g/cm³. And yes, even after upsizing the area by 1.6x, the aluminum conductor is still about half the weight of the copper one.

But in a data center, nobody cares about weight. They care about volume. The 1.6x area penalty means the aluminum conductor takes up 60% more space. In a world where every square millimeter of a server rack or transformer is battled for, this is a non-starter.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!oTnn!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8043a921-28ba-42f5-b3fb-8b7277d12abb_1404x777.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!oTnn!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8043a921-28ba-42f5-b3fb-8b7277d12abb_1404x777.png)

Then there's the thermal angle, which ends the debate.

> Copper’s thermal conductivity is ~390–400 W/m·K.
>
> Aluminum’s (6101-T6) is ~210–220 W/m·K.

This isn’t just trivia. This means copper conducts heat out of a system at nearly twice the rate of aluminum.

What is the single biggest problem for an AI data center? Heat. Every watt of electricity that goes in becomes a watt of waste heat that must be actively, and expensively, removed.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!G5IW!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fbe4fc6e7-d255-4910-a8b9-e15320897db2_1404x747.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!G5IW!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fbe4fc6e7-d255-4910-a8b9-e15320897db2_1404x747.png)

Copper is not just a passive conductor in this system; it is an active heat sink. It is helping to solve the thermal problem. Aluminum, with its lower thermal conductivity, compounds the thermal problem, forcing even more energy to be spent on cooling.

In the highest-growth, most critical application on earth right now, aluminum is not just “less good”, it is pro-cyclical to the problem. Substitution is a fantasy.

**From Useless Rock to 99.99% Metal**

The market's bullishness on AI is predicated on a supply chain it does not know exists. We are not "finding" copper; we are manufacturing it from useless rock.

Here is the glossary you will need.

> **The Rock (Ore):** We begin by digging up and moving mountains of rock that “typically contain between 0.25 and 1 percent of pure copper”. Put differently: 99.5% of what we mine is waste.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!s-Kd!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0be6ced6-6a27-40e9-94c8-d033d6184a7e_1404x780.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!s-Kd!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0be6ced6-6a27-40e9-94c8-d033d6184a7e_1404x780.png)

> **The Powder (Concentrate):** We then crush, grind, and use a process called froth flotation to turn this rock into a “concentrate.” This powder, which is the real globally traded commodity, averages **30% copper**.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!pRA0!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe404c9ca-f853-41a7-9b1a-9a921f0f52bf_1404x783.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!pRA0!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe404c9ca-f853-41a7-9b1a-9a921f0f52bf_1404x783.png)

> **The Impure Metal (Blister):** This powder is then put into a smelter, an energy-belching furnace, to produce “matte” (50-70% Cu) and then “converted” into “blister” copper, which is **98.5-99.5% pure**.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!CKkA!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8d3c1d50-5a4a-4eb2-a45a-fd2f9eba14c2_1404x774.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!CKkA!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8d3c1d50-5a4a-4eb2-a45a-fd2f9eba14c2_1404x774.png)

> **The Real Thing (Cathode):** This blister is cast into giant plates called anodes and then electrorefined (an incredibly energy-intensive bath) to create the final **>99.99% pure** copper “cathode”. This is the fungible unit that feeds the world.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!vifR!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd10d4cd1-fef7-4cee-8b0b-f8a152503bdb_1405x783.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!vifR!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd10d4cd1-fef7-4cee-8b0b-f8a152503bdb_1405x783.png)

This is a long, complex, multi-stage, and stupendously capital-intensive process. The market sees “copper” as one thing. But this glossary reveals two distinct businesses:

1. **Mining:** The (Rock-to-Concentrate) business.
2. **Smelting & Refining:** The (Concentrate-to-Cathode) business.

These two businesses can have (and do have) completely different fundamentals. The “health” of the entire system depends on the price mechanism between these two halves: the **Treatment & Refining Charges (TC/RCs)**. This is the fee the miner pays the smelter to process its concentrate.

Remember this. The entire bull thesis is hiding in this one obscure, unloved data point. The market, fixated on weak Chinese real estate and downstream operating rates, is missing the fact that the smelting part of the chain (Concentrate-to-Cathode) is in a full-blown, systemic crisis.

## **Why Engineers (Rightly) Fear Aluminum**

We have established aluminum’s volume penalty. Now, we must establish its risk penalty.

An engineer is not paid to be cheap. An engineer is paid to manage risk, specifically fat-tail risk.

We know that aluminum’s 61% conductivity leads to the practical “two AWG sizes larger” rule of thumb. This 1.6x area penalty is what breaks designs in tight conduits and densely packed panels.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!8egf!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4f871898-b4cd-420a-b6e1-fc82fdd9be4f_1404x768.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!8egf!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4f871898-b4cd-420a-b6e1-fc82fdd9be4f_1404x768.png)

But the real issue is reliability. We must lean hard on the physics of failure. Aluminum has two “fatal flaws” at connections:

1. It forms a hard, insulating oxide film instantly upon contact with air.
2. It has a higher thermal expansion coefficient and is a softer metal, making it prone to “creep” or “relaxation” over time.

The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) has a long and storied history with this. Their investigations into aluminum-wired homes in the 1970s provide the perfect, lethal case study. These “flaws” aren’t theoretical; **they caused fires**.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!wzCH!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F04181b4c-9152-4c37-9965-2af2e670009a_1405x786.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!wzCH!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F04181b4c-9152-4c37-9965-2af2e670009a_1405x786.png)

The mechanism is a thermal runaway cycle. The connection heats and cools. The softer aluminum “creeps” and relaxes, making the connection slightly looser. The insulating oxide film builds up. This increases resistance, which creates more heat, which causes more relaxation. The cycle continues until the connection reaches a “Fire Hazard Condition.”

Copper forgives. Its oxide is conductive, and it doesn’t “creep” under torque. Aluminum, as the user’s source states, is unforgiving and “requires strict workmanship”.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!B0Eg!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F963095e1-84d4-4fb0-a974-4f538e299c82_1404x793.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!B0Eg!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F963095e1-84d4-4fb0-a974-4f538e299c82_1404x793.png)

No sane engineer bets a $1 billion data center on the “strict workmanship” of 10,000 different connections. They pay the “premium” for copper not just for its conductivity, but for its forgiveness. It is a risk-management default.

## **“But Aluminum is Cheaper!”**

We must now address the one-note argument of the copper bear: “If copper prices go too high, everyone will just substitute for aluminum, which is cheaper.”

This argument is naive, lazy, and, as we will show, misses the real story entirely.

First, you're defining "cost" wrong.

You are correct that on a “cost per delivered amp” in an open span, aluminum wins. But as we just proved, in the new growth applications (AI, EVs), the primary constraint is density and heat. In these applications, the “cost” of aluminum’s 1.6x volume penalty and its 2x thermal disadvantage is infinite. It is a non-starter.

Second, let's be honest about where substitution is real.

Let’s be honest. The CRU report notes that substitution “will remain a threat”. They are seeing “thrifting” in applications like solar power cables, where copper intensities have fallen.

This is fine. This is healthy. This “thrifting” in non-essential, non-density-constrained applications (like a low-density solar farm cable run) is exactly what should happen. It frees up more of the scarce copper for the applications that cannot be substituted: the data centers, the EV motors, the transformer windings.

Third, the real squeeze.

The market is fundamentally mis-pricing the source of the supply problem.

Here are the facts, pulled directly from the ICBC Standard Bank report.

### **B) The “Pinch-Point”: Smelting, Refining, and Cathode**

This is the industry that turns the 30% concentrate “rock” into 99.99% pure LME-grade metal. And it is the layer that is currently suffocating.

The business model of a smelter is simple: they are paid a fee by miners (Treatment and Refining Charges, or TC/RCs) to process their concentrate. When concentrate is plentiful, TC/RCs are high, and smelters make good money. When concentrate is scarce, as it is now, thanks to Cobre Panamá’s outage and the “shadow integration” we just discussed, TC/RCs collapse as dozens of smelters bid desperately for a limited supply of raw material.

The market in 2025 to us could be in a state of crisis. The 2024 benchmark was set at a comfortable $80 per tonne. In 2025, spot TC/RCs have collapsed to historic lows. We are not just at zero; we are seeing negative spot TC/RCs, a scenario where smelters are paying miners for the privilege of processing their concentrate. For high-cost, un-integrated smelters, this is existential.

## **Connecting to the AI-Grid**

This entire narrative is about a physical process. We start by digging up rock that is, charitably, 0.5% copper. We spend billions to crush, mill, and float it into a 30% “concentrate.” It is then shipped to a smelter, which roasts it into 99.5% “blister,” then refines it into 99.99% pure cathode.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!TKmH!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9eaaa350-04b4-479d-b3a8-d44bc80fd694_1405x790.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!TKmH!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9eaaa350-04b4-479d-b3a8-d44bc80fd694_1405x790.png)

The price that equilibrates the miner (who has the rock) and the smelter (who has the furnace) is the TC/RC. As we’ve established, that price has collapsed to zero or negative.

That's the bottleneck. The world’s smelters, particularly the non-integrated ones in China and Japan, are suffocating. They are being squeezed from two sides:

1. **Supply Crisis:** A structural deficit of concentrate, thanks to mine outages (Cobre Panamá) and “shadow integration” (FCX/Ivanhoe pulling their own supply).

2. **Overcapacity:** A surplus of Chinese smelting capacity, which was built in anticipation of supply that never arrived.

This pain must propagate. A smelter that is losing money on every tonne it processes will do one of two things:

1. **Pivot:** Re-tool to process scrap (the Aurubis/Mitsubishi/Sumitomo strategy).

2. **Die:** Shut down capacity.

The inevitable outcome is that both of these actions result in less refined 99.99% cathode being produced from primary mined ore.

And this cathode supply-crunch is colliding directly with the AI-grid demand-pull, which we see in the “record backlogs” at NKT and the 33.3% growth in the transmission segment at Nexans. This demand is not “substitutable.” You cannot run a high-voltage direct-current (HVDC) subsea cable on aluminum. You cannot build a dense, high-ampacity data center busway with a metal that has 61% of copper’s conductivity.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!B40Z!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1750352b-8f74-465c-9f3b-ff7dad5663b1_1405x791.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!B40Z!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1750352b-8f74-465c-9f3b-ff7dad5663b1_1405x791.png)

The pivot to recycling, as seen in Aurubis’s new Georgia plant, is a response to this crisis, not a solution to it. It is a separate, parallel supply chain that feeds on old scrap. It does nothing to solve the primary bottleneck of turning new rock into new cathode, which is what the world needs to grow.

This map aligns perfectly with our thesis. The physical chain is broken at the “pinch-point” (smelting) at the exact moment that demand from the “pull” (grid/AI) is exploding. The setup is a multi-year repricing of the commodity and the companies that control flow.

#### Why Demand May Be Underestimated: AI and the Grid

The consensus model is failing to price in the two most explosive drivers: the AI data center buildout and the grid investment supercycle.

On **AI data centers**, the market is making a critical error. They see exponential investment, but then wave it away, citing declining copper intensity as fiber optics take share from ethernet. This is first-level thinking. The market is likely too conservative because AI has historically been a small end market, and its true copper consumption is not well known. Then there is the **grid investment supercycle**. This isn’t a minor detail; it is a massive, parallel demand. Electrical networks are the **second largest use of copper** after construction. The IEA’s “Announced Pledges Scenario” (APS) (the one required to hit net zero) expects investment to **scale up 4.5x by 2050**. While the APS is an ideal (and difficult to achieve) scenario, even a fraction of that required buildout represents a structural pull on copper.

#### The New Drivers: ESS, Renewables, and Infrastructure

What is new? The **Energy Storage (ESS or batteries)** market is booming, creating an entirely new and accelerating demand vector.

This is compounded by **Renewables**. The move to decarbonized energy is not a 1:1 replacement; it is a structural step-change in metal intensity. Renewable energy generation requires **3-15 times as much copper** as conventional power generation per unit of installed capacity.

The reason is physics. Renewables demand a massive amount of cabling to connect to the grid, and the core assets, generators, inverters, and transformers, are all copper-intensive.

The data confirms this:

* **Solar:** Copper demand from solar rose from 500kt in 2019 to **~2Mt today** and could reach **3Mt** by the end of the decade.
* **Wind:** Copper demand from wind rose from 400kt in 2019 to **~800kt today** and could reach **2Mt** by the end of the decade.

Finally, **Infrastructure**. The reshoring and massive infrastructure investments just getting underway in the West are a compounding positive. Copper is the primary beneficiary, as it is the essential metal for wiring, plumbing, appliances, telecommunications links, and HVAC systems. #### **A wall of supply?**

The market's hope for a "wall of supply" to solve the deficit does not survive contact with physical reality.

The project pipeline is a mirage. While there are **>65 projects** large enough to contribute 1% or more to global supply (which if achieved would add 170% to current production) the strong majority are nothing more than paper. They are stuck in pre-feasibility or scoping.

The reality? Only **two projects** are in construction and **two** are in pre-production. This is not a “wall of supply.” It is a trickle.

And this assumes projects can be delivered on time. The market is ignoring the timeline. It can take **20-30 years** to bring a new mine online in critical jurisdictions like the US, Chile, or Canada.

The market is also ignoring the evidence. The past year has been defined by a cascade of **supply disruptions**, which strengthens the view that these challenges are structural, not temporary. We are not just talking about weather delays; we are talking about catastrophic failures at major, established mines.

This is the new reality: **long-term challenges are driving up opex and capex intensity** for every new ton of copper. We are not just seeing cyclical inflation; we are seeing a structural repricing of the metal. This includes:

* **Declining Ore Grades:** The easy copper is gone. Miners are forced to move more rock for less metal.
* **Deeper, More Complex Mines:** The industry is moving from open pits to far more complex, dangerous, and expensive underground operations.
* **Rising Input Costs:** Equipment, labor, and energy costs are not returning to “normal.”
* **Regulatory & Permitting Strangulation:** The timeline for permits is now measured in decades, not years.

These factors are not cyclical. They are a structural floor, resulting in a **structurally higher copper price**.

**Why is copper supply getting tighter:**

The market is clinging to the hope of “newly added projects.” This is a critical error. These projects will not prevent a **supply deficit in 2025**. The consensus then points to a marginal surplus until 2027, but this is a statistical blip, not a solution.

The physical reality is a **widening, structural deficit** beginning in 2028. Primary supply itself is now forecast to **peak by 2033**. Supply is already tight, and for seven structural reasons, it is about to get much tighter.

**1. Copper is Geologically Scarce** Looking at a map, the market assumes copper is everywhere. This is a fatal misconception. While mines are globally distributed, copper is **geologically scarce relative to its demand**. The consumption of commodities typically correlates with their abundance; copper breaks this rule. It is **among the most over-utilized commodities** relative to its natural scarcity.

**2. The Ore Grade Collapse** The easy copper is gone. Global copper reserve grades have been in sharp decline since the mid-1990s. We are “high-grading” (mining above the average reserve grade) which is a short-term fix that guarantees a long-term disaster. The quality of future production is in freefall.

The math here is brutal. When ore grade falls from **1%** to **0.5%**, you must mine and mill **twice as much rock** to get the same amount of copper. This is a doubling of effort and cost.

This is the core of the problem:

* **Production Grade:** The average grade of current production has fallen from ~0.95% in the early 2000s to **0.60% in 2024**.
* **Reserve Grade (The Future):** The average grade of reserves has collapsed from 1% in the late 1990s to just **0.50% today**.

Since the late 1990s, the “copper to come” has been of lower quality than the “copper we mine”, a **sharp, structural departure** from all historical trends.

**3. We Are Finding Less Copper** The copper resource base is now flat. Major mines are increasingly harder to find and are uniformly deeper, which structurally requires higher cash costs.

The resource base (USGS estimates reserves at ~1,000Mt, with identified and undiscovered resources at ~2,100Mt and ~3,500Mt, respectively) only grows when discoveries exceed extractions. But the current era has seen **trivial growth (barely above 0%)**. We are no longer refilling the global inventory.

Even “significant” recent discoveries, like Rio Tinto’s Winu deposit, are a perfect illustration of the problem, not the solution. Winu is **500Mt at a 0.45% ore grade**, a grade that is already below today’s historically low average.

#### **Scrap can’t make up for primary weakness but recycled copper will increase**

The market often points to recycling as the simple fix for the supply gap. This is a dangerous miscalculation.

It is true that significant amounts of copper get recycled, but it is **not and will not be enough** to offset the structural decline in primary supply.

The physics of recycling are a constraint. Copper has an average in-use lifecycle of **35 years**. Of the copper that *does* reach its end-of-life, only **44%** is currently collected and separated for reuse.

Even with optimistic modeling, the numbers don’t work. If we assume the 35-year lifetime holds, and the collection rate improves from 44% to **55%**, driven by new recycling innovations and government mandates, the impact is linear in a non-linear demand environment.

In this “optimistic” scenario, recycled copper production could increase from ~10Mt in 2022 to **15Mt by 2040**. This moves scrap from ~30% of the production mix to ~50%. While this higher supply from scrap will *narrow* the potential deficit, it **cannot make up for the primary supply weakness**.

The timeline remains unchanged. We should still see a **copper deficit in 2025 and 2026**, followed by a temporarily balanced market, before the **deficit returns structurally from 2029 onwards**.

This problem is compounded by a geopolitical lag. **China is currently far behind** in the use of recycled copper, using it for only **10% of its mix** (versus 25% for the US and 35% for Asia ex-China).

Beijing knows this is a vulnerability. In response to the concentrate crisis, the Chinese government is now aggressively pushing to build its national recycling industry, with new targets (since 2024) to process old appliances and furniture. China *will* narrow this gap, but it is starting from a deep deficit, and its own industrial demand for scrap will only add another layer of competition to the global market.

**Geopolitical risks offer a lot of risk to the upside for copper**:

But the risk to copper is not just about future wars. It is happening right now, in two distinct ways.

First, **supply is being actively destroyed by policy and instability.** We are not talking about “coups and wars” in the abstract. We are talking about the *physical removal* of hundreds of thousands of tons of copper from the market.

#### Copper Production: New Tech Isn’t Transformational (Yet)

The market’s hope for a “tech breakthrough” to solve supply is misplaced. The physical processing of copper ore is dominated by two conventional, and costly, methods.

1. **The Pyrometallurgical Method:** This is the most widely used, conventional path. It is applied to **high-grade sulphide flotation concentrates**, not raw ore. The process is a multi-stage industrial chain: crushing, grinding, flotation, smelting-refining, and finally, electrorefining.
2. **The Hydrometallurgical Method (Leaching/SxEw):** This method is reserved for **oxides and lower-grade sulfides**. The process is distinct: crushing, leaching, solvent extraction, and electrowinning (SxEw). Its entire economic advantage is that it can extract copper from **low-grade ore** that would be completely uneconomical to process through the high-heat, high-cost pyrometallurgy route.

### The Post-2028 Deficit is Locked In

The market is being lulled to sleep by a “broadly balanced” forecast that runs until 2028/2029. This is the critical blind spot. The market is mistaking a near-term pause for a long-term solution.

After this point, a **significant supply deficit** begins to open and grow. This isn’t a minor gap; it is a structural chasm that could widen by **~1Mt per year**, exploding from equilibrium in 2029 to a **>30% deficit**. That represents over **12Mt of missing supply** by 2040.

This deficit is now effectively locked in. The market is ignoring the physical reality of mine development. The “5-10 year” project model is obsolete. We are now in a **20-30+ year** reality from discovery to operation, thanks to environmental permitting and labyrinthine processes. In the US, the average is **29 years**.

The supply needed for the 2029 deficit *should have been approved five years ago*. It wasn’t. It is increasingly difficult to see how this gap can be closed at today’s copper price.

The market’s obsession with the *near term* is the trap. Yes, the copper balance has many small moving parts, and the market appears “reasonably balanced” for the next few years, even after accounting for the outages at Grasberg (mudslide, September 2025), Kamoa-Kakula (flooding, May 2025), and Cobre Panama (shutdown, ongoing).

They are modeling the *knowns* and ignoring the *structural*. The physical reality is that copper mines are structurally difficult to bring online. Disruptions **do not average out**; they are compounding. And as we have established, long-term challenges are driving up opex and capex intensity, resulting in a **structurally higher price floor**.

### The Price Skew: The Real Market Signal

The best way to understand the copper market is to look at the **price vs. the marginal cost**.

* **Today’s Market:** The market is **one standard deviation tighter** than its 25-year average. We are at **~60 days of inventory** versus the long-term average of 68 days.
* **The Premium:** This tightness means the copper price (at ~$9,200/t) is already commanding a 59% premium to the marginal cash cost of extraction ($5,800/t).

In tight commodity markets, the odds are structurally skewed in favor of higher prices.

Let’s do the math. Global demand is ~30Mt a year. This means one day of inventory is 80kt, and one week is 600kt.

Now, look at the profound asymmetry:

* **Scenario 1 (Downside):** Inventories *grow* by one week. Prices fall to **$9,000/t** (a **15% drop**).
* **Scenario 2 (Upside):** Inventories *fall* by one week. Prices move toward **$16,000/t** (a **>50% jump**).

Even if you assume changes in inventory are random, the **price skew is structurally positive**. This is the physical reality, and it doesn’t even account for the fact that supply disruptions, which are accelerating, only ever push prices in one direction.
