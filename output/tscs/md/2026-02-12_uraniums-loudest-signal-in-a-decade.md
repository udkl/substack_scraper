**Uranium just flashed a signal it hasn’t sent in years. Most people missed it.**

For the first time this cycle, the uranium spot market went into backwardation, with spot prices north of $100/lb while term contracts sat at $88. If you trade commodities, you already know what that means. If you don’t: the market was screaming that there isn’t enough physical material available *right now*, and buyers were willing to pay a premium to get it today rather than wait for tomorrow’s “cheaper” price.

This almost never happens in uranium. When it does, pay attention.

Most people didn’t.

Spot has since pulled back to the mid-$80s. The tourists left. Twitter went quiet. The “uranium is dead” crowd resurfaced from whatever cave they’d been hiding in since the sector ripped 40% in a single month. And here we are, at what I believe is the single most asymmetric setup in commodity markets today, with fewer people paying attention than at any point in the last twelve months.

# Nobody’s Talking About SPUT

Let’s start with the elephant in the room that somehow remains invisible to most market participants.

In January, Sprott Physical Uranium Trust filed a $1.5 billion shelf prospectus and a $1 billion at-the-market facility. In the first 28 trading days of 2026, it raised $386.5 million. On a single day, January 27th, it pulled in somewhere between $60 and $70 million. It now sits on a cash war chest north of $200 million and holds 78.6 million pounds of uranium valued at roughly $6.74 billion.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!KrZ4!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9c0dcc56-dd79-4d65-a070-1e9bf4876f23_1403x783.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!KrZ4!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9c0dcc56-dd79-4d65-a070-1e9bf4876f23_1403x783.png)

Here’s where it gets interesting. Despite all that firepower, SPUT purchased only around 750,000 pounds of physical uranium in January. Justin Huhn, who runs Uranium Insider, a paid uranium newsletter (he’s a market participant with aligned incentive structures, but his analytical work is among the most rigorous in the retail space), described the trust as “treading lightly.” Why? Because the Ontario Securities Commission is in the middle of renegotiating SPUT’s annual purchase limits. The trust is behaving itself. For now.

Think about what happens when that negotiation resolves and SPUT deploys even a fraction of that $200 million war chest into a spot market where 100,000 to 200,000 pounds of buying can move the price. This isn’t a liquid market. This isn’t oil or copper. This is a market where the entire annual spot volume is roughly 50 million pounds, and one entity controls enough dry powder to hoover up a meaningful chunk of it.

The spot price trades at the margin. And SPUT is the margin.

But let me push on this, because I think the SPUT narrative deserves more scrutiny than it typically receives. The trust’s ability to move spot prices is real, but it’s also a double-edged sword. SPUT’s purchasing power creates a synthetic floor under spot, which is great for longs, but it also means the spot price increasingly reflects financial demand rather than utility procurement. If SPUT ever trades at a sustained discount to NAV (as it has in the past, reaching 15-17% discounts), the buying stops entirely. In a severe risk-off environment, SPUT becomes a source of price fragility rather than support. The question isn’t whether SPUT can push prices higher; it’s whether the physical market is tight enough that prices would hold if SPUT disappeared tomorrow. Huhn himself acknowledges the downside scenario: SPUT at a large discount to NAV in a risk-off environment could mean 15-20% downside from current levels. That’s a meaningful asymmetry that cuts both ways.

There’s also a subtlety here that most commentary misses. SPUT isn’t the only financial player in the spot market. Hedge funds, banks, and commodities traders are all positioning net long. This financialization is distinct from utility demand, and when these positions unwind (as they inevitably will at some point), the selling pressure could be sharp. The physical market is tight, yes, but a chunk of what’s being called “tightness” is really financial accumulation masquerading as structural demand.

Kazakhstan Is Peaking. Full Stop.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5Tbo!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F76de5823-e8fe-4203-85ea-26875bcca9ba_1404x759.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5Tbo!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F76de5823-e8fe-4203-85ea-26875bcca9ba_1404x759.png)

I want to be very precise about this because it’s the most consequential development in the supply picture, and the market has not priced it in.

President Tokayev signed amendments on December 26, 2025, requiring Kazatomprom to hold a minimum 75% ownership stake in any new uranium agreement (up from 50% previously). For extensions or production increases, the law requires either a minimum 90% Kazatomprom stake, or the foreign partner must transfer uranium conversion and enrichment technologies. That technology transfer alternative matters, because it means Russia and France could theoretically maintain higher stakes by sharing enrichment know-how, but it also means Kazakhstan is extracting strategic concessions either way. Simultaneously, a new tiered mineral extraction tax took effect January 1st: up to 18% for production exceeding 4,000 tonnes per year, with additional price-linked surcharges: 0.5% above $70/lb, 1% above $80/lb, 1.5% above $90/lb, 2% above $100/lb, and 2.5% above $110/lb. Maximum effective rate at current prices: 20.5%.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!4rJ_!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8de87f70-ce72-4bc4-97a6-b559046168b4_1404x783.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!4rJ_!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8de87f70-ce72-4bc4-97a6-b559046168b4_1404x783.png)

The message from Astana is unmistakable. We’re done subsidizing the world’s nuclear fuel at the expense of our own treasury. You want our uranium? Pay up.

But here’s the part that matters most. Kazatomprom’s 2026 nominal production level came in at 29,697 tonnes on a 100% basis, down from 32,777 tonnes previously, representing roughly a 10% reduction. The company has also signalled it expects to exercise its 20% downflex provision, which could push actual output meaningfully lower. That’s approximately 8 million pounds removed from global supply at the nominal level alone, or about 5% of annual primary production. And the Budenovskoye joint venture, which was supposed to bring substantial new supply online, has been delayed repeatedly. One hundred percent of its 2024-2026 production is already committed to Russia.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Z8xP!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F969cfb5b-f5d0-496b-a59e-d9337d60df97_1403x777.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Z8xP!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F969cfb5b-f5d0-496b-a59e-d9337d60df97_1403x777.png)

Kazakhstan accounts for over 40% of the world’s uranium production. Its output appears poised to peak within two to four years before entering structural decline. The ISR wells deplete. New acid consumption increases. The easy geology is behind them.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Dhra!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd3e6c5ce-c280-4964-8b72-bf86ce32a1a5_1404x738.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Dhra!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd3e6c5ce-c280-4964-8b72-bf86ce32a1a5_1404x738.png)

If you’re building a long-term supply model that assumes Kazakhstan can continue to backstop global demand indefinitely, you’re building on sand.

# The MET’s Real Impact: Price, Not Volume

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!x5JM!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F87d78441-fd79-46a6-8144-ff0e07ffe980_1405x732.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!x5JM!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F87d78441-fd79-46a6-8144-ff0e07ffe980_1405x732.png)

Here’s an analytical point that deserves more attention than it gets. The new mineral extraction taxes are unlikely to reduce Kazakh production volumes, particularly from the two largest joint venture projects. Budenovskoye (Russia’s JV, up to 6,000 tonnes/year capacity) and the Katco JV with Orano (up to 4,000 tonnes/year) will both face the highest tax tiers. But Russia and France are the two entities globally most short on uranium. Russia needs feed for its massive enrichment capacity; France needs it for its reactor fleet. Neither party can afford to slow production.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!h_E2!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd77f80ae-b80a-4c7a-811d-bc7cdd915c78_1405x732.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!h_E2!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd77f80ae-b80a-4c7a-811d-bc7cdd915c78_1405x732.png)

What the MET does is raise the effective cost floor. If Kazakhstan is taxing production at 18-20.5%, that cost gets embedded in the term price. Producers negotiating contracts with utilities are going to pass that through. This is a structural upward ratchet on the global cost curve, not a production cut. It’s more insidious than a supply reduction because it’s permanent and it compounds with every contract signed.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!on0Q!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1e4668ea-2347-42e7-9488-e9095394ab90_1403x762.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!on0Q!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1e4668ea-2347-42e7-9488-e9095394ab90_1403x762.png)

The ownership restructuring (75/25 for new projects, 90/10 for extensions) is arguably even more consequential long-term. Joint venture partners who previously held 49-51% stakes will see their economics deteriorate sharply on renegotiation. Whether they continue investing at the same rate is an open question, and one the market hasn’t seriously grappled with.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Xy7h!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F068bdb10-d1d4-40f7-b952-3442b008ca4a_1404x765.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Xy7h!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F068bdb10-d1d4-40f7-b952-3442b008ca4a_1404x765.png)

A bipartisan bill proposes a $2.5 billion Strategic Resilience Reserve. Trump launched something called “Project Vault” on February 2nd, a $12 billion strategic minerals reserve initiative. Budget reconciliation allocated $7.5 billion for critical minerals.

Energy Secretary Chris Wright told reporters the US should “boost its strategic uranium reserve” and that the administration is “furiously at work” rebuilding domestic fuel supply chains.

Here’s my problem with all of this. None of it produces a single pound of uranium before 2030 at the earliest. Enrichment facilities take years to build. # The Strategic Reserve Nobody Wants (Except the Government)

There’s an interesting political dynamic here that’s worth unpacking. The US government wants to establish a strategic uranium reserve. Utilities are actively lobbying against it. This seems counterintuitive until you understand the incentive structure.

Utilities currently hold roughly 12-18 months of inventory coverage, down from a historical 2-3 years.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FngV!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fced7f45a-a1fe-46a2-a476-25c0c43e22d4_1403x741.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FngV!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fced7f45a-a1fe-46a2-a476-25c0c43e22d4_1403x741.png)

A strategic reserve would, in theory, provide a buffer against supply disruptions. But utilities see a different picture: a price-insensitive government buyer entering an already tight market, competing with them for physical pounds and pushing prices higher. Their concern is immediate cost pressure on their bottom lines, not long-term supply security.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!RRP9!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F48fb2ee0-4323-4d56-931f-8572fe1062de_1404x741.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!RRP9!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F48fb2ee0-4323-4d56-931f-8572fe1062de_1404x741.png)

The government is offering to underwrite, at no cost to utilities, an insurance policy against exactly the kind of supply crisis that the data suggests is coming. And utilities are objecting because it might raise their procurement costs in the near term. This is the kind of short-termism that has characterized utility fuel buying for decades, and it’s precisely why 13 consecutive years of below-replacement contracting happened in the first place.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!oxFF!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F62143963-4509-45e1-86e4-1d997abcbf21_1403x720.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!oxFF!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F62143963-4509-45e1-86e4-1d997abcbf21_1403x720.png)

Whether the reserve materializes is uncertain. The utility lobby is powerful, and the budget politics are complicated. But I’d note that the spirit of the reserve isn’t really about protecting utilities; it’s about recognizing that 20% of the US grid runs on nuclear power, that the US mines only a couple of million pounds domestically while consuming roughly 50 million, and that the country is dangerously dependent on Russia for conversion and enrichment and on Kazakhstan for raw material. That’s a national security problem, not a fuel procurement optimization problem.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!zi6Y!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fbb819658-fff5-4956-bc47-f3f9012650b4_1404x780.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!zi6Y!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fbb819658-fff5-4956-bc47-f3f9012650b4_1404x780.png)

# Big Tech’s Nuclear Land Grab

Now we get to the part that should genuinely terrify anyone who’s short uranium or thinks this is a crowded trade.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!0sO_!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7213270f-80cf-48e6-810c-53be3522d53d_1404x741.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!0sO_!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7213270f-80cf-48e6-810c-53be3522d53d_1404x741.png)

Over 16 GW of nuclear capacity has been announced by Big Tech in the past year, with Meta alone committing to 6.6 GW in a single month (January 2026), spanning PPAs with Vistra, TerraPower Natrium reactors, and Oklo’s Aurora campus. Amazon’s portfolio exceeds 7 GW, Microsoft is restarting Three Mile Island with Constellation, and Google has contracted roughly 1.1 GW via Kairos Power. Huhn said it plainly on the MacroVoices podcast (January 2026 episode): “The nuclear renaissance is absolutely game on.” But here’s the question nobody in Silicon Valley has answered yet: where does the fuel come from?

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!a-jO!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1a0e0fee-5413-4be3-94cc-560f7a784a5a_1404x783.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!a-jO!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1a0e0fee-5413-4be3-94cc-560f7a784a5a_1404x783.png)

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!SYAh!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F31eaa160-e2ed-4ba1-a81d-1b1c42b93510_1405x783.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!SYAh!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F31eaa160-e2ed-4ba1-a81d-1b1c42b93510_1405x783.png)

These companies have signed power purchase agreements. They have not, to my knowledge, signed fuel procurement agreements. They’ve locked in the electricity but not the uranium. And there’s a fascinating precedent developing. Amazon recently signed a copper offtake agreement with Rio Tinto, going directly to the mine for its metal supply. If Amazon applies that same logic to uranium, and it would be irrational not to, we’re looking at a completely new class of buyer entering a market that already can’t satisfy existing demand.

Imagine Zuckerberg’s procurement team competing against EDF and Korea Hydro for uncontracted pounds from Cameco. That’s not a hypothetical. That’s the next three years.

# A Word of Skepticism on Big Tech Demand

I want to challenge my own thesis here, because the Big Tech nuclear narrative has become consensus, and consensus deserves scrutiny.

The 16+ GW of announced nuclear capacity is impressive on paper. But announcements are not reactors. The history of US nuclear construction is a history of delays, cost overruns, and cancellations. Vogtle Units 3 and 4, the most recent US large-scale nuclear build, came in years late and billions over budget. The AP1000 design that was supposed to be “standardized” and “modular” turned out to be neither in practice.

SMRs remain largely theoretical at commercial scale. NuScale’s first project was cancelled. TerraPower’s Natrium is still pre-construction. The assumption that tech companies can simply will nuclear capacity into existence by writing large checks ignores the regulatory, engineering, and construction realities that have plagued this industry for decades.

Moreover, the timeline matters enormously for the investment thesis. Even if every announced reactor gets built, most won’t be operational until the 2030s at earliest. The uranium demand impact from Big Tech is a 2032-2040 story, not a 2026-2028 story. For the near-term price trajectory, existing reactor demand, life extensions, and the contracting gap matter far more than Silicon Valley press releases.

The right-tail scenario that Huhn identifies, tech companies directly offtaking uranium from producers, would be genuinely transformative. But it hasn’t happened yet, and there’s a meaningful gap between “it would be rational for them to do this” and “they will actually do this.” Tech companies may simply rely on their utility counterparties to manage fuel procurement, which would mute the direct impact on uranium markets.

# The Contracting Gap That Swallows Everything

Let me walk you through the single most important chart in the uranium market, the one that tells you everything about where this is going.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!rCW2!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fca2d3413-717d-4622-8b64-cd762a6f2e72_1405x726.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!rCW2!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fca2d3413-717d-4622-8b64-cd762a6f2e72_1405x726.png)

In Q4 2025, utilities contracted approximately 72 million pounds of uranium, more than half of the entire year’s volume crammed into the final quarter. Full-year 2025 contracting came in at roughly 116 million pounds. That sounds like a lot. It’s not. The annual replacement rate, the amount utilities need to contract just to maintain current inventory coverage, is approximately 150 million pounds. 2025 was the thirteenth consecutive year that contracting fell below replacement.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!qLy0!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9f1cdd39-4ca6-4c89-8c84-2ac2829b00cc_1405x783.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!qLy0!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9f1cdd39-4ca6-4c89-8c84-2ac2829b00cc_1405x783.png)

Thirteen years. Let that number settle in. The 150 million pound annual replacement rate assumes steady-state operation of the current global fleet of approximately 440 reactors at historical capacity factors, plus initial fuel loadings for reactors entering operation. If fleet utilization declines or retirements accelerate, the replacement rate drops. Under almost any plausible scenario, however, contracting has been insufficient. The question is whether the gap is 20% or 35%.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!aLoP!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe80948c4-3b48-4610-8e9e-ea1c39a0ec6e_1403x786.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!aLoP!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe80948c4-3b48-4610-8e9e-ea1c39a0ec6e_1403x786.png)

UxC estimates cumulative uncovered utility requirements at roughly 2.1 billion pounds through 2040. That’s not speculative demand from hypothetical reactors in China. That’s fuel needed for reactors that already exist, that are already generating electricity, that have already been licensed to operate through the 2040s and beyond.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FkWi!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7e581a61-5044-4291-a6c7-54ac6c30ef0c_1404x780.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FkWi!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7e581a61-5044-4291-a6c7-54ac6c30ef0c_1404x780.png)

Why Utilities Have Been Wrong for a Decade (and Why They’re Starting to Change)

To understand the contracting gap, you need to understand how utility fuel buyers think, and why their mental model has been catastrophically wrong for this cycle.

The history of uranium procurement, from the late 1990s through the 2010s, trained fuel buyers to believe that cheap uranium would always be available. The Megatons to Megawatts program delivered 20 million pounds per year of down-blended Russian HEU into the US market for 20 years. The post-Fukushima demand destruction released hundreds of millions of pounds of surplus inventory. Utilities engaged in hundreds, possibly thousands, of carry trades where they’d instruct a trader to buy spot material for delivery 2-4 years out, avoiding long-term producer commitments entirely.

This worked beautifully in a world of structural oversupply. It does not work in the current environment, and the fuel buyers are only now beginning to accept this.

The carry trade, in particular, deserves explanation because it’s central to understanding why the buffer is gone. When surplus inventory existed, traders could buy spot material cheaply, store it (the carry cost was low), and deliver it to utilities at a slight premium to spot. This was cheaper than long-term contracts and gave utilities maximum flexibility. But SPUT’s emergence in 2021 triggered a “reverse carry trade”: traders holding pounds in carry saw spot prices spike, sold those held pounds into the rising spot market for profit, then went back to the midterm market to re-procure from producers. This process systematically drained mobile inventory from the market.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!k9kz!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe68847e7-f833-4fd1-87f5-7faef8d6673f_1405x732.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!k9kz!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe68847e7-f833-4fd1-87f5-7faef8d6673f_1405x732.png)

UxC declared in August 2023 that “the age of inventory overhang is over.” They weren’t exaggerating. The mobile commercial inventories that buffered every previous price spike are largely gone.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-TLQ!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F488bb994-bca6-40fd-aa74-a0496634ef23_1405x756.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-TLQ!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F488bb994-bca6-40fd-aa74-a0496634ef23_1405x756.png)

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!l8w7!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F57ed3e7f-7be5-495e-98c4-ac4ff94deb6c_1404x360.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!l8w7!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F57ed3e7f-7be5-495e-98c4-ac4ff94deb6c_1404x360.png)

Now look at what’s happened to contract terms. The old fixed-price contracts ($40-60/lb base-escalated deals signed in the late 2010s) have been replaced by market-referenced formulas with floor-ceiling spreads. Floors are running $75 to $85 per pound. Ceilings have migrated from $130 to $140-160 per pound. Cameco’s CFO recently noted that the company is demanding ceilings of $140 to $150 per pound, up from $130.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!kd9n!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd44d7b82-b2f4-4048-8511-8a0ed9024d58_1403x768.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!kd9n!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd44d7b82-b2f4-4048-8511-8a0ed9024d58_1403x768.png)

The ceiling on a uranium supply contract is the market’s confession about where it thinks spot could go. When Cameco demands a $150 ceiling and utilities agree to it, both parties are implicitly acknowledging that $150 spot uranium is a real possibility.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!2brD!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdd3752fd-de6e-4ea7-aac8-70aedc96b2b8_1403x771.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!2brD!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdd3752fd-de6e-4ea7-aac8-70aedc96b2b8_1403x771.png)

# Utility Issue: Locked Revenue, Open-Ended Costs

Here’s an analytical dimension that most uranium commentary overlooks entirely, and it’s the mechanism that’s going to force the next wave of contracting.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!c1QJ!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdedb4f0a-a77c-45b6-a681-be9e27087173_1405x747.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!c1QJ!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdedb4f0a-a77c-45b6-a681-be9e27087173_1405x747.png)

Utilities are signing long-term power purchase agreements with electricity consumers, often 10-year deals with fixed prices adjusted for inflation. These PPAs lock in what the utility will earn per megawatt-hour. Then the utility calls Cameco to source fuel and hears: “$85 floor, $150-160 ceiling, referenced to market at time of delivery.”

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!8i_a!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4da9642a-5fd1-4c4a-9769-5ca0fe19b6c4_1405x786.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!8i_a!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4da9642a-5fd1-4c4a-9769-5ca0fe19b6c4_1405x786.png)

Do the arithmetic. The utility knows what it’s earning. It has no idea what it’s paying for fuel. The spread between floor and ceiling is $65-75 per pound. For a large utility burning 10-15 million pounds per year, that’s a variance of $650 million to over $1 billion on their fuel bill depending on where spot sits at delivery.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!KlLW!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd6db3b41-a868-4fbc-b3b2-7f732d636903_1404x786.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!KlLW!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd6db3b41-a868-4fbc-b3b2-7f732d636903_1404x786.png)

This is untenable for any CFO trying to run a predictable business. And yet the alternative, not contracting and relying on spot, is worse. The spot market is thinner, more volatile, and increasingly dominated by financial players.

Producers know this, which is why they’re demanding market-referenced contracts rather than fixed-price deals. They want exposure to higher prices. Their shareholders, after enduring a decade of sub-economic pricing that led to massive dilution and write-downs through the 2010s, are pressuring management to capture upside rather than give it away in fixed-price contracts. When brownfield restart companies signed base-escalated contracts at $80/lb, shareholders pushed back, demanding that management hold out for market exposure.

This dynamic tells you more than any supply-demand model. The sellers are so confident prices are going higher that they’re structuring contracts to capture that upside. The buyers are slowly moving from denial to acceptance, to borrow Huhn’s framing. And each contract signed at wider ceiling ranges becomes the new benchmark for the next negotiation.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!OvZs!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7d344270-d1de-41c8-8cb3-7ae5d9bc461b_1403x782.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!OvZs!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7d344270-d1de-41c8-8cb3-7ae5d9bc461b_1403x782.png)

Cameco’s CFO also said something revealing about tariffs, which I address in detail in the bear case below.

# The Secondary Supply Collapse

This section requires some technical detail, but it’s essential because the secondary supply picture is where the bull thesis either holds or breaks.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!bz13!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3ec4a720-aa38-4363-92ae-89033a0aaa53_1404x759.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!bz13!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3ec4a720-aa38-4363-92ae-89033a0aaa53_1404x759.png)

Secondary supply comes from two main sources: mobile commercial inventories (discussed above) and technical supply from the enrichment process (underfeeding and tails re-enrichment).

Underfeeding works like this: when an enricher has excess centrifuge capacity, it can spin the cascade to a lower tails assay than the utility’s contract specifies. This extracts more U-235 from the feed material than necessary, leaving the enricher with surplus natural-equivalent UF6 that it can sell into the market. Five years ago, underfeeding contributed 25-30 million pounds per year to global supply. That number has collapsed to approximately 10 million pounds, and Huhn suggests it could fall further.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!li57!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff74fe17e-513a-4b0c-b97d-cdcff3414f14_1404x789.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!li57!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff74fe17e-513a-4b0c-b97d-cdcff3414f14_1404x789.png)

The reason is straightforward: enrichment demand is rising, and excess capacity is disappearing. As more enrichment capacity gets contracted, there’s less room to underfeed. In fact, the market may soon tip into “overfeeding,” where enrichers require more natural uranium feed than the standard assay would dictate, creating incremental demand rather than supply. This reversal, from a 25-30 million pound annual supply contribution to a potential demand contribution, is one of the most underappreciated shifts in the entire market.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Vwtc!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdac34d1e-cbc5-4563-b53e-896f7236f096_1404x783.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Vwtc!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdac34d1e-cbc5-4563-b53e-896f7236f096_1404x783.png)

Tails re-enrichment (spinning depleted tails material back up to natural-equivalent UF6) has similarly declined as enrichment capacity tightens. Russia, which historically performed most tails re-enrichment, is redirecting capacity toward primary enrichment contracts.

UxC projects secondary supply declining from roughly 27 million pounds (14% of total supply) in 2025 to about 15 million pounds (4% of total) by 2040. Every single secondary supply buffer is shrinking simultaneously while demand growth accelerates.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!zn8l!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4184623c-c172-4f67-bc14-c387ea9c4f7e_1404x762.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!zn8l!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4184623c-c172-4f67-bc14-c387ea9c4f7e_1404x762.png)

US utilities currently hold 12-18 months of coverage, down from a historical 2-3 years. European utilities are somewhat better positioned at 2.3-2.7 years, but they’re also facing the loss of Russian enrichment services.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!AI_Z!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa3bb2470-0d9f-414e-8593-5e73d78e2f31_1404x414.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!AI_Z!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa3bb2470-0d9f-414e-8593-5e73d78e2f31_1404x414.png)

# A Note on China’s Inventory

One common bearish argument cites China’s massive uranium inventory, reportedly north of 600 million pounds, as evidence that global supplies are more abundant than bulls suggest. This deserves a careful response.

China’s inventory figure includes total strategic and commercial holdings, encompassing military inventory (nuclear submarines, weapons programs), strategic state reserves, and civilian utility stockpiles. The portion actually available for commercial reactor fuel is substantially smaller than the headline number, and crucially, none of it is for sale. China is a net accumulator. Once uranium enters the country, it doesn’t leave (with minor exceptions for trading Russian enrichment services into Western markets at small volumes).

China has 58-59 operable reactors, 33 under construction, and targets 150-200 GWe by 2035. They are scouring the globe for additional supply, negotiating directly with Cameco and looking to Canada as a primary source. When a sovereign with the world’s most aggressive nuclear build program is simultaneously holding a massive strategic stockpile and aggressively procuring more, that’s not a bearish signal. It’s an indication that the country’s own internal assessment of future supply adequacy is deeply pessimistic.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!IK3y!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F251336d3-9f08-4146-9348-8fa95abf201f_1403x789.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!IK3y!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F251336d3-9f08-4146-9348-8fa95abf201f_1403x789.png)

India is reportedly finalizing a $2.8 billion deal with Cameco for 100 million pounds over ten years, announced at the G20 sidelines in Johannesburg in late November 2025. The deal hasn’t been formally signed as of this writing, but the diplomatic signalling is unambiguous.

Both India and China are state-owned nuclear operators approaching Canada (the West’s most reliable supplier) for long-term commitments. When Eastern sovereigns are stepping up with price-insensitive buying while Western utilities are still “hemming and hawing” over a few dollars per pound, that tells you who understands the supply picture and who doesn’t.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!KX33!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F690a8ea0-8d11-4738-8f34-da8a847e89ca_1405x789.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!KX33!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F690a8ea0-8d11-4738-8f34-da8a847e89ca_1405x789.png)

# The Equity Rip Was Real, and It’s Not Done

January 2026 was one of the most violent moves in uranium equity history.

The Northshore Global Uranium Mining Index gained 39.49% in a single month. The Nasdaq Sprott Junior Uranium Miners Index did 45.25%. URNM returned 26.74% on price alone, 40.73% total return YTD. Cameco hit an all-time high near $135, up 37% year-to-date, nearly doubling year-over-year.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!L7js!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F39780f63-4b71-4b59-aab5-727975be6190_1405x758.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!L7js!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F39780f63-4b71-4b59-aab5-727975be6190_1405x758.png)

I’ll say two things about NexGen. First, the Culper report deserves to be taken seriously, not because short sellers are always right (they’re usually wrong about commodity projects), but because CapEx escalation in mining is the norm, not the exception, and anyone who has modeled Arrow at the DFS numbers without stress-testing them was being naive. Second, the CNSC Part 2 hearing runs February 9-12 and represents the final regulatory step before a construction license. If it passes, the short thesis collapses overnight. If it doesn’t, we have a much larger problem.

# Arrow and the Supply Narrative’s Fragility

This is worth dwelling on because NexGen’s Arrow deposit is not just another mining project; it’s load-bearing infrastructure for the entire uranium supply outlook.

The nuclear industry’s supply-demand models essentially pencil in Arrow producing 29 million pounds per year starting around 2031. Huhn was blunt about this on the same MacroVoices interview: “That isn’t happening.” Even by NexGen’s own updated timelines, first production is 2032 at earliest, and that would be ramp-up, not nameplate capacity. Full production at the scale the market expects is likely a 2033-2034 event, and historically, uranium mines are never built on time or on budget.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!4LIu!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fcdb549d4-07c4-4566-a757-ab90e1f125c6_1405x788.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!4LIu!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fcdb549d4-07c4-4566-a757-ab90e1f125c6_1405x788.png)

NexGen’s management is already publicly signalling that they may modulate production based on market conditions, rather than running flat-out at nameplate. This is rational from a shareholder value perspective (why flood a tight market when you can extract monopoly rents by restricting supply?), but it means the industry’s assumption that Arrow solves the 2030s supply gap is built on sand.

If Arrow is delayed by two years, comes in at 70% of nameplate, or both, the supply gap widens dramatically. And Arrow is the single most important development-stage project globally. Every other major greenfield project is earlier-stage, lower-grade, or more geologically challenging.

Goehring & Rozencwajg, whose commodity calls have been among the best in the business this cycle, have noted that short interest surges in uranium equities historically provide “the best buy signal we get this cycle.” Rick Rule, who has been doing this for forty years and holds meaningful uranium positions, calls it “sure money.” I wouldn’t use that phrase myself, as nothing in commodities is sure, but Rule’s track record in resource cycles gives the comment more weight than the typical newsletter pump.

# The Equity Landscape: Who Benefits and How

If the thesis holds, the question becomes how to express it. The uranium equity universe is small, illiquid, and stratified by risk in ways that matter more than in most commodity sectors. What follows is a snapshot of the primary vehicles (as of early February 2026), not recommendations, but a map of how capital is currently positioned across the space.

At the top sits Cameco (CCJ/CCO), the Western world’s dominant integrated uranium producer with a market capitalisation of roughly $52 billion, licensed capacity exceeding 30 million pounds annually, and a vertically integrated business spanning mining, conversion, fuel services, and a controlling interest in Westinghouse through the Brookfield partnership. At $120 per share (having touched an all-time high near $135 in January), Cameco trades at a premium that reflects its status as the sector’s de facto blue chip, the safest expression of the thesis but arguably the most fully priced. Its contract book is increasingly market-referenced, meaning shareholders get direct exposure to rising term prices. Kazatomprom (KAP), the world’s largest producer by volume, offers exposure to the supply side of the equation discussed at length above, but carries the jurisdictional, fiscal, and ownership restructuring risks that make it a fundamentally different proposition.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Lv7k!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5a18ac48-c851-47ee-851c-0856836b6da6_1402x637.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Lv7k!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5a18ac48-c851-47ee-851c-0856836b6da6_1402x637.png)

The developers occupy the high-beta end of the spectrum, and two names stand out. NexGen Energy (NXE), at roughly $10 billion market capitalisation and $15 per share, is the single most important development-stage company in the sector. Arrow’s 29 million pounds per year nameplate capacity and sub-$10/lb operating costs are extraordinary on paper; the Culper short report and CNSC Part 2 hearing (February 9-12) introduce binary near-term risk. Huhn’s framing on MacroVoices is the right one: if you value NexGen at $120 uranium rather than the $50-60 assumed in older feasibility studies, “it’s a very different environment.” Denison Mines (DNN), at roughly $3.5 billion and $3.93 per share, offers a complementary risk profile: its Wheeler River Phoenix deposit uses in-situ recovery (Canada’s first, if approved), with a potentially faster path to production (targeting 2028) and lower capex requirements than NexGen’s conventional mining approach. Both are pre-revenue, both are Athabasca Basin assets, and both carry permitting risk that could make or break the investment, but the revaluation potential if the term price migrates to $120-150 is substantial. Both Huhn and Townsend hold positions in these names and in SPUT, which is worth noting not as an endorsement but as evidence that informed market participants with skin in the game consider the risk-reward at current levels compelling.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-2c3!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F77a9f96c-47dc-4049-8bbf-e88370db1594_1402x615.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-2c3!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F77a9f96c-47dc-4049-8bbf-e88370db1594_1402x615.png)

On the US-focused side, Uranium Energy Corp (UEC) carries a market capitalisation near $8 billion with ISR assets across Texas and Wyoming, positioned as the primary domestic supply security play. It is producing, albeit at modest volumes, and its portfolio of hub-and-spoke ISR operations can theoretically scale faster than conventional mines. Energy Fuels (UUUU), recently expanded through its acquisition of Rio Tinto’s Sweetwater mill and Wyoming assets, has licensed capacity of 12.1 million pounds annually and diversification into rare earths, though its current production remains well below that theoretical ceiling. Centrus Energy (LEU) offers something different entirely: enrichment exposure rather than mining, which is arguably the more constrained bottleneck in the fuel cycle, though its revenue profile is heavily dependent on government contracts and the HALEU program.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!b2j3!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fad0d2475-57c3-4263-beb7-1d0b1025fda2_1402x789.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!b2j3!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fad0d2475-57c3-4263-beb7-1d0b1025fda2_1402x789.png)

For those who want commodity exposure without single-stock risk, Sprott Physical Uranium Trust (SPUT/U.UN) may be the single most attractive risk-reward vehicle in the sector. Huhn described the setup on MacroVoices as offering maximum downside of 15-20% in a severe risk-off scenario against potential upside of 100% or more if the spot squeeze materialises, a ratio that is difficult to find elsewhere in commodities at present. SPUT provides direct exposure to physical uranium, and as discussed at length above, sits on a war chest that could meaningfully move the spot market once OSC negotiations resolve. Yellow Cake (YCA), its London-listed equivalent, offers similar exposure through a long-term supply agreement with Kazatomprom. The ETFs, URNM and URA, provide diversified baskets weighted toward the names above, with URNM more concentrated in pure-play uranium equities and URA including broader nuclear exposure.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!DsT6!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F79d4505b-fb4b-40e7-961c-cd8165b22915_1405x369.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!DsT6!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F79d4505b-fb4b-40e7-961c-cd8165b22915_1405x369.png)

Finally, at the frontier end, Paladin Energy (PDN) has restarted its Langer Heinrich mine in Namibia and represents leveraged Australian-listed exposure to spot pricing, while Global Atomic’s Dasa project in Niger offers high-grade economics overshadowed by significant post-coup jurisdiction risk, a useful illustration of the spectrum between geological quality and political investability.

The critical analytical point is that the market currently prices developers’ future production at a fraction of what it pays for Cameco’s. NexGen trades at roughly $179 per pound of future production capacity versus Cameco at over $1,400; Denison sits even cheaper. Whether that discount reflects genuine execution risk or structural mispricing is the central equity question in this sector. If the term price migrates toward $120-150 as the contracting data suggests it must, the developers’ economics transform, and their current valuations become difficult to justify as anything other than optionality on execution. That said, optionality has a way of expiring worthless in mining, which is why the diversified basket approach, developers plus a core SPUT holding for direct commodity exposure, remains the most sensible construction for anyone sizing this as a meaningful portfolio allocation rather than a speculative punt.

This probability distribution suggests meaningful allocation with strict position limits rather than concentrated betting. The thesis is high-conviction directionally, but the range of outcomes is wide enough that sizing discipline matters more than entry price.

# What Could Go Wrong?

I’d be dishonest if I didn’t address the bear case, and unlike most uranium commentary, I want to give the risks genuine weight rather than listing them as caveats to dismiss. If you’re going to allocate meaningful capital to this thesis, you need to understand what could destroy it.

Nuclear Accident: The Unhedgeable Tail Risk

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!8ehN!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3bbebdc3-6a9c-489f-93dd-9761907f1a3f_1402x767.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!8ehN!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3bbebdc3-6a9c-489f-93dd-9761907f1a3f_1402x767.png)

Fukushima destroyed 10-15% of global uranium demand overnight. Japan shut off all 54 reactors. Germany accelerated its nuclear phase-out. Belgium and Taiwan initiated their own exit plans. The price crashed and didn’t recover for over a decade. The probability of a comparable event is low but the tail risk is enormous, and it’s genuinely unhedgeable. If a reactor in France or the US has a serious incident, this trade goes to zero and stays there for years. You cannot position-size your way out of this risk. You can only accept it.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!YzGD!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd61209b0-3979-40a4-b032-a1ce8d909f3e_1405x764.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!YzGD!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd61209b0-3979-40a4-b032-a1ce8d909f3e_1405x764.png)

That said, I want to qualify this risk more carefully than the standard bear case does. The post-Fukushima industry has implemented significantly more stringent safety protocols. And critically, demand destruction from an accident depends on the political response, not just the event itself. In a world where 31 countries have signed the Declaration to Triple Nuclear Energy, where tech companies have committed billions, and where energy security has become a first-order geopolitical concern, the political response to an accident might be different from 2011. Not guaranteed, but the structural incentives around nuclear have fundamentally shifted. A Fukushima-equivalent event in 2026 would probably cause a severe drawdown in uranium equities, but the demand destruction might be more limited and shorter-lived than it was in 2011, when nuclear had no powerful institutional champions.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!pvC1!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F50f324bd-999f-4872-98ad-82d8221bafc6_1406x708.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!pvC1!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F50f324bd-999f-4872-98ad-82d8221bafc6_1406x708.png)

## The Enrichment Bottleneck: The Most Underappreciated Risk

Russia controls 44% of global enrichment capacity. The West has no viable short-term alternative. This is the risk that keeps me up at night more than any other.

The uranium bull thesis implicitly assumes that the fuel cycle works. You mine U3O8, convert it to UF6, enrich it, fabricate it into fuel assemblies, and load it into a reactor. If any step in that chain breaks, demand for raw uranium could paradoxically fall even as the need for nuclear electricity rises. Reactors that can’t get enriched fuel shut down, regardless of how much yellowcake is sitting in a warehouse somewhere.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!NZ88!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fec653aed-90f8-4352-ab67-614f81a84dd7_1405x789.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!NZ88!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fec653aed-90f8-4352-ab67-614f81a84dd7_1405x789.png)

The US import ban on Russian enrichment doesn’t fully phase out until 2028, and even then, replacing 44% of global enrichment capacity takes a decade of construction. Centrus, General Matter, and Orano’s new US facilities won’t reach meaningful scale until the early 2030s at best. In the interim, the West is dependent on either Russian enrichment (politically untenable if the conflict escalates) or creative workarounds like enrichment swaps through Chinese intermediaries (which the US is already investigating).

Here’s the paradoxical outcome nobody models: if Russia restricts enrichment exports (or the West tightens sanctions further), uranium prices could spike on the headline, but utilities physically cannot fabricate fuel. Reactor shutdowns follow. Demand destruction occurs not because countries don’t want nuclear power, but because the supply chain is broken. In this scenario, uranium equities could initially rip higher on the squeeze, then collapse as the market realizes the demand isn’t coming because the downstream infrastructure doesn’t exist.

I assign this scenario a probability in the range of 20-30% over a five-year horizon, which may surprise readers given how rarely enrichment supply chain risk appears in uranium commentary. The reason for the higher estimate is that the risk isn’t singular. It compounds across at least four independent pathways: further US sanctions tightening in response to geopolitical escalation, Russian domestic reallocation of enrichment capacity as its own reactor fleet expands (Goldman notes Russia plans to add 15 reactors), closure of Chinese intermediary channels under sanctions enforcement, and political expiration of waiver mechanisms. Each carries perhaps a 7-10% individual probability over five years.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!eS1-!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0778ba73-36d6-4e1a-b755-a1387e174707_1405x785.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!eS1-!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0778ba73-36d6-4e1a-b755-a1387e174707_1405x785.png)

The compound probability that at least one materializes is considerably higher than any single pathway suggests. And critically, the Western enrichment buildout that would resolve this vulnerability won’t reach meaningful scale until the early 2030s. Orano’s new Oak Ridge facility targets initial LEU production in 2031. The gap between now and then is the danger zone, and the magnitude of the downside is severe enough that it should reshape how aggressively anyone sizes this position.

## Tariffs: More Than Econ 101

Cameco’s CFO described the tariff impact as “Econ 101”: a 10% tariff leads to roughly a 10% price increase. This framing is convenient, but it conceals more than it reveals. Tariff incidence in a market with inelastic demand and concentrated supply doesn’t distribute evenly across the value chain; it falls almost entirely on the buyer. US utilities consuming roughly 50 million pounds annually while producing only a couple of million domestically have no meaningful ability to substitute away from Canadian supply, which accounts for 36% of imports.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!sixa!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6dfb92d7-2f37-458b-a108-3bf8d45fd18f_1405x789.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!sixa!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6dfb92d7-2f37-458b-a108-3bf8d45fd18f_1405x789.png)

But the second-order effects matter more than the headline cost. First, tariffs risk creating a bifurcated market. US-delivered uranium trades at a tariff premium while rest-of-world pricing reflects the underlying supply-demand balance. If Canadian producers can sell non-US pounds at a lower effective price while US utilities pay inflated rates, the “global” spot price becomes a less meaningful signal. This matters for the thesis because SPUT buys globally, but the supply deficit is most acute in the US fuel cycle specifically.

Second, the political economy of tariffs interacts dangerously with nuclear’s support base. Nuclear power’s current bipartisan popularity in the US is broad but shallow; it hasn’t been tested against consumer sticker shock. If tariffs layer a 10-25% cost increase onto electricity from nuclear plants at the same moment that ratepayers are absorbing higher energy costs from AI data center buildouts, the political backlash could be swift. Americans are broadly pro-nuclear in the abstract. Whether they remain so when their power bills reflect the full cost of fuel supply chain restructuring is an open question.

Third, and this is the scenario I find most concerning, tariffs interact with the enrichment bottleneck. If tariffs apply to Canadian uranium while Russian enrichment becomes progressively unavailable, US utilities face a simultaneous cost shock on raw material and a physical shortage of enrichment services. That’s a pincer that no policy aspiration resolves in the near term. The result could be reactor curtailments not because fuel doesn’t exist globally, but because the US-delivered cost stack becomes untenable for specific utility operators.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!vzBC!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fcfa1b075-523a-406e-8cc4-d8658d4ee9db_1403x790.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!vzBC!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fcfa1b075-523a-406e-8cc4-d8658d4ee9db_1403x790.png)

I want to be precise about what the tariff risk does and does not do to the investment thesis. It does not reduce global uranium demand; those reactors still need fuel regardless of who pays the tariff. It does compress the margin that US utilities earn on nuclear generation, which could slow life extensions and reduce contracting urgency for price-sensitive buyers. And it creates a near-term political vulnerability for nuclear energy at precisely the moment when policy support is most critical for long-term demand growth. The risk isn’t that tariffs kill the uranium bull case. It’s that they complicate the smooth, consensus-friendly version of it.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Pp3m!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdd0451ae-b693-4041-b2f1-a89223a38d24_1405x497.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Pp3m!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdd0451ae-b693-4041-b2f1-a89223a38d24_1405x497.png)

## Marginal Supply Response: The Long-Run Ceiling

At $150+ per pound, new supply sources become economic. Uranium recovery from phosphoric acid during fertilizer production has been demonstrated at industrial scale and is viable at $44-66 per pound using solvent extraction (per Kim et al., 2016, published in Resources Policy), with newer ion-exchange processes potentially bringing costs to $33-54 per pound. US phosphoric acid production alone could yield roughly 5.5 million pounds per year, more than current domestic mine production.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mTdy!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdef65e19-5ce6-4f2f-aad9-081baa006b37_1402x594.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mTdy!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdef65e19-5ce6-4f2f-aad9-081baa006b37_1402x594.png)

Longer-term unconventional sources like seawater extraction remain decades from commercial viability despite recent lab breakthroughs, and are irrelevant to the near-to-medium-term supply picture.

These marginal sources cannot scale before 2030, which is precisely when the structural deficit becomes acute. But commodity prices above marginal cost of production have a way of calling forth supply that nobody expected. The uranium market has a history of underestimating supply response (see Kazakhstan’s ISR revolution in the 2000s). At $150-200/lb sustained for several years, the phosphate byproduct pathway could add 10-15 million pounds globally. That’s not enough to solve the deficit, but it would cap the upside in the $150-200 range rather than allowing a runaway spike to $300+.

## Demand Destruction From Unexpected Sources

This is the bear case that gets the least attention and deserves the most. What if global nuclear demand projections are simply wrong?

China has the world’s most aggressive nuclear build program, but China has also reversed course on major infrastructure programs before. If China’s economic growth slows further, if their domestic renewable buildout proves sufficient for baseload needs, or if a political decision shifts priorities, the demand projections that underpin every supply-demand model in the sector would need to be revised dramatically. China alone accounts for a massive share of projected demand growth through 2040.

Similarly, the WNA’s 1,428-1,446 GWe projection for 2050 (from their World Nuclear Outlook Report 2025) is explicitly conditioned on government targets being met, operations being extended to 80 years, and all planned, proposed, and potential reactors being built. The WNA itself notes that 542 GWe of the target capacity “is not yet supported by identified projects.” The US target of 400 GWe by 2050 would require building more capacity in five years than the entire world built during the 1980s peak. This is possible. It is not probable at face value.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!NbG8!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fea76cc45-05a5-41d6-a2d6-2aa81c0a1b98_1402x785.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!NbG8!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fea76cc45-05a5-41d6-a2d6-2aa81c0a1b98_1402x785.png)

A more sober assessment might use the WNA’s Nuclear Fuel Report reference scenario, which projects 746 GWe by 2040 (still a near-doubling from today), or even the IEA’s Current Policies Scenario of 728 GWe by 2050. These are still extremely bullish for uranium prices, but they produce a different magnitude of deficit than the headline numbers suggest.

I mention this not because I think the bull thesis is wrong, but because intellectual honesty requires distinguishing between what is likely and what is the maximum possible. The uranium supply deficit exists under almost any reasonable demand scenario. The question is whether it’s a 15% deficit or a 40% deficit, and that difference matters enormously for how high prices go and how long they stay elevated.

# The Structural Deficit Nobody Can Fix in Time

Here’s where I stop hedging and tell you what I actually think.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!PASX!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0731ff60-d1e5-4a67-8d1f-d82f3841cb65_1405x789.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!PASX!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0731ff60-d1e5-4a67-8d1f-d82f3841cb65_1405x789.png)

The WNA’s Nuclear Fuel Report (2025 edition) projects global uranium requirements rising from roughly 68,920 tonnes in 2025 to over 150,000 tonnes by 2040 under the reference scenario, and over 204,000 tonnes in the upper scenario. Goldman Sachs’ global supply-demand model, updated in their December 2024 “Nuclear Nuggets” report by strategist Brian Lee, projects a cumulative deficit of 1.914 billion pounds through 2045 (revised upward by 211 million pounds from the prior estimate), representing approximately 32% cumulative undersupply. That model incorporates 80-year reactor lifetime assumptions, forward-looking capacity factors for each reactor, and new builds in Russia and the US. A separate, more conservative Goldman coverage note from their August 2025 UEC initiation estimated the annual deficit growing from 20 million pounds in 2025 to 130 million pounds by 2040, a 40% gap versus demand. The two figures use different methodologies and coverage universes, but both point in the same direction: the gap is large, widening, and accelerating.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!gLuQ!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4c0eb905-c4fa-4618-a997-0e9187e8cdeb_1405x767.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!gLuQ!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4c0eb905-c4fa-4618-a997-0e9187e8cdeb_1405x767.png)

The marginal cost of new uranium production sits at $90-100 per pound. The greenfield incentive price, the price at which a miner can justify building a new mine from scratch, is $117-130 per pound at minimum. NexGen’s Arrow deposit, the single most important uranium development project in the world, realistically doesn’t produce until 2032-2033. Existing mine output is expected to halve between 2030 and 2040.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Seeo!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc5426e04-72cd-4334-8a23-40975e0fec38_1405x789.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Seeo!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc5426e04-72cd-4334-8a23-40975e0fec38_1405x789.png)

Every single secondary supply buffer is shrinking simultaneously while demand growth accelerates. This doesn’t resolve with a press conference or a policy announcement. It resolves with dramatically higher prices sustained for years, potentially decades.

## The Punchline

For context, the 1976 uranium peak was approximately $225 per pound in today’s dollars. The 2007 peak was roughly $210 adjusted. Today’s term price of $88 per pound exists in what Huhn correctly identifies as a “much more favorable demand environment” than either previous cycle.

The question is not whether uranium prices are going higher. The question is how high, and whether the mining industry can respond fast enough to prevent a genuine fuel supply crisis at a moment when the world has collectively decided that nuclear power is the answer to both climate change and AI’s insatiable electricity demand.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!gcFP!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1900c5a1-90ab-42ed-bb88-f13e3490c560_1402x711.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!gcFP!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1900c5a1-90ab-42ed-bb88-f13e3490c560_1402x711.png)

I don’t think it can. And I think the equities, particularly the developers sitting on permitted, high-grade deposits, have not yet priced in the reality that the term price may need to reach $130-150 per pound just to incentivize the supply the world needs.

But I want to be honest about what I don’t know. I don’t know whether the enrichment bottleneck breaks the fuel cycle before prices can fully express the supply deficit. I don’t know whether Big Tech’s nuclear ambitions survive their first encounter with NRC permitting timelines. I don’t know whether China’s demand projections hold through the next economic cycle. And I don’t know whether a black swan, whether accident, geopolitical shock, or technological disruption, reshapes the landscape before the thesis plays out.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!WZ-L!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc0e02026-25b9-491c-8cdf-e0eeae7c9292_1405x789.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!WZ-L!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc0e02026-25b9-491c-8cdf-e0eeae7c9292_1405x789.png)

What I do know is this: thirteen years below replacement-rate contracting. A marginal price setter sitting on $200 million in cash. The world’s largest producer peaking under new fiscal constraints. A secondary supply buffer that has been systematically drained. Contract ceilings migrating to $150-160 per pound. And a spot market so thin that a few hundred thousand pounds of buying sends it above $100.

This is not a crowded trade in the way that matters. Retail has arrived and will leave. Institutional allocation to uranium equities remains negligible as a percentage of commodity AUM. And the utilities, the buyers whose contracting actually drives term price discovery, haven’t even begun to move with the urgency the data demands. This is the most obvious structural supply deficit in commodities, and the market is still not positioned for it.
