## **The Inelastic Markets Hypothesis (IMH) – Math**

### **The Lie of Liquidity**

If Mike Green is the prophet of the passive doom, Xavier Gabaix and Ralph Koijen are the actuaries of the apocalypse.

Their “Inelastic Markets Hypothesis” (IMH) is the most important piece of financial research in twenty years, and almost nobody on Wall Street understands it because it terrifies them.

The standard “Efficient Market Hypothesis” (EMH) assumes that the demand curve for stocks is flat (perfectly elastic). The theory goes: If Apple trades at $150, and you try to buy it for $150.01, millions of sellers should theoretically emerge to arb away that discrepancy. The market is viewed as a deep, limitless ocean of liquidity.

Gabaix and Koijen prove this is absolute bullshit.

Their research indicates that the market is “macro inelastic.” The demand curve isn’t flat; it’s steep. Vertical. “In a seminal paper, Xavier Gabaix and Ralph Koijen introduced ‘The Inelastic Markets Hypothesis,’ in which they proposed that the multiplier could be as high as 5”.

### **The Multiplier from Hell: M=5**

Let’s break down the math of this nightmare. **Multiplier = 5**.

This means that if a buyer wants to purchase 10% of the stock market (in dollar terms), prices don’t go up by 0.5% or 1%. They go up by **50%**.

Conversely, and this is the part that should make you wet the bed: **If someone needs to sell $1 billion worth of stock, they don’t destroy $1 billion of market cap. They destroy $5 billion.**

Why? Because “institutional funds are often constrained, i.e. have pretty firm positions (be it 100% equity or 70% equity)”. A pension fund mandated to hold 60% equities *cannot* sell just because prices are high. An index fund *cannot* sell, period. There are “not enough arbitrageurs to supply you with the $1 of stocks you wish”. The arbitrage capital, the smart money, is a thimble of water in a desert of mandated, mindless passive money.

### **The “Free Money” Trap**

The XIV was an Exchange Traded Note designed to short volatility. It was the “free money” machine of the post-GFC era. As markets rose and volatility fell, XIV went up. It was the ultimate “carry trade” for the retail bro who thought he was a hedge fund manager.

But the product had a fatal flaw: it had to rebalance its exposure at the end of every day to maintain its inverse leverage. If the VIX went up, XIV had to buy VIX futures to reduce its short exposure.

This is the exact same logic as a passive fund rebalancing, just on a faster timescale. It is a mechanical, non-thinking instruction: “If X happens, do Y.”

### **Feedback Loop**

On that fateful day, the VIX spiked. This meant XIV had to buy VIX futures. But everyone knew this. The market is predatory.

“Given the rise in the VIX earlier in the day, market participants could expect leveraged long volatility ETPs to rebalance... They also knew that inverse volatility ETPs would have to buy VIX futures to cover the losses”.

The sharks smelled blood. “There were signs that other market participants began bidding up VIX futures prices at around 15:30 in anticipation of the end-of-day rebalancing”.

It was a slaughter. The sharks bought futures, driving the price up. This forced the XIV algorithm to buy *more* futures to rebalance, which drove the price up *further*, which forced XIV to buy *even more*.

### **4:15 PM: The Kill Shot**

In one minute at 16:08, “transaction data show a spike in trading volume to 115,862 VIX futures contracts... at highly inflated prices”.

This was the death blow. The feedback loop spun out of control. “The value of one of the inverse volatility ETPs, XIV, fell 84% and the product was subsequently terminated”.

The fund went to zero effectively overnight. Billions of dollars of “wealth” evaporated because of a mechanical flaw in the rebalancing logic.

### **The Lesson Ignored: SVIX and the Next Bomb**

Did we learn anything? Of course not. We just reinvented the same bomb with a different name.

The SVIX (a newer short vol product) explicitly warns that “SVIX can lose all or most of its value, as XIV and SVXY did during the ‘volmageddon’ in 2018”. Yet, investors pile back in.

# Modelling The Imminent Collapse

Boring for some, inspirational for others. See stated limitations before criticising.

First lets get some things out of the way.

## What Cannot Be Proven Mathematically

The following claims, while central to IMH applications, are empirical hypotheses rather than

mathematical theorems:

• That markets actually operate according to this aggregated elasticity model

• That the estimated M » 5 is stable across time, sectors, or market conditions

• That collapse scenarios will manifest as the model specifies

• That ‘passive’ investors are genuinely price-insensitive (rather than heterogeneous)

• That the fundamental value V is knowable or exogenous

## STATED LIMITATIONS

### The M » 5 Parameter Problem

Gabaix and Koijen’s M » 5 estimate derives from specific identification strategies applied to 2010s US equity markets. This estimate:

• May not generalize to other market caps (small caps likely have different M)

• May vary with market conditions (bull vs. bear regimes)

• Is sensitive to the measurement window and econometric specification

• Conflates multiple types of ‘active’ investors with different elasticities

### The Heterogeneity of ‘Passive

The framework treats passive capital as monolithically price-insensitive. In reality, ‘passive’ encompasses:

• Pure index funds (truly passive, but rebalance at discrete intervals)

• ETFs (subject to creation/redemption arbitrage, providing some elasticity)

• Target-date funds (mechanically adjust allocations, potentially countercyclical)

• Pension funds with rebalancing mandates (often countercyclical)

• Corporate buybacks (price-sensitive, but often misclassified)

Many ‘passive’ strategies are actually stabilizing, not destabilizing. The model’s binary classification obscures this heterogeneity.

### Missing Policy Response

This simulation does not model central bank intervention. The Fed’s actions in March 2020

fundamentally altered the collapse trajectory. Any realistic scenario analysis must acknowledge that policy response functions exist but are not modeled here. The simulated collapses represent trajectories absent intervention, not predictions of realized outcomes.

### Fundamental Value (V) Assumption

The model assumes V is exogenous and knowable. In practice, V is contested, endogenous to market prices (reflexivity), and depends on discount rates that are themselves market-determined. The ‘price deviation from fundamentals’ in our simulation is therefore a modeling convenience, not an observable quantity.

#### **The XIV “Death Loop” Timeline (Feb 5, 2018)**

The mechanics of the XIV collapse serve as the Rosetta Stone for the next crash.

1. **3:30 PM:** VIX futures rise. XIV (Inverse VIX) drops in value.
2. **3:50 PM:** Traders anticipate XIV *must* buy VIX futures at 4:15 PM to rebalance. They front-run the trade, buying futures, pushing the VIX higher.
3. **4:00 PM:** Market closes. XIV NAV is calculated. It is massively under-hedged.
4. **4:05 PM - 4:15 PM:** XIV algorithm blindly buys VIX futures at any price.
5. **Result:** VIX futures double. XIV value drops 84%. Fund liquidates.

*Insight:* Passive ETFs rebalance daily or monthly. If the market drops 10% in a day, leveraged ETFs and certain risk-parity funds face the exact same “Death Loop.”

#### **The Private Equity “Logjam”**

The $3.2 trillion unsold asset figure is not just a number; it is a systemic blockage.

> **The Aging Inventory:** “26% of that is four years old or older”. In PE land, an asset older than 5 years is “stale.” An asset older than 7 years is “dead.”
>
> **The Distribution Crisis:** LPs operate on a cash-in, cash-out model. If GPs don’t sell (distribute), LPs can’t commit to new funds. This is why fundraising is down.
>
> **The Renovo Reality:** Renovo represents the “mark-to-myth” colliding with reality. You can mark a roll-up at 15x EBITDA on your books, but if the bank cuts your line of credit and you file Chapter 7, the mark is zero. The loss of $100M-$500M in liabilities with essentially zero assets is a total wipeout of capital that was likely marked as “stable” months prior.
