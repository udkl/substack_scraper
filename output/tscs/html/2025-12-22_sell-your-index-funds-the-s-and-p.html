The Inelastic Markets Hypothesis (IMH) – Math

The Lie of Liquidity

If Mike Green is the prophet of the passive doom, Xavier Gabaix and Ralph Koijen are the actuaries of the apocalypse.

Their “Inelastic Markets Hypothesis” (IMH) is the most important piece of financial research in twenty years, and almost nobody on Wall Street understands it because it terrifies them.

The standard “Efficient Market Hypothesis” (EMH) assumes that the demand curve for stocks is flat (perfectly elastic). The theory goes: If Apple trades at $150, and you try to buy it for $150.01, millions of sellers should theoretically emerge to arb away that discrepancy. The market is viewed as a deep, limitless ocean of liquidity.

Gabaix and Koijen prove this is absolute bullshit.

Their research indicates that the market is “macro inelastic.” The demand curve isn’t flat; it’s steep. Vertical. “In a seminal paper, Xavier Gabaix and Ralph Koijen introduced ‘The Inelastic Markets Hypothesis,’ in which they proposed that the multiplier could be as high as 5”.

The Multiplier from Hell: M=5

Let’s break down the math of this nightmare. Multiplier = 5.

This means that if a buyer wants to purchase 10% of the stock market (in dollar terms), prices don’t go up by 0.5% or 1%. They go up by 50%.

Conversely, and this is the part that should make you wet the bed: If someone needs to sell $1 billion worth of stock, they don’t destroy $1 billion of market cap. They destroy $5 billion.

Why? Because “institutional funds are often constrained, i.e. have pretty firm positions (be it 100% equity or 70% equity)”. A pension fund mandated to hold 60% equities cannot sell just because prices are high. An index fund cannot sell, period. There are “not enough arbitrageurs to supply you with the $1 of stocks you wish”. The arbitrage capital, the smart money, is a thimble of water in a desert of mandated, mindless passive money.

Keep in mind this is an extreme.

Flows are the Only Thing That Matters

This implies that “roughly over one third of all stock market fluctuations are driven by capital flows” rather than news, earnings, or fundamentals.

The “dark matter” of asset pricing, the mysterious movements that fundamental analysts can’t explain, is just flows hitting an inelastic wall.

When you see the market rip 2% higher on a Tuesday for no reason, it’s not because the “market expects better earnings.” It’s because a massive pension fund rebalanced, or a foreign sovereign wealth fund dumped cash into the system, and the inelastic multiplier (M=5) magnified that flow into a hallucination of wealth.

When flows are positive, this M=5 multiplier creates a euphoric “wealth effect.” A small amount of buying creates a massive amount of paper wealth. It feels like magic. It feels like we are all geniuses.

But it works in reverse.

If flows turn negative, if the Boomers start selling their 401(k)s to pay for nursing homes, or if a recession forces liquidations, or if the Fed tightens too hard, the market will crash 5x harder than the fundamentals justify.

STATED LIMITATIONS

The M » 5 Parameter Problem

Gabaix and Koijen’s M » 5 estimate derives from specific identification strategies applied to 2010s US equity markets. This estimate:

• May not generalize to other market caps (small caps likely have different M)

• May vary with market conditions (bull vs. bear regimes)

• Is sensitive to the measurement window and econometric specification

• Conflates multiple types of ‘active’ investors with different elasticities

The Heterogeneity of ‘Passive

The framework treats passive capital as monolithically price-insensitive. In reality, ‘passive’ encompasses:

• Pure index funds (truly passive, but rebalance at discrete intervals)

• ETFs (subject to creation/redemption arbitrage, providing some elasticity)

• Target-date funds (mechanically adjust allocations, potentially countercyclical)

• Pension funds with rebalancing mandates (often countercyclical)

• Corporate buybacks (price-sensitive, but often misclassified)

Many ‘passive’ strategies are actually stabilizing, not destabilizing. The model’s binary classification obscures this heterogeneity.

Missing Policy Response

This simulation does not model central bank intervention. The Fed’s actions in March 2020

fundamentally altered the collapse trajectory. Any realistic scenario analysis must acknowledge that policy response functions exist but are not modeled here. The simulated collapses represent trajectories absent intervention, not predictions of realized outcomes.

Fundamental Value (V) Assumption

The model assumes V is exogenous and knowable. In practice, V is contested, endogenous to market prices (reflexivity), and depends on discount rates that are themselves market-determined. The ‘price deviation from fundamentals’ in our simulation is therefore a modeling convenience, not an observable quantity.
