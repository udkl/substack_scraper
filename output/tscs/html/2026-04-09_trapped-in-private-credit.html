Section 2: The Composition Thesis

The severity and duration of the private credit stress cycle depends on a single variable: the composition of the redemption wave.

If it is predominantly wealth-channel (financial advisers pulling clients out of products making headlines for the wrong reasons), the wave is self-limiting. There are a finite number of wealth-channel investors in semi-liquid funds. The gate mechanisms enforce 5% quarterly caps. At those caps, even a 20% peak request rate clears in three to five quarters. That is the iCapital estimate, published April 2, and it is consistent with the only available precedent.

If institutional LPs are joining the queue, the timeline extends dramatically and the entire structure fractures, because institutional capital is the permanent base that makes the model work.

A note on OTIC’s headline 40.7% request rate: when wealth advisors know a fund is capping at 5% and prorating requests, they inflate their figures to receive the actual cash they want after proration. Request $10 million when you need $2 million, receive your $2 million at 20% fulfilment. The demand to exit is real. The 40.7% figure includes phantom queue inflation. The fulfilled amount is the cleaner signal.

No fund has disclosed the exact split. Not Blackstone. Not Apollo. Not Ares. Not Blue Owl. This is the most important variable in a $1.8 trillion market and it is completely unobservable from public data.

What we have instead is five convergent data points.

The Goldman signal. Goldman Sachs’ private credit fund saw 4.999% redemption requests versus the industry average of 15%. Goldman attributed it to institutional capital. Alex Blostein’s March 19 research note projected $50 to $70 billion in net outflows from the retail channel over the next two years. Retail credit fund assets grew from $34 billion at end of 2021 to $222 billion at end of 2025. That $188 billion delta is the pool that is draining.

Blostein also pushed back on the catastrophist framing: “Credit is credit. It’s not equity.” A 30% drawdown in public software stocks does not translate to equivalent lender losses given 60 to 70% equity cushions underneath first-lien loans. Goldman’s own non-accrual rate: 12 basis points. Not 12%. Twelve basis points.

The manager commentary. Jon Gray at Blackstone described Q1 redemptions as driven by “retail and wealth-management clients,” noting “financial advisors can say, hey, I want to redeem.” Blue Owl stated OTIC’s pressure was “amplified by fund’s more concentrated shareholder base, particularly within certain wealth channels and regions,” citing the Asian wealth channel specifically.

The institutional behaviour. CalPERS deployed roughly $6 billion in new private credit commitments in Q3 2025 and is increasing its target allocation from 5% to 8%. No pension fund, endowment, or sovereign wealth fund has publicly disclosed redeeming. European bank CEOs at Morgan Stanley’s financials conference expressed comfort with private credit exposure and see no systemic risk.

The inflow data. iCapital noted approximately $7 billion in gross inflows into larger private credit funds during Q1, which “could indicate consistent institutional funding.” Seven billion dollars of institutional money walking in while $13.9 billion of wealth-channel money tries to walk out is a compositional picture, not a systemic one.

The BREIT precedent. Blackstone’s non-traded real estate fund faced a nearly identical redemption crisis starting November 2022. Peak requests hit $5.3 billion in January 2023. Blackstone continuously hit caps. Total withdrawals: $14.3 billion over 15 months. By February 2024, requests had dropped 82% from peak, and Blackstone fulfilled 100% for the first time.

Fifteen months from first gate to full resolution. Entirely wealth-channel driven. The precedent is imperfect. BREIT was backed by real estate with observable liquidation value and transaction comps. Software credit has no comparable floor, as the distressed practitioner data in Section 3 confirms. If the tail scenario materialises, the resolution timeline could run longer than 15 months.

If the BREIT base rate holds and Q1 2026 is month one, the resolution window is Q2 to Q3 2027. The iCapital estimate of three to five quarters aligns.

I hold this compositional view at roughly 65% confidence. It moves to 80% if BCRED Q2 shows channel-level disclosure with institutional requests below 2%. It drops to 40% if Blackstone stops distinguishing between channels in its Q2 communications, because that silence is itself the answer. The 35% where I am wrong is the scenario where institutional LPs begin queuing in Q2 or Q3, the permanent capital base fractures, and the 15-month timeline doubles.

This is the bet. Not whether the signals are adverse (they are), but whether the adversity has a shelf life.

Section 3: The Evidence Against Me

That is the bull case. Here is why it might be wrong.

The opacity is worse than I told you. Barclays published research showing actual software exposure across four large managers (Apollo, Ares, Blackstone, Blue Owl) is approximately 25%, versus the 19% they report. The gap is structural: “software” is sliced into subcategories that individually stay below concentration limits but aggregate higher. Blue Owl’s OCIC prospectus limits any single industry to 20%. Actual software-plus-technology exposure: “just over 30%.” The limit is observed in letter but violated in spirit. The BIS quantified the scale on March 16. Outstanding loans to SaaS firms: $8 billion in 2015. Over $500 billion by end of 2025. That is 19% of total direct loans.

A third of private credit funds have extended loans to software companies. BDCs with higher SaaS exposure underperformed peers by 5 percentage points. No US regulator has produced anything comparable. No US regulator has taken any enforcement action on valuation practices. The DOJ warned about “creative marks” in November. Since then: zero subpoenas, zero indictments, zero investigations. Warren has issued statements. Nobody has issued consequences.

Jamie Dimon, in his April 7 shareholder letter: “At some point insurance regulators will insist on more rigorous ratings or markdowns, which will likely lead to demands for more capital.” The CEO of the largest bank in America is telling you the marks are coming down.

The question is when, not whether. And Boaz Weinstein, on the Matt Levine podcast on March 19, warned that falling NAVs force asset sales that trigger more redemptions: “I really think this could be a systemic nightmare.” The regulatory cavalry is not coming. The loop has to exhaust itself.

The FSK downgrade is a new category of stress. Moody’s cut FS KKR from Baa3 to Ba1 on March 23: the first BDC downgraded to junk in this cycle. PIK income at 14.7% of investment income versus a 6.3% peer median. Non-accruals at 5.5%. Q4 net loss of $114 million. NAV eroded 11.7% over the full year. Software: 16.4% of the portfolio.

iCapital identifies the 10% PIK threshold as the critical marker: “if PIK income exceeds 10% of total income, the portfolio could face liquidity stress.” FSK blew through it. PSEC, at approximately 12.5%, is next in line. The mechanism is lethal: BDCs are Regulated Investment Companies required to distribute 90% of taxable income as cash dividends. When 14.7% of investment income is PIK (non-cash interest capitalised into the loan principal), the fund books taxable income it never received in cash, but must still pay the dividend. The gap gets funded by drawing on revolvers or selling assets at distressed prices. PIK at this level is not an accounting nuance. It is a cash-flow death spiral.

Sector average: 0.78x book, a 22% discount, up from 15% in Part 3. That is a 126-percentage-point spread between the best and worst managers. The market is differentiating. But it is also widening the discount across the board, and that widening compresses the fee base that funds the entire alternative asset management industry.

The software loan market froze. Reuters reported in late February that “there are currently no leveraged loan deals for software companies.” Zero. Deutsche Bank was stuck holding $1.2 billion in loans backing Conga Corp. March 2026 was the first month without a single loan repricing since June 2023. When the primary market shuts entirely, every borrower approaching maturity faces a binary: restructure or default. And 48 software instruments in the Part 4 dataset mature by 2028.

But the secondary market found a floor. I pulled the full 227-loan software term loan dataset from the Bloomberg Terminal on April 8. A clarification on taxonomy: these are Broadly Syndicated Loans, not direct lending. They carry CUSIPs, trade on screens, and have observable secondary bids. True private credit is bilaterally negotiated, untraded, and marked-to-model. We use the liquid BSL software market as the real-time canary for what the opaque private marks are hiding. The weighted average bid: 86.42. In Part 4, using the same universe three weeks earlier, it was 86.46. The average spread: 417.3 basis points, versus 417 in Part 4. The credit market has stabilised while the equity market kept falling.

Two recovered, two deteriorated, one flat. That is not a uniform collapse. Barracuda’s 5-point recovery from 57.81 to 63.25 is the strongest single-name improvement in the distressed cohort. One data point that the bears should find uncomfortable: BCRED’s Class I NAV was $24.38 as of February 27, per Bloomberg. That is an all-time low. It is also the last available data point, recorded the day before Operation Epic Fury began. Whatever happened to the portfolio in March, when BIZD hit new lows, when industry-wide gating erupted, when FSK was downgraded to junk, is not yet in this number. The March NAV, when it is eventually disclosed around Blackstone’s April 16 to 17 earnings, will almost certainly be lower. The $400 million capital injection went to fund redemptions, not to support NAV.

And Golub’s $600 million in unsecured notes at 2.5%, due August 2026, shows no refinancing on Bloomberg’s debt distribution as of April 8. Eight issues outstanding, $3.6 billion total debt, weighted average coupon of 4.86%. That $600 million maturity is four months away with no replacement announced. Based on recent BDC refinancings (ARCC at 5.25%, GSBD at 5.10%, FSK at 6.13%), Golub is looking at roughly doubling its coupon cost. That is $15 to $20 million in additional annual interest expense on a single maturity.

The CLO cushion is thinning. PGIM’s March 26 report: average BB CLO MVOC fell 1.2 points to 104%, a 4% cushion. Twenty-three percent of BB tranches now have thin cushions below 3%, doubled in 2026. Roughly 13% of BB tranches show MVOC below zero. Clopremium found 22.6% of US BSL CLO BB tranches show MVOC below 100%. No manager has publicly reported a software-specific OC test breach, the smoking gun I flagged in Part 4. The Q1 CLO trustee reports, due May and June, will tell us whether it has fired.

The carry breakeven is thinner than I told you. All-in yields have compressed from 10 to 12% (Part 3) to 8.5 to 9.7%. Meanwhile BDCs are refinancing 2 to 3% coupons into 5.1 to 6.1%, with the weakest (ICMB) requiring an affiliate backstop at roughly 9.5% because public markets would not lend to them.

The 10-year has risen 15 basis points in a bear flattening that compresses the carry from both sides. And Fitch’s default composition tells you the headline numbers are understating the stress: 60% PIK conversions, 27% maturity extensions, 6% actual payment defaults. Morningstar DBRS confirms the pattern independently: 94% of all downgrades to D or SD in the 12 months through February were distressed exchanges. The marks say performing. The cash register says empty.

The buffer exists. It is meaningfully narrower. And the rate-cut scenario that would have eased it is priced out: CME FedWatch shows 83% hold at May, 89% at June.

A distressed practitioner told me the breakeven may be worse than the table shows. A board member of the Turnaround Management Association with direct operational experience in mid-market software restructurings, who asked to remain anonymous, provided recovery data from two recent restructurings sourced through an investment banker. In the preferred conversion structure, the lender took a 60% haircut with that portion rolling into preferred equity. In the common conversion, roughly a 20% discount with some preferred and 15% common at the expense of the sponsor. The recovery range he is seeing across current restructurings: 40 cents to 80 cents, depending on the structure. The 40-cent figure sits right at my tighter breakeven scenario.

This is the insight that rewrites the recovery math for software credit.

In traditional restructuring, you weigh the restructuring path against liquidation value. In manufacturing or real assets, that floor is tangible: plant, equipment, inventory. In software 20 years ago, the IP itself stored value because it took months or years to build. Today, with AI, you can develop a comparable software application almost overnight. The IP stores almost no residual value. The only real value left is the going concern: the ARR, the customer base, the switching costs. And that is the exact asset under pressure from AI-driven budget reallocation.

His words: “This is something the restructuring industry has never really encountered before.”

He estimates 20 to 30% of the distressed software loan pool are genuine zombies: negative loan-to-value, no realistic path to exit at par. Roughly 50% of 2026 year-to-date software defaults are repeat offenders, companies where the first restructuring did not cut deep enough. Skillsoft, bidding at 39.08 on the Bloomberg Terminal as of April 8, is the template: this is a company that already went through Chapter 11 in 2020, emerged, got re-levered, and is now trading at levels that suggest the lenders will end up owning it again. Owning a software company whose competitive moat is being eroded by the same AI that caused the loan to go bad is not a recovery. It is a consolation prize.

This directly challenges the 30-cent recovery assumption I used for the bottom tier of the breakeven table. If liquidation value in software is approaching zero for the Dead Zone credits, the tail risk in CLO and BDC portfolios is worse than my table shows. It also widens the Fortress Zone versus Dead Zone gap even further, because the Fortress names are the ones whose customer lock-in, regulatory moats, and institutional switching costs constitute genuine going-concern value. That value is real. The question is whether it is enough to carry the entire portfolio when the Dead Zone floor has collapsed underneath it.

Gundlach on March 23: “Everything is overvalued, cracks are starting to form. But everyone’s like, it’s all contained, it’s no problem, it’s just software. But it’s not just software.”

He might be right. The compositional evidence says he is probably wrong, for now, for the wealth-channel portion of the stress. But “probably” is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and 65% confidence means I am wrong one time in three.

That is the bear case. I think it is a timing problem, not a terminal one. Here is why.
