Four of five major supermarkets in South Korea sold out of waste disposal bags by March 24.

Taiwan's Costco stores imposed purchase limits on plastic wrap and containers. South Korea's government designated seven petrochemical feedstock items as "crisis items" under their Supply Chain Stabilization Act, a law most Koreans didn't know existed until their trash bags disappeared.

The commodity behind this isn't oil. It's polyethylene: the plastic in the bags under your sink, the lining inside your cereal box, the caps on your water bottles. The most produced polymer on earth.

About a fifth of the world's capacity to make it has physically shut down. Another chunk is running at emergency minimums. The effective output loss is worse than either number suggests.

I've been watching one chart for five weeks. Not Brent (everyone has Brent up). Not the tanker queue (430 ships and counting, sitting in the Gulf like the world's most expensive parking lot).

Not the ceasefire, which collapsed on April 12, roughly four days after markets celebrated it. The chart is the US-Asia polyethylene spread. In February it was around $500 a metric tonne. Normal. Boring. The kind of number that lives in a corner of a commodity desk and never makes it onto CNBC. As of late March, it blew past $1,200.

Oil gets the Bloomberg real estate, the Goldman notes, and the cable news chyrons. The real story is underneath: a petrochemical feedstock chokepoint that is already showing up on shelves in Seoul while American analysts are still arguing about Brent. And the part that really has my attention isn't the shortage.

Shortages end. What I'm watching is the crackers that shut down and whether any of them ever come back.

The two-speed world

Ethylene, the molecule at the base of the petrochemical chain, comes from two completely different feedstocks depending on where you are.

In the US, roughly 80-85% of ethylene production runs on ethane. Ethane comes from natural gas processing. It gets piped in from the Permian Basin and the Marcellus Shale. None of it touches a tanker. None of it goes near the Strait of Hormuz. US ethane production hit a record 2.8 million barrels per day in 2024, and the Permian alone accounts for 63% of it.

In Asia, in Europe, in most of the rest of the world, ethylene comes from naphtha. Naphtha is a crude oil derivative. And a staggering amount of the world’s naphtha supply transits the Strait of Hormuz. South Korea imports 77% of its naphtha from the Middle East. Japan imports over 60%, with 70-74% of that from the Gulf.

So when Hormuz closes, the US petrochemical complex barely notices. American crackers keep running on domestic ethane at $200-350 per tonne. Meanwhile, Asian naphtha-based production costs surge from around $620 to above $1,000 per tonne. The spread between the two doesn’t just widen. It detonates.

Jim Fitterling, Dow’s CEO, stood up at CERAWeek on March 26 and said something that should have gotten more attention than it did. The integrated margin spread between US ethane-based production and Asian naphtha-based production, normally under $500 a tonne, had blown past $1,200. Every Gulf Coast asset Dow owns was “running flat out.” Port Houston saw a 12% surge in export volumes within two weeks.

The US petrochemical complex is the beneficiary, not the victim. The disruption is splitting the world in half along a feedstock line that most investors have never looked at.

The other half is in serious trouble.

LG Chem, South Korea’s largest chemical company, cut all naphtha-fed crackers to 60% utilization. That’s their floor. The operational minimum. Below that, you shut down entirely. Yeochun NCC declared force majeure on March 4 and hasn’t lifted it. The South Korean government banned naphtha exports, designated it an “economic security item,” and started negotiating emergency supply deals that would have been unthinkable six months ago.

How unthinkable? In late March, LG Chem bought 27,000 metric tonnes of Russian naphtha. South Korea’s first Russian naphtha import in roughly four years. This wasn’t a sanctions breach. The US Treasury issued a specific waiver (OFAC General License 134A), authorized by Secretary Bessent, running from March 12 to April 11. South Korea’s Director General for Industrial Resource Security, Yang Ki-wook, confirmed they “communicated with the U.S. Treasury Department and received confirmation.” Payment went through in yuan, rubles, and dirhams.

That waiver expired on April 11. No extension has been officially announced. Reuters reported Trump and Bessent agreed it was “a good idea” to extend, but the blockade declaration complicates everything.

A US ally, importing Russian hydrocarbons under a US emergency waiver, paying in non-dollar currencies, to keep its chemical plants running at 60% instead of zero. Six months ago this would have read as fiction.

Japan is in similar shape. Twelve ethylene plants nationwide, overwhelmingly naphtha-fed, importing over 60% of naphtha with 70-74% from the Middle East. Half of Japan’s ethylene plants cut production within two weeks. Mitsubishi Chemical, Mitsui Chemicals, Sumitomo, Tosoh, Idemitsu Kosan: all announced cuts.

China’s exposure is mixed, which matters. Naphtha cracking still dominates at about 69%, but coal-to-olefins (CTO) and methanol-to-olefins (MTO) together provide roughly 16% of Chinese capacity. These are not equally insulated. CTO plants convert domestic coal directly to olefins; they are genuinely insulated from Hormuz and running at full rates. MTO plants convert methanol to olefins, and China imports significant methanol volumes from the Gulf (Saudi Arabia, Oman, UAE). MTO is partially Hormuz-exposed, a point that matters when you reach the Kill Zone scenario below, where Mitsubishi and Ar-Razi have already declared force majeure on methanol. The truly insulated capacity is narrower than the 16% headline suggests. The economics for CTO are extraordinary right now: $557/tonne versus $1,348 for naphtha cracking.

But CTO can offset maybe 10-15% of the global shortfall. Helpful. Not sufficient. And some Chinese naphtha facilities have shut anyway: Shell-CNOOC in Huizhou, Wanhua Chemical declared force majeure.

The cascade

Thirty-four force majeure declarations in three weeks. One (QatarEnergy, early March) turned into 34 across three continents by mid-March, and the number has continued climbing. The list reads like a roll call of the global chemical industry. Sadara, 1,500 kilotonnes per year of ethylene, the entire Jubail complex shut. Yeochun NCC in South Korea, 2,280 kt. Formosa Petrochemical in Taiwan, 2,930 kt. Shell-CNOOC in Huizhou, China. Chandra Asri in Indonesia. Aster Chemicals in Singapore. Mitsubishi, Mitsui, Sumitomo, Tosoh, and Idemitsu across Japan. LyondellBasell declared force majeure on European polyolefin supply from Rotterdam. (Note: LYB’s Carrington polypropylene plant in the UK, along with sites at Berre, Münchsmünster, and Tarragona, is subject to a sale agreement with Munich-based industrial investor Aequita, announced June 2025 with closing expected in H1 2026. The force majeure status of Carrington depends on whether the transaction has completed; if so, operational responsibility has transferred to Aequita.)

S&P Global’s Walt Hart presented the numbers at the World Petrochemical Conference in Houston on March 26. His colleague Jim Burkhard, S&P Global’s Head of Research, opened with the framing: “The biggest supply disruption in history.” Hart’s projection: 22 million metric tonnes of global ethylene output cut in 2026. That’s about 12% of the 185 million tonnes the world produced in 2025. Twenty-nine million tonnes of ethylene capacity sits inside the conflict zone. Another 7-8 million tonnes of Asian production is lost because the feedstock to run it isn’t arriving.

And those numbers assumed Hormuz would reopen by end of April. The ceasefire collapsed on the 12th. Trump declared a naval blockade on the 12th, with enforcement beginning on the 13th. Hart’s estimate is almost certainly conservative now.

But the shock amplifies as it moves downstream. A roughly 17% crude oil supply loss (Goldman’s Daan Struyven called it “the largest supply shock in history” in his March 22 note, and for once the hyperbole was accurate) doesn’t produce a 17% petrochemical hit. It produces something much worse.

Ethylene crackers have a hard operating floor at about 60-70% utilization. Below that, the heat balance collapses, product separation economics fail, and catalyst performance degrades to the point where you have to shut the whole unit down. LG Chem hit that floor at all its Korean naphtha crackers and held there.

So a feedstock reduction that pushes utilization below threshold doesn’t cause proportional output loss. It causes total output loss. When you combine that with the force majeure cascading (each supplier’s FM triggers their customers’ FM, which triggers their customers’ FM), and the shipping paralysis (430+ stranded vessels that need to clear before new cargoes can move), a 17% crude supply shock propagates into a 19% effective output loss: 30 million tonnes per year of ethylene capacity physically shut down, producing zero, plus another 14 million tonnes per year lost from crackers running at emergency minimums. That’s 44 million tonnes out of 232 million in nameplate capacity (19%), or about 24% of actual 2025 production of 185 million tonnes. But the fragility is wider than the output number: count every facility running at its operational floor, and roughly a third of global capacity is operating abnormally. The output number is what matters for pricing. The abnormal-operations number is what matters for understanding how one more shock propagates.

The recovery clock

This is where the thesis separates from the oil story.

Fitterling, at the same CERAWeek session, said the supply chain unwind could take 250 to 275 days. “The die is being cast for the rest of the year,” as he put it. That number has been the backbone of the recovery thesis since I started working on this piece. Fitterling runs the company that benefits most from an extended disruption. Discount accordingly. But the structural reasoning checks out independently of who said it.

But there’s a strong counterargument I have to engage with before I ask you to take that number seriously. Hurricane Harvey in August 2017 shut down over 50% of US ethylene production. Actual physical damage: flooding, power failures, equipment destruction, chemical releases at more than 40 sites.

The Fed documented that the bulk of the Gulf Coast energy network came back within three weeks. Chevron Phillips’ Cedar Bayou complex was devastated by flooding, declared force majeure, and had no restart timeline as late as mid-September; full operations did not resume until late November or December 2017, pushing the startup of their new 1.5 million tonne cracker into Q1 2018. ExxonMobil’s Baytown and Beaumont chemical plants did not resume normal operations until September 21, roughly four weeks after landfall, not days. Some ethylene force majeures persisted well into Q4 2017, with LyondellBasell’s Corpus Christi cracker restart extending flaring through October and over 50% of US ethylene capacity still offline more than a week after landfall. The refining recovery was faster than the chemical recovery, which actually reinforces the point about petrochemicals sitting further down the restart pecking order.

So why should Hormuz take 250-275 days when Harvey, with real physical damage, saw most facilities back within one to four weeks and the stragglers within a few months?

Because Harvey was local and Hormuz is global. When Harvey flooded the Gulf Coast, every non-Gulf-Coast producer in the world kept running and shipping. Feedstock was available from every other direction. Hormuz removes feedstock for an entire hemisphere simultaneously, with no alternative source at any routing. Crackers are machines; they turn back on. But you can’t turn on a cracker that has nothing to crack. The binding constraints are feedstock availability, logistics sequencing, and permanence.

Logistics first. When Hormuz reopens (partially, through escorted convoys, at reduced throughput), petrochemicals are last in line. Oil goes first. Then LNG. Then LPG. Then jet fuel. Then gasoline. Then, eventually, naphtha, which is itself the primary chemical feedstock for Asian and European crackers. Fitterling said this explicitly. Petrochemicals will be “somewhere down the list.”

There is a prior problem that even oil analysts are underestimating. Reopening the Strait does not instantly restore supply. Production shut-in of 11-13 million barrels per day means those barrels have already been drained from storage globally. Every day of shut-in creates a cumulative deficit that takes months of tanker transit to begin replacing, and the available tanker fleet has to clear a queue of 430+ stranded vessels (300+ of them oil tankers) before it can even start. The barrels are gone; they are not waiting in a pipeline to be turned back on. Chemical cargoes sit behind all of that.

And even once they move, the container dislocation doesn’t self-correct in weeks. Andrew Neale, S&P Global’s VP and Global Head of Chemicals, put it this way at the WPC on March 25: polymer units have been building inventory in yards with nowhere to ship it. The containers that should be circulating between Asian factories and Western distribution centers are in the wrong places. Neale’s estimate: “quarters instead of months to unwind.” The COVID shipping crisis should have taught everyone that. It didn’t.

Then permanence. Some of the infrastructure damage isn’t coming back. QatarEnergy’s Ras Laffan complex took missile hits. The recovery timeline there is 3-5 years. SABIC’s Jubail complex caught missile debris on April 7, and the company hasn’t released a damage assessment. When a company the size of SABIC stays quiet about damage to its flagship complex, the damage is not minor. And the crackers that were already bleeding money before the crisis won’t restart even if feedstock returns. That’s the structural thesis for 2027-2028, and the most contested part of what follows.

Three ways this plays out

I’m running three scenarios

The Snap-Back (15% probability). Diplomacy works. A ceasefire solidifies in late April, escorted convoys start running by mid-May at maybe 15 ships per day (versus 150 normal), and full commercial transit resumes by mid-June. The US-Asia spread decays from $1,200 to $800 by July, to $550 by October, back to something like $450 (pre-crisis normal) by Q1 2027. Inventory rebuild creates a secondary price bump of $100-200/mt above equilibrium through Q4, but the structural story fades. Pre-crisis rationalizations proceed on their existing timelines. No crisis-accelerated closures. BofA’s DeYoe is right. The chemical stocks give back their crisis premiums. The trade in this scenario is to sell the Tier 1 chemical rallies and buy chemical tanker weakness on normalization fears (because SNI.OL and ODF.OL will re-rate once Q2 earnings show the crisis benefit even if the crisis is ending).

I’m giving this 15%. The ceasefire collapsed on April 12. Trump declared a naval blockade later that day, with enforcement beginning April 13. The trajectory is escalation, not resolution. More structurally: every Hormuz crisis in modern history (1987-88, the tanker war; 2019, the Abqaiq attacks) de-escalated because both sides had an economic incentive to keep the strait open. This time Iran’s revenue is already largely sanctions-constrained, and the US is a net energy exporter benefiting from the disruption. The mutual incentive structure that historically de-escalated these crises is weaker than at any point since 1988. But diplomacy is nonlinear and I’ve been surprised before.

The Grind (60% probability). This is the Red Sea template applied to Hormuz. Intermittent ceasefires and breakdowns. Escorted convoys begin in June but sustain only 30-45 ships per day. Iran retains enough leverage to disrupt via drone and mine threats, keeping war-risk premiums at 2-5% of hull value. No full normalization until late Q1 2027 at the earliest.

In this scenario, the US-Asia spread decays to $900 by August, then holds at $700-850 through year-end. It never goes back to $500 because war-risk premiums, longer routing, and insurance costs are embedded in every molecule. Fitterling’s 250-275 day unwind applies in full. Asian converter inventories remain critically low through H2 2026. The restocking wave, when it comes, creates sustained price support of $150-250/mt above pre-crisis equilibrium through mid-2027. Think the 2020-2021 COVID restocking that pushed PE to $1,400-1,600/mt and took 18 months to unwind. Same mechanic, different catalyst.

The rationalization accelerates. Crackers that were “under review” become permanent closures. LyondellBasell’s Berre and Münchsmünster (sale to Aequita closing; buyer may never restart under feedstock stress).

BP Gelsenkirchen (sale to Klesch Group agreed on March 19, 2026; closing expected H2 2026, subject to regulatory approval; Klesch is a refining-focused operator and may not prioritize petchem operations).

Dow Terneuzen (idled since June 2025; indefinite mothball). In Japan, Maruzen Chiba, Idemitsu Chiba, and ENEOS Kawasaki timelines all accelerate from 2027-2028 to 2026-2027. In South Korea, the government-mediated restructuring gets political cover it didn’t have before the crisis.

Total crisis-accelerated permanent exit above pre-crisis baseline: 8-12 million tonnes per year globally. That tightens the 2027-2028 PE supply curve enough to matter.

The trade in this scenario is the core position: long DOW and WLK on pullbacks, long chemical tankers, short packaging consumers with repricing lags, credit shorts on leveraged packaging. The structural rationalization thesis supports the position even if a ceasefire eventually arrives, because permanent capacity exits don’t reverse.

The Kill Zone (25% probability). Escalation continues. Iran retaliates against the US blockade with mine-laying, drone attacks on commercial vessels, or asymmetric strikes on Gulf petrochemical infrastructure. (Iran has reportedly published a targeting list covering 32% of regional olefin supply and 42% of PE capacity.) No commercial transit through December 2026.

The spread goes to $1,500-2,000+. Asian naphtha becomes unavailable at any price. Standalone MTO plants restart but methanol supply itself is partially Hormuz-dependent (Mitsubishi and Ar-Razi in Saudi Arabia already declared force majeure on methanol). Physical scarcity, not price, becomes the binding constraint. South Korea has already begun rationing (the “crisis items” designation under the Supply Chain Stabilization Act is a rationing framework, even if the government isn’t calling it that).

Permanent reshaping of global petrochemical geography. The world splits into two tiers: US/Canadian ethane-based and Chinese coal-based production as structurally advantaged, and everyone else dependent on seaborne feedstock flows through chokepoints that are now permanently repriced for risk. Fifteen to 20 million tonnes per year of permanent capacity exit over 2026-2028.

The combined probability of extended disruption (Grind plus Kill Zone) is 85%. That’s consistent with Fitterling’s 250-275 day estimate and with where the diplomatic trajectory sits today.

The rationalization question (or: is BofA right?)

The strongest argument against the trade I’m about to lay out isn’t that I’m wrong about the disruption. It’s that the disruption doesn’t matter long-term because the market was already rationalizing.

Chemical Week’s January 5, 2026 cover story, published two months before anyone was thinking about Hormuz, was “Petchems rationalization drive rolls on.” European cracker closures were announced throughout 2024 and 2025. ExxonMobil shut Gravenchon. ExxonMobil shut Mossmorran. Versalis closed Porto Marghera, Brindisi, and is converting Priolo. SABIC closed a cracker at Geleen. Dow announced Böhlen for Q4 2027. Total announced Antwerp NC2 for end-2027. Japan announced closures at Maruzen Chiba, Idemitsu Chiba, ENEOS Kawasaki. South Korea signed a voluntary restructuring pact in August 2025 to trim 2.7-3.7 million tonnes per year, roughly 20-28% of its cracker capacity.

Dow was up 40% year-to-date by February 13. Before Hormuz. On nothing but its own “Transform to Outperform” restructuring announcement from January 29.

Citi’s Patrick Cunningham upgraded both Dow and LyondellBasell to Buy on March 12 and raised Dow’s price target to $48 in early April, projecting 2-3 quarters of disruption. RBC upgraded Dow to Outperform on March 9 and subsequently raised its price target to $47 on April 10. Alembic Global upgraded to Overweight on April 9. The bull camp exists and it’s not small.

But DeYoe at BofA has an argument I can’t dismiss. He downgraded Dow to Underperform on April 6 with a $35 price target and LyondellBasell to Underperform at $68. His thesis, in his own words: “little desire to capitalize on material overearning associated with the Iran conflict.” He expects “the conflict to resolve without material asset rationalization.” The crisis premium, in his view, is exactly that: a premium that reverses.

LyondellBasell’s stock told you DeYoe had an audience. It peaked above $83, then drew down roughly 15% to the low $70s after the BofA downgrade landed the same week as the (brief) ceasefire. The market gave back a meaningful share of the crisis move in a week.

Neale, the same guy I’m citing on the recovery timeline (”quarters not months”), also said this: “Longer term, the petchems sector remains an oversupplied market. We’re not going to rationalize our way out of that.” That sentence matters. China alone accounted for 70% of the 40 million tonnes of ethylene capacity added between 2020 and 2025, and it’s still adding: a BASF 1 million tonne flex-feed steam cracker at Zhanjiang (naphtha and butane; inaugurated March 26, 2026), a HAPCO 1.65 million tonne cracker, a Sinopec/INEOS 1.2 million tonne ethane cracker (on-stream since late 2023), 6.5 million tonnes of paraxylene capacity coming in Q4 2026. China’s CTO plants run on domestic coal and don’t care about Hormuz at all. Their economics right now: $557/tonne vs. naphtha cracking at $1,348.

So is the rationalization thesis dead? No, but it’s narrower than I initially thought.

The crisis is not solving structural global oversupply. Neale and DeYoe are probably right about that. What it is doing is accelerating the exit of high-cost naphtha crackers in Europe and Asia that were going to close anyway, and demonstrating in real time that the cost curve has a permanent break in it.

Wood Mackenzie’s Lee Andrew Fagg (VP of Chemicals Consulting) published something on March 25 that captured this: “The blockade may compress years of supply rationalisation into months, hastening an inevitable rebalancing of global production capacity.” Plants that were bleeding cash since 2022 “may simply not restart if feedstock supply or logistics don’t normalise quickly.”

Note the “may.” Fagg is hedging. So am I.

The confirmed European closures already announced or executed come to about 4.3 million tonnes per year. The crisis-vulnerable assets (LyondellBasell’s Berre and Münchsmünster plants being sold to Aequita, BP Gelsenkirchen, Dow Terneuzen idled since June 2025) add another 2.0-2.8 million tonnes. (Note: INEOS Grangemouth is excluded from this list. Its KG cracker runs on US ethane imported via Dragon Ship carriers from the Marcellus shale under 15-year supply contracts, making it structurally insulated from Hormuz. Grangemouth is a crisis beneficiary, not a casualty.)

INEOS Project ONE at Antwerp, which brings 1.45 million tonnes of new ethane-fed capacity online in early 2027 (not 2026, as some reports have it; INEOS CEO John McNally said mechanical completion before end-2026, full startup early 2027), partially offsets the exits. Net European exit in a prolonged disruption scenario: roughly 4.9-5.7 million tonnes per year.

Japan’s losing about 1.3-1.4 million tonnes near-term from Maruzen Chiba, Idemitsu Chiba, and ENEOS Kawasaki. The Asahi Kasei/Mitsubishi Mizushima JV consolidation is announced but on a 2030 timeline, so I’m not counting it as crisis-accelerated.

The investable argument is not “the world is running out of polyethylene.” It isn’t. China will make sure of that. The argument is: the cost curve is permanently splitting, the low-cost producers are American and they know it, and the market is paying a crisis premium for what is actually a structural advantage. When the crisis ends, the spread compresses. But it doesn’t go back to $500. The high-cost European and Japanese crackers that close during this period don’t reopen just because Hormuz does. A naphtha cracker that was losing money at $500 spreads doesn’t restart into a world where the cost disadvantage is now permanently visible to every board and every lender.

Whether that’s enough to reshape the 2027-2028 supply curve in a way that sustains elevated margins is the question the trade hangs on. I think it probably is. I hold that at about 60% conviction, and I’m sizing the long-duration trade accordingly. The chemical tanker and pair trades don’t depend on this question. The rationalization trade does, and 60% conviction means position sizing that survives being wrong. DeYoe and Neale might be right. With that uncertainty explicit, here are the trades.

The trades

Four positions. I’m being explicit about entry logic, what kills each trade, and what I’m worried about.

1. Chemical tankers: Stolt-Nielsen (SNI.OL) and Odfjell (ODF.OL)

The polyethylene shortage I’ve been describing is a solid polymer story. PE ships as pellets, bagged and palletized inside standard dry shipping containers on container ships. Stolt-Nielsen and Odfjell do not carry PE. They operate liquid parcel tankers carrying bulk liquid chemicals: methanol, styrene, ethylene glycol, aromatics, acids, and other intermediates that sit upstream of the finished polymer. The reason this trade works is that the same dual-chokepoint shock that is starving Asian crackers of naphtha is simultaneously rerouting every liquid chemical cargo on the planet.

Liquid intermediates are the connective tissue of the global chemical supply chain, and Stolt and Odfjell carry them. The PE container dislocation is a separate but related problem that would express through container liner exposure, not chemical tankers. The setup: two chokepoints are closed simultaneously. Hormuz since March 2. Bab el-Mandeb (Red Sea/Houthis) still disrupted. Chemical tankers rerouting via the Cape of Good Hope add 10-14 extra days each way on an Asia-Europe voyage, or 20-28 extra days per round trip. On a 55-65 day baseline round trip, that is a 30-43% increase in voyage time. That absorbs 23-30% of effective fleet capacity without a single ship leaving the market. The orderbook-to-fleet ratio is 17-18%, which sounds heavy, and it will be if both chokepoints normalize simultaneously. But right now, newbuild deliveries cannot keep up with the rerouting demand.

Stolt-Nielsen has 162 ships, 3.1 million deadweight tonnes, the largest chemical parcel tanker fleet in the world. They reported Q1 revenue of $716.8 million, up 6.1% year over year, and that quarter only partially captured the crisis. CEO Udo Lange withdrew their $600-750 million EBITDA guidance on April 9, saying they had “limited visibility on how the conflict in the Middle East will play out.” The stock is at roughly NOK 311. P/E is around 5.6x on pre-crisis earnings. Q2 will be the first full-crisis quarter. They’ve got 12 newbuilds on order delivering through 2029.

Odfjell runs about 80 ships, has 18 on order, and reports next on May 6. Trading around NOK 119.

I’d look at SNI.OL below NOK 300, Odfjell below NOK 115. The structural tightness (dual chokepoint, aging fleet, limited orderbook relative to rerouting demand) persists even in a partial normalization.

I’m wrong if chemical tanker 1-year time charter rates for the 19,999 DWT segment decline below $18,000/day by September 2026, which would mean newbuild deliveries are overwhelming the rerouting demand. These rates aren’t on Bloomberg; you need Clarksons ($30K/year) or SNI.OL and ODF.OL quarterly earnings as proxies. Stolt’s Q1 deep-sea TCE was about $23,627/day, mostly pre-crisis.

The risk that keeps me up: if Hormuz AND Red Sea normalize at the same time, tonne-mile demand collapses 15-25% and the newbuild wave floods a suddenly oversupplied market. Chemical tankers would give back everything fast. But I keep coming back to two things. The chemical tanker fleet is old (average age above 14.5 years, 9% of the fleet above 25 years). And the orderbook, while heavy on paper, delivers into a market where two chokepoints are absorbing a quarter to a third of effective fleet capacity. If even one chokepoint stays disrupted, the math works. It would take both normalizing simultaneously, with a heavy delivery year, to break the trade. That’s possible. It’s not probable.

2. Long US ethane producers: Dow (DOW) and Westlake (WLK)

Dow’s ethane advantage is structural, not cyclical. It was there before Hormuz and it will be there after. The stock went from about $30 pre-crisis to roughly $39.91 at the peak, and it has held most of those gains at around $39 now. Unlike LyondellBasell, which gave back almost its entire crisis rally, Dow retained the move. The market is telling you it views Dow’s advantage as sticky. Jefferies’ Laurence Alexander estimates every $5/bbl move in oil adds about $500 million in EBITDA for Dow. Oil has moved from $67 to $102+. One offset: Dow owns 35% of Sadara Chemical Company, the $20 billion joint venture with Saudi Aramco that I mentioned earlier as having shut down entirely at Jubail. Dow does not consolidate Sadara (it is an equity-method investment), which limits the immediate P&L hit, but the impairment exposure is real. Sadara’s $3.7 billion senior debt has a principal repayment grace period expiring June 15, 2026, and the complex is generating zero revenue with no restart timeline. If you want a cleaner expression of the US ethane advantage without Middle East JV risk, Westlake (WLK) is the purer play at the cracker level: its ethylene crackers are North American and ethane-fed. However, Westlake has meaningful European downstream exposure through Vinnolit (four PVC and chlor-alkali sites across Germany, generating over €900 million in annual revenue) and Westlake Epoxy (Rotterdam, Germany, Spain), which are directly vulnerable to European feedstock and energy cost pressures.

I would split the position between DOW and WLK. WLK has no Middle East JV risk (unlike Dow’s Sadara exposure), but its European downstream operations face the same margin compression the piece describes. On balance, I would still tilt toward WLK as the larger weight, but the sizing edge over DOW is narrower than a pure-play thesis would suggest.

Short side: Amcor (AMCR). Amcor is the world’s largest packaging company after the Berry Global merger, with combined post-merger adjusted EBITDA running at approximately $3.5 billion annualized (based on H1 FY2026 adjusted EBITDA of $1,736 million, which includes $93 million of Berry acquisition synergies). Resin (PE, PP) is estimated at roughly 60% of their cost of goods sold. Their formula pricing mechanisms reprice quarterly, meaning calendar Q2 2026 (Amcor’s fiscal Q4 2026, as their fiscal year ends June 30) absorbs the full force of the March-May PE price spikes before catch-up.

The math here is worth being precise about. Amcor’s total PE-exposed resin spend is roughly $7-8 billion of their $12-14 billion total resin and input costs. That $7-8 billion figure includes both raw pellet cost and conversion-related input costs (energy for film extrusion, co-extrusion, lamination). The raw PE pellet component is roughly 50-60% of that, call it $4-5 billion annually, or approximately $1-1.25 billion per quarter. At pre-crisis pricing of roughly $0.60/lb, that is 1.6-2.1 billion pounds of PE purchased per quarter. A $0.35/lb cumulative price spike on that volume produces a quarterly unrecovered cost headwind of $560-730 million before any emergency pass-through mechanisms activate. Against quarterly adjusted EBITDA of $826 million (Amcor’s reported Q2 FY2026, quarter ended December 31, 2025), that is a 67-88% hit in a single quarter.

Even if emergency repricing clauses in their major CPG contracts compress half the lag, you are still looking at a 34-44% quarterly EBITDA compression on a company that just completed an $8.4 billion merger, is ramping integration, and carried net debt of $14.1 billion at December 31, 2025, implying approximately 4.1x net leverage on annualized EBITDA. That is not a company you want to be long. Large FMCG customers (Nestle, P&G) will push back hard on emergency cost pass-throughs when consumers are already watching prices and tariff-driven inflation is in the headlines. The formulas assume gradual $0.05-0.10/lb moves and normal price discovery. This is a $0.35/lb shock in three months. The short is back on. The leverage is lower than the Trade 3 names, so the covenant risk is not existential, but the Q2 earnings miss will be material and the stock has not priced it.

I am wrong on the long side if US PE contract prices reverse below $0.60/lb by August 2026. That would mean the spread compression is happening fast enough that Dow’s and Westlake’s margin expansion stalls. I am wrong on the short side if Amcor’s emergency repricing clauses activate within 30 days and US PE contract prices reverse below $0.65/lb by July, meaning the cost headwind was too brief to compress margins materially.

The risk on the long side: Dow is “only” up 33% from pre-crisis, which is less stretched than the 60-80% moves in the broader chemical group, but the Sadara impairment risk is an offset that consensus has not modeled. Westlake has no such offset. The risk on the short side: Amcor’s formula pricing may activate faster than I am estimating, and $650 million of targeted merger synergies ($93 million captured in H1, tracking at the upper end of management’s run-rate guidance) provide a buffer I cannot precisely size. If demand destruction kicks in (see bear cases below), even ethane-advantaged producers lose volume, and Amcor’s resin costs fall with the market, compressing the short.

3. Credit shorts: leveraged packaging

The quarterly repricing lag that produces a 34-44% EBITDA compression even at a scaled operator like Amcor (after partial emergency pass-throughs) becomes an existential question at 5x leverage. The names: Novolex (Apollo-backed; completed its $6.7 billion acquisition of Pactiv Evergreen in April 2025, financed with approximately $3 billion in term loans and $1.6 billion in high-yield bonds; combined entity runs at roughly 5x pro forma leverage with heavy PE/PP exposure in commodity food packaging and limited pricing power) and Mauser Packaging (S&P issuer rating B, dominant HDPE input costs, already stressed pre-crisis).

I’ll have live CDS levels and covenant analysis when I’m on the terminal. For now, the logic: if a one-quarter repricing lag compresses EBITDA by 34-44% at a company of Amcor’s scale, the same math at 5x leverage blows through maintenance covenants. If you don’t have Bloomberg access, the equity pair (Trade 2) expresses the same thesis at lower resolution.

4. Structural rationalization (long-duration)

This is the position that depends on whether you believe the cost curve split is permanent and whether crisis-accelerated closures actually happen. Every million tonnes per year of permanent cracker exit tightens the 2027-2028 supply curve. Even if the crisis resolves tomorrow, crackers that close during this period don’t reverse. Furnaces that go cold in Berre and Münchsmünster don’t get hot again because a diplomat shook hands in Islamabad.

The beneficiaries are the last-standing low-cost US producers. Dow and Westlake. INEOS (private, but its Project ONE comes online into what should be a tightened European market in early 2027, 70% complete as of June 2025, total investment about €4 billion).

I’m wrong if zero additional European or Asian cracker closures beyond the pre-crisis announced list are confirmed by December 2026. That would mean the crisis didn’t accelerate anything, DeYoe was right, and this is just crisis premium that fades.

The risk: you’re betting against Neale (”not going to rationalize our way out of oversupply”) and DeYoe (”conflict to resolve without material asset rationalization”). Both are smart. I’m betting they’re partially right (global oversupply persists) but wrong on the margin (the cost curve split widens permanently). That’s a subtle thesis, not a high-conviction one. Size accordingly.

The bear cases

Here they are.

“This is just oil with extra steps.” The strongest version: every dollar of petrochemical disruption traces back to Hormuz. When Hormuz reopens, crackers restart in days to weeks (Harvey proves this, or so the argument goes; the actual timelines were longer, as documented above), feedstock flows resume, and the 250-275 day number is a worst-case corporate estimate designed to manage analyst expectations downward. The EIA assumes production shut-ins fall to 6.7 million barrels per day in May under rapid resolution and normalize by late 2026.

My response: the cracker restart part of this is correct. Machines turn back on. But the feedstock availability and logistics sequencing are the binding constraints, not the machines. The pecking order I described above means petrochemicals are last in line. The stranded vessel queue needs to clear. Container dislocation takes quarters. The bear case is right that 250-275 days overstates the global applicability, but wrong that normalization is quick. The honest number is probably 4-6 months for most of the non-conflict-zone chemical industry to normalize, with the conflict zone itself taking years.

“The rationalization was already priced.” I’ve already made this argument as strong as I can in the section above. DOW up 40% pre-Hormuz. Chemical Week January cover. LYB’s 42% crash proving how much was crisis premium. DeYoe and Neale both arguing the same side. I’m not going to restate it. If you read that section and think they’re right, don’t take the long-duration trade.

“Demand destruction kills everything.” This is the one that actually scares me. In 2008, ethylene contract prices went from 74.5 cents per pound in July to 28.5 cents in January 2009. A 62% collapse in six months. LyondellBasell filed Chapter 11. US chemical demand fell 9.4% against the ACC’s forecast of negative 3.6%.

Hart himself, the guy I’m citing for the 22 million tonne figure, said at the same WPC presentation: “We are going to see demand destruction.” ISM Prices at 78.3 with New Orders declining from 55.8 to 53.5 is the classic divergence that precedes demand-side reversals. ISM Chair Susan Spence (she chairs the Manufacturing Business Survey Committee specifically) warned: “Our concern is that the demand indicators are going in the wrong direction.” Among negative survey comments from March, about 40% cited the Iran war and about 20% cited tariffs. (To be clear: that’s comment sentiment distribution, not a formal decomposition of the index. Spence said it was “too soon to tell” the actual breakdown.)

The difference from 2008: Lehman Brothers amplified the demand destruction into a systemic collapse. But the chemical demand destruction started in July 2008, two months before Lehman, driven by oil prices alone. The current setup doesn’t have the financial crisis catalyst, at least not yet. It doesn’t need one for demand destruction to matter. But $100+ oil eating into consumer spending while ISM New Orders are already softening is not a setup I can dismiss. If this extends through Q3 and recession hits, the supply thesis gets overwhelmed by the demand collapse.

If US Manufacturing PMI falls below 48.0 and ISM New Orders falls below 45.0 simultaneously before August 2026, this thesis is dead. I’d be out of everything.

The consumer story you’re not going to hear for two more months

The most visceral evidence (empty shelves, purchase limits, rationing) is in Asia, not the US or UK. The US consumer story is real but it’s a pricing story, not a shortage story. US PE contract prices are up roughly 20% since March. LyondellBasell successfully pushed through price increases for March and April, and has nominated further hikes for May, targeting a cumulative $0.35/lb. US crackers are ethane-fed and running full. There are no empty shelves at US Walmart. US producers are choosing to export at $1,200/mt premiums rather than sell domestically at $500.

The US hit arrives through a different channel. Asian PE production is down 30-50%. The factories in China, South Korea, and Taiwan that turn PE into the finished plastic products Americans buy can’t get feedstock. Ocean freight takes 2-5 weeks. At 42+ days into the crisis, the first wave of Asian finished-goods disruption should be hitting US ports now. You won’t see it at retail for another month or two. By June or July, the US consumer story catches up to the Asian one: not as empty shelves, but as price hikes and allocation notices on imported plastic goods.

In Europe, it’s closer. LyondellBasell declared force majeure on polyolefin supply from Rotterdam (see earlier note on the Carrington sale to Aequita). European PE prices have risen €200-500/mt. Packaging publications report brand owners struggling to secure material on normal terms.

What I’m watching (leading indicators)

What I’m watching to figure out which scenario is playing out.

Chinese CTO/MTO operating rates. SunSirs publishes weekly. When these push above 85% for 8 consecutive weeks, China’s substitution response is capping the shortage. Watch alongside Asian PE prices.

European cracker force majeure status. ICIS or the CMS Law FM tracker (free at cms.law), updated weekly. Declining count means normalization. Rising count means deterioration.

Force majeure trend globally. The direction matters more than the level. Rising from 34+ means the cascade is still spreading. A sustained decline toward 20 means the worst is past.

North American PE days-of-supply. ACC publishes monthly. Referenced in Dow and LyondellBasell earnings calls quarterly. Pre-crisis was about 37 days versus 45-day balanced.

Chemical tanker time charter rates. SNI.OL and ODF.OL quarterly earnings are the accessible proxy (Clarksons has the raw data but costs $30K/yr). Stolt’s Q2 earnings in August will be the first full-crisis data point.

I’m wrong if the US-Asia HDPE spread compresses below $600/mt before September 30, 2026.

I’m out of everything if US Manufacturing PMI then falls below 48.0 and ISM New Orders falls below 45.0 simultaneously before August 2026. (Note: chemical equities are hyper-leading cyclicals and will likely give back crisis premiums well before ISM confirms a recession. The leading indicators above are the real trigger; the ISM thresholds are the final confirmation.)

I’m wrong if DOW, LYB, and WLK all trade below their February 28 pre-crisis closing prices simultaneously at any point before December 31, 2026.

I’m wrong if two or more European naphtha-fed crackers that declared force majeure (OMV Burghausen, Orlen Plock, Versalis Priolo) announce full restart at normal utilization before September 2026. (Note: LyondellBasell’s force majeure was on polyolefin supply from Rotterdam, a downstream polymer operation, not a cracker. It is excluded from this trigger.)

None of these is triggered as of April 15. I’ll update the status in every subsequent piece. One caution on the demand destruction triggers: these indicators may not degrade gradually. If suppressed demand signals snap rather than slide, the window between “fine” and “triggered” could compress to weeks. The monthly ISM print is the formal trigger, but I will be watching higher-frequency proxies (Asian port throughput, weekly resin inventory reports, freight rate movements) for early confirmation between prints.

Physical Brent (Forties grade) traded at $147 on April 10. Futures were at roughly $97, having cratered from their early-April highs as the market briefly priced in a ceasefire that collapsed two days later. Spot benchmarks sat around $127. A $50 premium of physical over paper, more than 50% backwardation. When physical oil trades at a $50 premium to futures, the market is not speculating. It is screaming.

And that’s the oil market. What no one has structured for investors is what’s happening one level below, in the feedstock layer that turns that oil into everything you touch.

Everyone’s still watching Brent.
