Company profile and corporate structure

Saramin is Korea’s largest online job matching platform by web traffic, listed on KOSDAQ since 2012 and renamed from SaraminHR in April 2023. Think of it as the Indeed of Korea, except with a genuine duopoly market structure that Indeed could only dream of in the US.

The platform ecosystem spans multiple verticals. The flagship saramin.co.kr handles mainstream full-time recruitment. Saramin is controlled through the Daou Kiwoom Group’s pyramid structure, with Daou Technology holding 32.66% of shares. Treasury shares account for another ~8.1%. The full ownership chain and its implications are examined in the governance section below. The current CEO, Hwang Hyun-soon, was appointed in early 2024. His background and the circumstances of his appointment warrant scrutiny. His shareholding of just 0.06% provides minimal direct alignment.

The Remember Co windfall creates a negative enterprise value

The single most transformative recent event is Saramin’s exit from Remember & Company, widely called “Korea’s LinkedIn.” Remember began as a business card management app in 2013 and evolved into an AI-enabled HR tech platform with 5 million+ users, 10,000+ enterprise clients, and 500+ million business cards in its database. Revenue exploded from KRW 5.8 billion in 2021 to KRW 68.4 billion by 2024 (a 12x increase in three years) and the company reached monthly profitability by mid-2025.

In August 2025, EQT’s BPEA Private Equity Fund VIII acquired 93.1% of Remember at a valuation of approximately KRW 500 billion (~USD 400 million). The deal closed October 23, 2025. Tag-along rights in the shareholder agreement ensured all minorities received the same per-share price, no minority discount applied.

Korea’s hiring freeze is the worst in a generation

I want to be very clear about what is happening to Saramin’s revenue, because the market appears to be conflating cyclical pain with structural decline. This is not a competitive failure. It is not LinkedIn eating their lunch. It is not AI disruption. It is Korea’s deepest labor market contraction since the 1997-98 Asian Financial Crisis, and it is hitting every employment platform indiscriminately.

The government’s WorkNet job openings-per-seeker ratio (구인배율) plunged to 0.28 in January 2025, the lowest January reading since 1999, meaning just 28 openings per 100 seekers. Through mid-2025, the ratio hovered between 0.35 and 0.43, broadly confirming the thesis figure of 0.37. In July 2025, it stood at 0.40, described officially as a 26-year low. New job listings on WorkNet fell a staggering 42.7% year-on-year in January 2025.

Several forces converged. Korea’s GDP grew only ~1.0% in 2025, the weakest since the pandemic, battered by the December 2024 martial law crisis, President Yoon’s impeachment, US tariff shocks, and weak domestic demand. Manufacturing employment has been in persistent decline, with 112,000 jobs lost in March 2025 alone. Construction employment fell for 21+ consecutive months. Youth employment (ages 15–29) dropped 178,000 in 2025, the largest decline since COVID.

Korea’s demographic trajectory amplifies the structural challenge. The total fertility rate of 0.72–0.75 is the world’s lowest. Working-age population is already shrinking and projected to halve by 2065. Korea crossed the “super-aged” threshold (20%+ aged 65+) in late 2024. The OECD projects aggregate labor force participation peaked in 2025 and will begin contracting absolutely by 2028.

The cyclical outlook shows early signs of stabilisation. 2026 GDP is forecast at 1.8-2.1% across IMF, OECD, KDI, and Bank of Korea estimates. The BOK has cut rates 100 basis points since October 2024 to 2.5%, with further easing expected. Two supplementary budgets totalling KRW 44.3 trillion were passed in 2025. The 2026 national budget of KRW 727.9 trillion represents an 8.1% increase, the largest ever. A February 2026 Incruit survey found 73.4% of firms confirmed new graduate hiring plans for 2026 (+7.9 percentage points year-on-year), with large companies showing particular recovery at 87.3%. However, the Korea Labor Institute characterises the outlook as “quantitative stability with qualitative instability”, a gradual grind rather than a V-shaped snapback.

This matters enormously for the Saramin thesis. We are not underwriting a V-shaped recovery. We do not need one. But I want to be precise about what we are underwriting, because there is an obvious tension between the cyclical recovery thesis and the demographic data presented above. If working-age population has shrunk by roughly 750,000 to 1,000,000 between 2019 and 2028, why should gross job posting volumes return to 2019 levels? The honest answer is that they probably will not, at least not in absolute terms. Our base case assumes hiring volumes recover to roughly 85 to 90% of 2019 levels, reflecting cyclical normalisation partially offset by the smaller labor pool. There are plausible reasons volumes could exceed this: rising platform-mediated hiring of foreign workers (KoMate’s job postings tripled in its first year), increasing job mobility among experienced professionals, and a secular shift from offline to online recruitment among smaller employers. But we are not relying on those tailwinds. Even at 85% of 2019 volumes, which implies revenue of roughly KRW 135 to 140 billion (versus KRW 121 billion today and KRW 149 billion at peak), operating leverage on Saramin’s near-zero variable cost base pushes operating profit to KRW 25 to 28 billion. The stock would trade at roughly 5 to 6x operating earnings on a net-cash-adjusted basis, and the share price would need to more than double just to reach its historical median valuation. The asymmetry here is not about heroic assumptions. It is about mean reversion in a cyclical business that the market has priced as though the cycle will never turn.

A genuine duopoly with high barriers to entry

Saramin operates one half of Korea’s full-time online recruitment duopoly. The other half is JobKorea/Worxphere, which is 90% owned by Affinity Equity Partners and 10% by Australia’s SEEK Ltd following a 2021 acquisition at an enterprise value of ~KRW 900 billion (~17x EBITDA).

By web traffic, Saramin has overtaken JobKorea in recent years: SimilarWeb data from August 2025 shows saramin.co.kr at 17.2 million monthly visits versus jobkorea.co.kr at ~10.6 million. Saramin ranks #1 in Jobs & Employment in South Korea. Mobile app MAU data similarly favours Saramin at 9.22 million cumulative users (H1 2024). Brand awareness is virtually tied at ~80%, and recent usage rates are neck-and-neck. The duopoly is real, no other player approaches these volumes. Incruit draws ~3.5 million monthly visits; Wanted ~617,000 MAU

.

Pricing behaviour tells you everything you need to know about duopoly health. Both platforms operate tiered models: free basic listings with paid premium placements ranging from KRW 182,000 to KRW 6,000,000 per posting. Estimated ARPU runs approximately KRW 1.2 to 2.4 million per active employer per year (~$850 to $1,700). For context, StepStone charges €1,195 per basic listing in Germany. Korean employers get far more for far less, which is both the duopoly’s strength (enormous installed base of price-sensitive SMEs) and its constraint (limited room to raise prices aggressively). Revenue derives from promoted job postings, resume database access, enterprise brand pages, and subscription packages. Saramin’s ~99.9% gross margin reflects the near-zero marginal cost of a platform business. The duopoly structure naturally discourages destructive price competition, neither player needs to undercut when both enjoy entrenched market positions.

Foreign competitors have failed to gain meaningful traction. LinkedIn has an estimated 4-5 million Korean users but is used primarily for multinational/expat hiring and high-income professional networking, not mainstream domestic recruitment. Indeed operates indeed.co.kr as a job aggregator but has negligible market share. The barriers are formidable: Korean-language interface requirements, cultural hiring norms (structured 자기소개서 formats, academic credential emphasis), business registration requirements for job posting, and deeply embedded network effects with millions of resumes and postings concentrated on Saramin and JobKorea. As KED Global noted, the total addressable market of ~KRW 300 billion is “too small for Naver and Kakao to enter”, itself a barrier against new domestic entrants.

The competitive landscape beyond the duopoly

Before assessing the durability of Saramin’s moat, it is worth mapping the competitive landscape beyond the duopoly, because the threats to Saramin’s position are more likely to come from niche specialists than from a frontal assault on mainstream recruitment:

Remember deserves particular attention. Acquired by EQT for ~₩500B in August 2025, Remember evolved from a business card management app into Korea’s closest analogue to LinkedIn, a professional networking platform targeting experienced, higher-earning professionals. Its 500 million name card records and premium “Remember Black” service (targeting professionals earning >₩50M) position it to capture the high-value lateral hiring segment that represents Saramin’s growth frontier. Under EQT’s backing (which also owns Beamery UK, HRBrain Japan, and Handshake US), Remember has significant firepower for further expansion.

Wanted Lab operates a differentiated success-fee model (averaging ₩4.57M per successful hire), appealing to tech companies and startups that prefer pay-for-outcomes over advertising spend. At ₩36.7B revenue with 32,000 corporate clients, it’s profitable in some quarters but turned to operating losses in 2024 as the hiring market softened.

JobKorea/Worxphere is building the broadest ecosystem: full-time (JobKorea), part-time (Albamon, with ~72.5% part-time market share), company reviews (Jobplanet, acquired 2025), and ATS (NineHire, acquired 2024). Its PE ownership under Affinity with SEEK’s 10% strategic stake provides both capital and global best-practice transfer.

Saramin’s moat rests on four pillars of varying durability

Two-sided network effects (moderate strength). Saramin’s platform liquidity, built on millions of registered users generating 30 million+ annual applications, is formidable. However, academic research on job platforms consistently finds these are “winner-take-most” rather than “winner-take-all” markets. Multi-homing is too easy, job seekers search multiple platforms in the same session, and employers post across platforms simultaneously. This distinguishes job boards from social networks where identity and connections create higher switching costs.

Korean-language and cultural embeddedness (high durability). This is arguably Saramin’s strongest moat source. The Korean resume format (photo, military service, spec culture), structured application processes, and Korean-only interface create barriers that have proven insurmountable for LinkedIn and Indeed despite years of effort. LinkedIn’s 0.07%

year-over-year user growth in Korea versus double-digit growth in most markets illustrates the depth of this cultural moat. As one Korean user on Blind noted: “Friends earning over ₩200M get LinkedIn offers, but I find it inconvenient, maybe because my English isn’t good.”

Data assets (growing but contestable). Saramin’s accumulated data, 17M user profiles, years of application behavioral data, 2.16M+ psychometric assessment results, and a massive Korean-language cover letter corpus powering its Spectrum AI screening tool, creates an information advantage. The company’s AI Lab (since 2014) and 20+ proprietary algorithms leverage this data for matching, recommendations, and screening. However, JobKorea holds comparable data assets, and Remember’s 500M business card records represent a differentiated data set for the professional segment.

SME customer relationships and brand (moderate durability). Saramin is described as “particularly favored by small and medium-sized enterprises.” Its free-to-post model, government-subsidized ATS program (targeting 4,000 SMEs with 80% subsidy), and expanding suite of affordable HR tools (assessments, ATS, AI screening) create a multi-product relationship with SME employers. Brand recognition among younger demographics (~84% awareness among 20–30s) is extremely high.

Why the moat is narrow, not wide

Benchmarking Saramin against global peers crystallizes its moat width. The most instructive comparison is SEEK Limited - like Saramin, a locally dominant platform in a linguistically distinct, mid-sized market. Morningstar rates SEEK as narrow moat despite SEEK’s even more dominant 90% share of time spent on Australian job sites. The parallel is clear:

Three factors prevent Saramin from reaching wide-moat status. First, multi-homing is too prevalent, employers and job seekers routinely use both Saramin and JobKorea, limiting either platform’s individual lock-in. Second, revenue is declining (three consecutive years of decline from the 2022 peak), demonstrating meaningful cyclical vulnerability and limited pricing power during downturns. Third, the AI investment gap is widening. If AI-powered matching becomes the primary competitive differentiator, Saramin’s capacity to compete technologically is constrained by its revenue base of just KRW 121 billion. I examine this risk in detail in the AI section below.

Financial performance reflects cyclical trough, not structural decline

Revenue peaked at KRW 148.9 billion in 2022 and has declined in each subsequent year: KRW 131.5 billion in 2023 (-11.7%), KRW 128.4 billion in 2024 (-2.4%), and KRW 121.2 billion in 2025 (-5.5%). The cumulative decline of ~18.6% tracks closely with the collapse in Korean job postings. Operating profit followed the same trajectory: from ~KRW 40 billion in 2022 (27% margin) to KRW 16.8 billion in 2025 (13.8% margin). Q1 2025 was particularly brutal, with operating profit falling 63% year-on-year to KRW 2.0 billion.

The historical growth profile was attractive. From IPO in 2012 through 2019, revenue compounded at approximately ~10% annually. The COVID-era hiring boom pushed revenue growth to +34% in 2021 before the cycle reversed. This pattern is consistent with a high-quality platform business experiencing a severe cyclical downturn rather than permanent impairment. The career platform segment (~80% of revenue) generates virtually all operating profit; the outsourcing (~15%) and recruitment consulting (~5%) segments are labour-intensive, margin-dilutive, and contribute negligibly to earnings.

The balance sheet is now fortress-like. Pre-Remember sale, total liabilities were just KRW 22 billion against KRW 213 billion in assets. Post-sale, the company sits on estimated net cash of approximately KRW 180 billion, consistent with our central estimate used throughout this analysis. The dividend stands at KRW 500 per share, yielding ~3.5% at current prices (closer to ~4% at the 52-week low). The company executed a KRW 6 billion buyback program in late 2024 and a KRW 3 billion program in 2023. Morningstar’s total yield estimate including buybacks is 5.23%.

ROCE Analysis

The same cyclical dynamic is visible in returns on capital. Return on capital employed tells a complementary story to the margin compression above. Using Cap IQ data:

At peak (2021), ROCE was likely ~24-25%. At the current trough, ROCE has compressed to 10-11%, still respectable but significantly below mid-cycle quality. The critical insight is that this compression is entirely revenue-driven, not capital-driven. The capital base has barely changed; it is earnings that have halved. If operating profit merely returns to 2019 levels ($23M EBIT), ROCE immediately re-rates to 16%. If it returns to the 2021-22 peak ($32-34M), ROCE returns to ~22-24%.

The post-Remember balance sheet introduces a complication: the ~$100M+ in net cash now sitting on the balance sheet inflates the capital employed denominator, mechanically depressing ROCE. On a cash-adjusted basis (stripping excess cash from capital employed), the operating business generates a substantially higher ROCE, perhaps 25-30%+ even on depressed earnings, confirming the thesis that this is a high-quality business temporarily obscured by both cyclical headwinds and a lazy balance sheet.

The TEV/EBIT from Cap IQ tells the story cleanly: 2.55x at peak earnings (2022), expanding to 6.91x on depressed LTM earnings. But critically, all of these multiples are pre-Remember-sale, meaning the true pro-forma TEV is roughly negative $20-50M.

AI investment is both opportunity and existential question

Now for the part that keeps me up at night. Saramin established its AI Lab in 2014, early by industry standards, and has built a reasonable suite of AI-powered tools. But “reasonable” is not the same as “defensible,” and this is where I want to be intellectually honest about the bear case. Current AI-powered services include personalised job recommendations (analysing 2.27 million+ monthly data points), AI resume and self-introduction coaching using NLU, ChatGPT-integrated interview preparation, and the Saramin Recruitment Center launched July 2025 to automate SME hiring workflows.

The AI disruption risk, however, is the most significant long-term threat. OpenAI plans to launch a Jobs Platform by mid-2026, aiming to match employers and AI-skilled candidates through LLM-powered matching. Indeed has partnered directly with OpenAI. LinkedIn is rolling out AI Job Match scores, AI recruitment agents for SMBs, and natural-language semantic search globally. ChatGPT’s codebase already contains job search feature flags. AIM Group industry analysts have drawn parallels to Stack Overflow’s traffic collapse after ChatGPT’s launch.

Saramin’s Korean-language moat provides meaningful but diminishing protection, and I want to distinguish between two layers that are eroding at very different speeds. The linguistic barrier, the claim that LLMs are less optimised for Korean than English, was true in 2023 and is rapidly becoming less true. Korean is a high-resource language with abundant training data, and frontier models already handle it competently. I would not build a three-year thesis on this barrier alone. The institutional and cultural barrier is substantially more durable. It is not just that Korean employers use structured 자기소개서 formats; it is that Saramin has built a decade of scoring models, template libraries, and employer workflows around those formats. It is the integration with Korean business registration systems, the compliance infrastructure for labor regulations, and the trust relationships with tens of thousands of SME HR managers who have never used anything else. These are not barriers that a better algorithm dissolves overnight. But nor is this a binary: the moat either “holds” or “breaks.” The more likely scenario is gradual erosion at the margins, with LinkedIn growing from 4.6 million to perhaps 8 million Korean users over five years, Remember capturing more of the experienced-hire segment under EQT’s ownership (backed by EQT’s portfolio of HR tech assets including Beamery, HRBrain, and Handshake), and Saramin’s traffic slowly leaking without a single dramatic rupture.

Notably, both Google and Meta previously attempted and failed to break into job matching, and experts remain sceptical that horizontal AI companies can replicate the deep employer relationships, regulatory compliance, and trust that dedicated platforms have built. The greater risk may come not from AI startups but from incumbent platforms supercharging their offerings, specifically LinkedIn’s Hiring Assistant (launched October 2024, reportedly cutting sourcing time by 33%) and Indeed’s automated Hiring Platform.

CEO Hwang has made “AI transformation” a stated strategic priority, and management committed Remember proceeds to AI investment. The problem is that Saramin’s entire annual revenue of KRW 121 billion ($86 million) is roughly what Recruit Holdings spends on R&D in a single quarter. If AI-powered matching becomes the primary competitive differentiator in recruitment, Saramin simply cannot outspend its way to relevance. The moat has to be cultural and linguistic, not technological.

I think the institutional moat holds for three to five years against a frontal assault, but the gradual erosion scenario is harder to time and harder for management to respond to. The signals to watch are LinkedIn’s Korean user growth rate, Remember’s enterprise client count under EQT ownership, and the share of Saramin’s referral traffic coming from AI chatbots (currently 7.42% of desktop, a number that deserves quarterly monitoring).

Beyond that, I have less conviction, and I suspect the market’s AI anxiety is a meaningful portion of why this trades where it does. The Monster.com precedent is instructive: it peaked at an $8 billion market cap, dismissed Indeed’s aggregator model as irrelevant, and filed for Chapter 11 in June 2025. The job boards that were sold off fetched just $28 million. Moats in this industry can erode fast when you stop investing. The difference, for now, is that Saramin is investing and Monster stopped years ago.

Saramin’s management, governance, and valuation under the microscope

This is where the thesis gets uncomfortable. Everything above paints a picture of a cheap, well-positioned platform business at a cyclical trough. What follows is the reason it is cheap, and it is not pretty.

Saramin sits at a striking inflection point: one half of Korea’s job board duopoly trading near its adjusted enterprise value of zero, controlled by a scandal-tainted chaebol group with an unproven heir, led by a CEO who was pushed out of his last role after a KRW 494B risk management disaster. The core bull thesis, that KRW 140–145B in post-tax Remember proceeds plus a KRW 17B operating profit business are being offered for KRW 149B in market cap, is arithmetically compelling. But the management and governance layer introduces risks that can permanently destroy value: an untested controlling shareholder, a CEO whose appointment smells of cronyism, zero analyst coverage, no stated capital return plan, and a group structure engineered for succession tax avoidance rather than minority shareholder value creation.

CEO Hwang Hyun-soon: loyal, not a visionary

Hwang’s career is inseparable from the Daou Kiwoom Group. Born in 1967, educated at Seoul National University (BA and MA in Business Administration), he worked briefly at Korea Long Term Credit Bank and IBM Korea before joining Kiwoom.com Securities as a founding member in 2000. He spent the next 23 years rising through the ranks, IB division head, retail division head, Daou Kiwoom Group Strategic Management Office head (2015), and finally CEO of Kiwoom Securities from January 2022. He grew Kiwoom to #1 in domestic trading volume and expanded into overseas stock trading, fractional shares, and comprehensive financial services. By any measure, his operational achievements at Kiwoom were substantial.

Then 2023 happened. Two catastrophic risk management failures struck within six months. In April 2023, the SG Securities CFD stock manipulation scandal revealed that a manipulation ring led by Ra Deok-yeon had exploited Kiwoom Securities’ CFD platform. Hwang publicly defended founder Kim Ik-rae, who had sold KRW 60.5B of Daou Data shares two trading days before the crash, telling reporters: “I’ll stake my job on it. There is a 0.00001% chance of involvement.” This statement was widely ridiculed as either naive or deliberately dishonest.

In October 2023, the Youngpoong Paper margin collapse delivered the killing blow. While every other major Korean brokerage had raised the margin deposit rate on Youngpoong Paper to 100% by Q1 2023, effectively blocking leveraged purchases, Kiwoom maintained a 40% rate until the day trading was halted on October 19. This allowed a stock manipulation syndicate operating through ~100 abnormal accounts to accumulate KRW 494.3B in margin receivables, exceeding Kiwoom’s entire H1 2023 net profit. The firm booked approximately KRW 450B in provisions in Q4 2023. Hwang did not personally set the margin rate, but as CEO he bore ultimate responsibility for a risk management culture that produced two massive failures in six months. He submitted his resignation on November 9, 2023, citing “道義的 責任”, moral responsibility.

If you are not familiar with Korean corporate culture, let me translate: 도의적 책임” (moral responsibility) is the standard euphemism executives use when they are being pushed out but need to preserve face for future redeployment within the group. It signals institutional rather than personal culpability, positions the departure as technically voluntary, and, critically, preserves the executive’s reputation for future redeployment within the group. The Kiwoom board initially deferred acceptance (November 16), a ritualistic gesture, then accepted on November 27. Hwang remained as an unregistered non-executive president through early 2024 for “handover purposes.” Four months later, he was appointed CEO of Saramin at the March 20, 2024 AGM. At the same meeting, the company shortened director terms from 3 to 2 years, interpreted by some as keeping his appointment flexible.

The Saramin board’s official rationale cited his “deep understanding of the IT industry and customers” and the importance of “linkage with other industries including finance.” Korean business media characterized the appointment as “제 식구 챙기기” - taking care of one’s own people. Industry observers noted his entire career was in financial services, making him a poor fit for an HR/recruitment platform. His shareholding began at a negligible 6,619 shares (0.06%) and has since increased to 20,941 shares (0.18%), still trivially small for a CEO whose decisions will allocate KRW 140B+ in cash.

Under Hwang’s strategic vision of transforming Saramin from a job-listing site into a “career platform” and eventually a “life platform,” the company has launched AI resume coaching, AI mock interviews, a Saramin Store (career materials marketplace), a foreign worker recruitment service (KoMate), a senior career platform (Wonderful Senior), and even fortune-telling and dating features. App installs are up 25.9% and job applications up 15.1%. But financial performance has deteriorated continuously: FY2025 revenue fell 5.5% to KRW 121.2B and operating profit dropped 21.1% to KRW 16.8B.

The net income surge to KRW 91.6B was entirely driven by the one-time Remember disposal gain. There is a growing disconnect between Hwang’s activity-based metrics (app downloads, feature launches) and the business fundamentals that actually drive equity value. Revenue is down. Margins are compressing. Cash conversion is being obscured by a one-time Remember gain. And the strategic vision has expanded to include fortune-telling and dating features. I will say that again: a job board is now offering fortune-telling. The “life platform” vision may prove inspired. The financials so far suggest it is empire-building dressed up as strategy, the kind of scope creep that happens when management has too much cash and too little accountability.

The Daou Kiwoom pyramid: 1.5% economic interest, 100% of the decisions

Kim Dong-jun, born 1984, controls Saramin through a four-layer pyramid structure that belongs in a corporate governance textbook, and not as a positive example. Educated at Monta Vista High School in Cupertino, California, USC (BS Accounting), and Cornell (MBA), he is described by Korean financial press as “Korea’s Howard Hughes.” No official photograph of him exists publicly. Let that sink in for a moment: the man who ultimately controls a ~KRW 10 trillion conglomerate encompassing 48 affiliates, ranked 52nd among Korean business groups, has never been photographed for the record.

The ownership chain runs as follows: Kim Dong-jun holds 33.13% of eMoney, which holds 31.56% of Daou Data, which holds 45.2% of Daou Technology, which holds 32.66% of Saramin. Multiply through, and his effective economic interest in Saramin is approximately 1.54%. Yet he exercises full control. The apex entity, eMoney, has capital of just KRW 830 million. That is not a typo. A company with less than $600,000 in capital sits atop a structure controlling tens of billions of dollars in enterprise value. If you have ever wondered why Korean equities trade at a persistent discount to global peers, this is the answer distilled to a single case study.

The pyramid was constructed through what Korean regulators call “편법 승계,” or irregular succession, analogous to Samsung’s Lee Jae-yong succession via Samsung SDS. The mechanics were textbook chaebol: Kim Ik-rae acquired 100% of eMoney in 2009, then donated half the shares back as treasury stock. eMoney then systematically purchased Daou Data shares using Kim Ik-rae’s personal guarantee as collateral. In 2020, at the depths of the COVID crash, Kim Ik-rae sold 2.24 million Daou Data shares to eMoney via off-hours block trades at depressed prices (KRW 5,290 to 7,650 per share), a transaction whose timing was, shall we say, convenient. Finally, he gifted 2 million Daou Data shares to his three children in October 2021. The net result: control of the group transferred across generations with minimal inheritance or gift tax. Efficient for the Kim family. Less so for minority shareholders who are now along for the ride.

eMoney’s operating model reveals the purpose of the structure. The entity derives 48 to 60% of its revenue from group affiliates through work-steering, essentially billing other Daou subsidiaries for services at rates set by the controlling family. Those profits, plus dividends flowing down from Daou Data, fund further Daou Data share purchases, tightening the family’s grip. eMoney also engaged in what can only be described as intra-group asset flipping: it bought Kiwoom E&S shares for KRW 6.06 billion in 2015, inflated the subsidiary’s value through internal work-steering, then sold it to Daou Technology for KRW 9.65 billion in 2020. A 59% return generated entirely through circular transactions within the group. And then there was the crypto venture: eMoney invested in Netmarble-issued cryptocurrency tokens and lost approximately 93%, a KRW 1.5 billion write-down that tells you everything about the risk management culture at the apex of this pyramid.

Kim Dong-jun’s direct track record as a manager is, charitably, unproven. Kiwoom Investment’s 2022 revenue halved. Kiwoom PE posted a KRW 11.8 billion net loss in 2022 versus KRW 16.7 billion profit the prior year. Korean industry observers have described him as “not yet proven as a manager.” His elevation to co-chairman of the Kiwoom Securities board in June 2025 was widely characterised not as earned promotion but as the “finishing touch” of the succession plan, the final formal step in installing the second generation.

One common misconception among foreign investors is that the founder, Kim Ik-rae, has stepped away from the group. He has not. He was summoned by the Kim Gunhee special prosecutor in July 2025. He was reappointed as a non-standing director at both Saramin and Daou Data in 2025. He took a new board seat at Kiwoom F&I, prompting criticism in Korean media that he never truly stepped away despite his May 2023 resignation as group chairman. The founder’s fingerprints remain firmly on the operating decisions. The succession is about control transfer, not retirement.

Governance structure: the bare minimum, delivered grudgingly

Saramin’s board comprises four members. Four. Kim Ik-rae, the founder, sits as a non-standing director, the controlling family’s direct representative at the table. Hwang Hyun-soon (CEO) and Yoon Kuk-seop (corporate officer since 2020) are inside directors. Park Sang-jo is the sole likely independent outsider. This meets Korea’s bare minimum requirement of 25% outside directors (one of four), and I want to emphasise the phrase “bare minimum” because that is exactly how this board was designed: to comply with the letter of Korean governance law while providing as little genuine oversight as possible. The company uses a standalone auditor, Kwak Jin-cheol, aged 76, rather than an audit committee. Standard for smaller KOSDAQ companies. Still a governance weakness.

The voting arithmetic makes the picture worse. The combined Daou Group stake, Daou Technology at 32.59% plus Daou Data at 6.24% plus Kim Ik-rae personally at 3.10%, totals approximately 41.93% of shares outstanding. But with 10.34% held as non-voting treasury stock, the group controls roughly 46.8% of actual voting rights. Any activist who looked at this structure, and I suspect a few have, would immediately walk away. You cannot win a proxy fight against a 47% block. The controlling family is entrenched, and they know it.

Two absences tell you more than any disclosure filing could.

First, Saramin has not participated in Korea’s Corporate Value-Up programme, launched in 2024 as part of the government’s push to close the “Korea discount.” It has not filed a value-up plan. It was not included in the Korea Value-Up Index. For a company trading below book value, sitting on excess cash worth more than its market cap, and holding over 10% of its shares as un-cancelled treasury stock, the decision not to participate is a statement of intent. Management is telling you, through inaction, that closing the valuation gap is not a priority.

Second, sell-side analyst coverage has essentially evaporated. The last external report with a rating was from KB Securities in April 2023, nearly three years ago. At KRW 149 billion in market cap (~$103 million), Saramin falls below the coverage threshold for most Korean brokerages. This creates an information vacuum that perpetuates undervaluation: institutional investors who might otherwise take interest simply never see the name. For a value investor, this is actually part of the opportunity. For a governance optimist, it is one more reason nobody is holding management accountable.

On related-party transactions, I want to be fair: no egregious arm’s-length violations specific to Saramin were identified. The company uses Daou Technology’s mobile coupon and Kakao notification messaging services, a disclosed intra-group commercial relationship that appears routine. Dividends flow upward through the pyramid in the expected direction: Saramin to Daou Technology to Daou Data to eMoney to the Kim family. The plumbing works exactly as designed.

But the absence of a smoking gun is not the same as the absence of risk. The structure creates the permanent possibility of value extraction, and the group’s documented behaviour at other entities, work-steering revenue to eMoney, crypto speculation, intra-group asset flipping at non-market prices, provides clear evidence of willingness to prioritise family interests when it is convenient to do so. The question for Saramin shareholders is not whether this has happened yet. It is whether the KRW 140 billion in fresh cash on the balance sheet creates a temptation that the governance structure is simply not equipped to resist.

Capital allocation history: one brilliant trade, no shareholder-first culture

Saramin’s dividend record since IPO tells you what kind of company this is. The payout started at KRW 100 per share in 2013, crept up gradually to KRW 700 at the 2022 peak, then was cut back to KRW 500 as earnings declined. Cautious, incremental, and entirely lacking in ambition. At no point in the company’s history has management made a capital return decision that could be described as aggressive or shareholder-friendly. The dividend has always been a rounding error relative to the cash the business generates.

The one exception, and it is a significant one, was the Remember investment. Saramin deployed KRW 80 billion in late 2021 across a direct stake and an LP position in Ark & Partners’ fund, then exited to EQT in August 2025 for approximately KRW 172 billion gross, a clean 2.1x in under four years. This was a financial investment, not an operating acquisition, and it is the single strongest piece of evidence that someone inside Saramin can allocate capital intelligently.

But I want to be honest about the limits of this evidence. Making a successful financial trade, buying a minority stake and riding a multiple expansion, is a fundamentally different skill from operating capital allocation: investing in R&D, making accretive acquisitions, building new product lines. Saramin’s operating capital allocation track record, as I document in the governance section, is far less impressive. One excellent trade does not guarantee the next KRW 140 billion will be deployed wisely.

The other acquisitions barely register. Lara Job, operator of the Dongne Alba part-time job platform, was acquired for KRW 9.66 billion in July 2023 (87.85% stake). TopDev in Vietnam cost approximately $1 million in February 2020. Neither was material. Saramin has since absorbed Lara Job via a small-scale merger completed in February 2026, and previously absorbed subsidiary O2Job. These are routine operational consolidations, not strategic capital deployment.

Which brings us to the question that matters: what happens to the KRW 140 to 145 billion in post-tax Remember proceeds?

As of December 2025, the answer is: nothing. Management has offered only the vaguest of platitudes, “AI technology investment,” “discovery and M&A of promising companies,” and my personal favourite, “various plans for shareholder value enhancement,” a phrase so empty it could mean literally anything. No special dividend. No incremental buyback. No treasury share cancellation. Nothing concrete whatsoever.

Korean investor communities have noticed. One shareholder was quoted calling Saramin “the worst company for communication I’ve ever seen,” and I struggle to disagree. When you are sitting on a cash pile worth more than your entire market capitalisation and your stock is at multi-year lows, the minimum expectation is that you tell your shareholders what you intend to do. The silence is not neutral. It actively destroys confidence and contributes to the discount.

The company did execute a KRW 6 billion buyback program in late 2024 and a KRW 3 billion program in 2023. These were constructive but modest, and critically, whether those repurchased shares were cancelled or merely held as treasury stock could not be confirmed from public sources. This distinction matters enormously: treasury shares that are held but not cancelled can be reissued at any time, diluting existing shareholders. Total treasury holdings currently sit at approximately 10.34% of outstanding shares, a substantial block that remains un-cancelled. If management were serious about returning value, cancelling even half of this treasury position would be the single easiest, most immediate signal they could send. They have not done it.

Valuation: you are being offered the operating business for free

At KRW 14,220 per share (February 13, 2026), Saramin trades at a market capitalisation of KRW 149 billion. The headline multiples, P/E of 14.16x, P/B of 0.81 to 0.88x, dividend yield of 3.52%, are already optically cheap for a profitable platform duopolist. But the headline multiples are not the story. The balance sheet is.

Using our central estimate of approximately KRW 180 billion in net cash (gross Remember proceeds of ~KRW 172 billion, minus taxes, plus pre-existing cash), the adjusted total enterprise value of the core operating business is somewhere between KRW 0 and 30 billion, depending on your assumptions about pre-existing cash balances and precise tax leakage. Against last-twelve-month EBITDA of roughly KRW 26 billion, that implies an adjusted TEV/EBITDA of 0 to 1.1x.

I want you to sit with that number for a moment. Here is how it compares to every relevant transaction and trading comparable I could find:

Read that table again. Affinity paid 17x EBITDA for JobKorea, the other half of the exact same duopoly, in 2021. EQT paid 62 to 69x for Remember, a business that had barely reached monthly breakeven. SEEK, the closest global structural analogue (dominant local platform, linguistically distinct market, narrow moat), trades at 18x. And Saramin, Korea’s most-visited job platform, is available at somewhere between zero and one times EBITDA. The market is not just pessimistic. It is pricing this business as though it has no future whatsoever.

Even a take-private scenario illustrates the absurdity. At a 50% takeover premium (KRW 21,330 per share, total equity value of KRW 224 billion), the implied enterprise value of approximately KRW 79 billion would put the deal at roughly 3.0x EBITDA. That is still less than one-fifth of the multiple Affinity paid for JobKorea. Any private equity firm running a screen for negative-EV platform businesses in structurally protected markets would stop scrolling the moment they saw this. The reason it has not happened yet is the Daou Kiwoom group’s 47% voting control, which makes a hostile approach impossible and a friendly deal unlikely unless the family decides to sell.

For historical context, Saramin’s own P/E has ranged from roughly 10x at the 2014 trough to 40x+ at the 2021 peak. The current ~14x sits near the bottom decile of its trading history, and that is on trough earnings before adjusting for the cash pile. On a net-cash-adjusted basis, you are paying effectively nothing for the earnings stream.

For the record, the last active analyst coverage was KB Securities in April 2023, nearly three years ago, and Morningstar’s quantitative fair value estimate sits at KRW 41,930. I would not rely on either figure. The valuation case does not need third-party validation. The arithmetic speaks for itself.

Five scenarios that could permanently destroy the investment case

Every long thesis needs a pre-mortem. Here are the five ways this trade goes to zero, or close enough.

Scenario 1: Management squanders the cash. This is the risk that keeps me most honest. Saramin has KRW 140 to 145 billion in deployable capital and no stated plan. The Remember investment worked beautifully as a financial play, a clean 2x in four years, but the company has zero track record of successful operating M&A. If management pursues a “transformational” AI or platform acquisition at inflated multiples, or worse, directs capital toward group-adjacent opportunities that benefit the Daou pyramid more than Saramin shareholders, the excess cash cushion evaporates overnight. And the Daou group’s institutional risk tolerance is, to put it charitably, alarming: eMoney, the apex entity in the pyramid, invested in Netmarble-issued cryptocurrency tokens and lost approximately 93%. The KRW 140 billion could easily be wasted on two or three mediocre deals, converting a net-cash story into a conglomerate discount story. This is not a theoretical concern. It is the single most likely path to permanent value destruction.

Scenario 2: Korean demographics permanently shrink the TAM. Korea’s total fertility rate of 0.748 is the lowest on the planet. Working-age population is already declining by roughly 250,000 per year and is projected to fall 55% by 2072, from 36.6 million to 16.6 million. Youth employment (ages 15 to 29) declined for 19 consecutive months through November 2025. Companies cut hiring plans 12.1% year-on-year for Q4 2025 through Q1 2026. Manufacturing jobs have declined for 17 consecutive months, construction for 19. Korea crossed the “super-aged society” threshold (20%+ of the population aged 65 and over) in December 2024. If all of this translates into structurally lower job posting volumes rather than merely cyclical weakness, Saramin’s addressable market contracts permanently and no amount of valuation support matters. Immigration, currently just 3.4% of the labour force, would need to expand dramatically to offset the decline, and cultural resistance to large-scale immigration remains high. I do not think this kills the thesis on a three-to-five year horizon, but on a ten-year horizon, it is the most important variable in the entire analysis.

Scenario 3: AI platforms disintermediate traditional job boards. I covered the AI landscape in detail above, so I will not repeat it here. But the bear case deserves its own emphasis. The 7.42% of Saramin’s desktop referrals already coming from AI chatbots and tools is an early warning signal. If AI matching gets good enough to shift value from platforms with the most eyeballs to platforms with the best algorithms, Saramin’s $86 million revenue base may prove insufficient to fund the R&D necessary to compete. The critical variables to watch are the pace at which Korean-language AI capabilities improve and whether OpenAI, LinkedIn, or Remember can build the employer trust and regulatory compliance that currently protect incumbents.

Scenario 4: The controlling shareholder extracts value. The Daou Kiwoom group’s history provides ample precedent for concern, and I have laid this out extensively in the governance section. The headline items: Kim Ik-rae sold KRW 60.5 billion of Daou Data shares two trading days before the SG Securities crash (subsequently cleared by prosecutors, but reputationally devastating). His KRW 60.5 billion charitable donation pledge remains unfulfilled more than two years later. He has a 2002 conviction for nominee account share purchases. The eMoney pyramid was systematically constructed through below-market share transfers and intra-group work-steering. The group deliberately avoids formal holding company conversion by inflating debt, because Korean law prohibits holding companies from simultaneously controlling financial and non-financial subsidiaries. If Daou Data were ever forced to convert, Saramin’s ownership could be restructured in ways adverse to minorities. And here is the structural incentive problem: Kim Ik-rae’s 23% Daou Data stake faces an eventual inheritance tax liability of approximately 60%, creating powerful incentives for cash to flow upward through the pyramid via dividends rather than being returned to Saramin shareholders directly. The group does not need to steal. It merely needs to prioritise the family’s tax optimisation over minority returns, and the structure makes that trivially easy.

Scenario 5: The hiring market never recovers to 2019 levels. This is the subtle version of the demographic bear case, and arguably more dangerous because it does not require population collapse to materialise. December 2024 saw Korea’s first monthly net job loss (negative 52,000) since February 2021. Beyond cyclical weakness, structural shifts are underway: AI automation is reducing junior and entry-level roles, which are Saramin’s core job-seeker demographic. Companies are shifting to contract and project-based hiring, which reduces recurring job board usage. The preference for internal mobility over external recruitment is growing. If the combination of demographics and automation means peak Korean hiring volumes are permanently behind us, then Saramin is a structurally declining asset regardless of how cheap it looks today. The Bank of Korea has projected the economy could begin contracting absolutely by 2040 if the workforce decline continues at its current trajectory. I do not think we are there yet, but I want to be transparent that the base case requires a belief that Korean hiring volumes have a cyclical floor, not a secular one.

Our position

Despite everything above, we are long Saramin.

The negative enterprise value arithmetic, laid out in the valuation section, is too compelling to ignore. But a necessary caveat: negative enterprise value is only meaningful if the cash is accessible to shareholders.

For a company where the controlling family has 1.5% economic interest and zero history of large capital returns, the cash on the balance sheet is not equivalent to cash in your pocket. It is cash under someone else’s control, with their interests structurally misaligned from yours. The market’s implicit ~17% discount on the cash balance is not obviously irrational. But we think the discount is far too large, and the operating business, valued at zero, provides an enormous margin of safety even after applying a governance haircut.

Let me frame the upside across three scenarios, because the appropriate multiple for Saramin is genuinely debatable. SEEK trades at 15x EBITDA, Recruit at 20x+, and Affinity acquired JobKorea at 17x. But all three comparables are either growing, have demonstrably competent governance, or were acquired at a control premium. Saramin has declining revenue, terrible governance, and no acquisition premium. ZipRecruiter, a more sobering comparable, trades at 6 to 8x. A declining-revenue platform with governance risk in a demographically challenged market probably deserves something in between.

Bear case: operating profit stagnates at KRW 22 billion (2023 levels, no recovery), 6x multiple reflecting persistent governance discount and no capital return. Equity value including cash: ~KRW 312 billion, or ~KRW 30,000 per share. Upside: ~110%.

Base case: operating profit recovers to KRW 25 to 28 billion (85-90% of 2019 volumes, per our adjusted hiring assumption), 8 to 10x multiple. Equity value including cash: KRW 380 to 460 billion, or KRW 36,000 to 44,000 per share. Upside: 150 to 210%.

Bull case: operating profit recovers to KRW 30 to 32 billion (full 2019 normalisation plus pricing power), 12x multiple (re-rating driven by capital return announcement or take-private speculation). Equity value including cash: KRW 540 to 564 billion, or KRW 51,000 to 54,000 per share. Upside: 260 to 280%.

The critical observation is that even in the bear case, with no earnings recovery and a governance-discounted multiple, the cash cushion delivers over 100% upside. The thesis does not require the bull case to work. It requires the cash to exist, which it does, and for the market to eventually acknowledge it, which is the genuine uncertainty.

The downside is substantially cushioned by the net cash position. For the stock to decline significantly from here, you would need to believe management will actively destroy the cash (Scenario 1 in the pre-mortem) or that the core business will deteriorate so rapidly that operating losses consume the buffer. I have examined both risks above and believe neither is the base case, though the former is a genuine concern.

The key risk is not valuation. The key risk is governance. Everything documented in the sections above, the pyramid, the CEO appointment, the absence of analyst coverage and Value-Up participation, is the reason the stock trades where it does.

I am not dismissing this risk. But I think the market is pricing the uncertainty of when value will be realised as though value will never be realised. These are different things. A stock can trade at a persistent discount to intrinsic value indefinitely if there is no mechanism to close the gap, and this is the textbook Korea discount. Our thesis requires a belief that a mechanism will eventually emerge.

Even under the Daou Kiwoom group’s imperfect stewardship, Saramin has historically paid dividends, executed (modest) buybacks, and made one genuinely excellent financial investment in Remember that returned 2x in four years. The governance is bad. It is not uniquely predatory. And the gap between what this business is worth and what the market charges for it is wide enough to compensate for a meaningful governance discount.

The catalysts we are watching:

First, capital allocation. A special dividend of KRW 50+ billion or a buyback with cancellation representing 5%+ of outstanding shares would immediately surface value and signal management alignment. At current prices, a KRW 50 billion special dividend would represent approximately KRW 4,750 per share, a 33% yield on a single payment.

Second, Korean labour market data. The WorkNet openings-to-seekers ratio (currently ~0.40) is the single best leading indicator for Saramin’s revenue. Any sustained move above 0.50 would signal cyclical inflection.

Third, the 2026 hiring cycle. The February 2026 Incruit survey showing 73.4% of firms confirming new graduate hiring plans (up 7.9 percentage points year-on-year) is the first concrete sign of corporate hiring intent improving. If this translates into actual job postings on Saramin’s platform through H1 2026, operating results should begin to stabilise.

The honest admission: this is a position that requires patience and tolerance for Korean small-cap illiquidity (~24,000 shares daily volume). It is not a momentum trade. It is not going to work next week. It is a bet that cyclical mean reversion in one of Asia’s most depressed labour markets, combined with a cash-rich balance sheet at a negative enterprise value, eventually produces a re-rating, whether through improved operating results, capital returns, M&A (as a target, not an acquirer), or simply the passage of time.

Our base case hold period is three to five years, but the following conditions would trigger earlier reassessment. First, if no meaningful capital return is announced by the March 2027 AGM (one full year from now), the governance thesis weakens materially, as it would confirm that management intends to sit on or redeploy the cash without shareholder input. Second, if Saramin announces a large acquisition (KRW 50 billion+) at a price or in a sector that suggests empire-building rather than strategic logic. Third, if the WorkNet openings-to-seekers ratio fails to recover above 0.45 by mid-2027, suggesting the labor market weakness is structural rather than cyclical. Fourth, if AI-sourced referral traffic on Saramin exceeds 15% of total, up from the current 7.42%, indicating faster-than-expected platform disintermediation. Any one of these would trigger a fundamental review; two or more occurring simultaneously would likely prompt us to close the position.

The bear case is a Japan-style scenario: prolonged demographic deflation in the labour market, compounded by AI-native platforms eventually eroding the Korean-language moat, while the Daou Kiwoom group slowly extracts value through the pyramid rather than returning it to minorities. If you believe Korea is headed for decades of secular decline with no cyclical respite, this is not your trade. I do not believe that, at least not on a three-to-five year horizon.

The margin of safety is not in the earnings. It is in the cash. And as long as that cash exists, the downside is defined.

Asymmetry without a catalyst is just a value trap with better marketing. I think the catalysts are forming, slowly, and the price is wrong enough that I am willing to own it while I wait.
