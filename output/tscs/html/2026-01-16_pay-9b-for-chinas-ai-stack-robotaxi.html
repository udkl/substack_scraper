Part I: Culture Rot – Qu Jing Controversy

If you want to understand why Baidu struggles to execute, you don’t look at their balance sheet first. You look at their soul. And in May 2024, Baidu’s soul was bared for the world to see on Douyin (China’s TikTok).

The “Wolf” in the Henhouse

Qu Jing was Baidu’s Vice President and head of public relations. She was a serious operator, a former reporter for the state-backed Xinhua news agency, and crucially, an ex-employee of Huawei. Why does the Huawei connection matter? Because Huawei is famous for its “Wolf Culture”, a brutal, high-performance, military-style ethos where you sacrifice everything for the company. Baidu, by comparison, has historically been seen as the “geek” culture of China, softer, engineering-focused, perhaps a bit complacent.

Qu Jing apparently decided that what Baidu needed was a good old-fashioned dose of trauma. She launched a series of videos on her personal Douyin account that were intended, presumably, to style her as a “hard-nosed tech executive,” the kind of iron lady who gets things done. Instead, she came off as a sociopathic middle-manager.

In one video, she addressed the staff with a coldness that sent shivers down the spine of every “996” (9 am to 9 pm, 6 days a week) exhausted worker in China. Her quotes were instant classics of toxic management:

“I’m not even obligated to find out if you cried or had a quarrel [with your boyfriend or husband], which does not serve my concern as a supervisor. I’m not your mother-in-law, also not your mum.”

Let that sink in. This isn’t a leaked internal memo. This is the Head of Public Relations posting a public video, thinking, “Yeah, this makes me look good.”

She went further. She explicitly told staff not to “expect weekends off”. In another clip, she recounted a story about an employee who refused to take a 50-day business trip during the height of the Covid-19 pandemic, a time when travel in China meant risking draconian lockdowns and quarantine camps. Qu’s response to this employee’s refusal was a masterclass in lack of empathy:

“How dare you tell me that your husband can’t stand it and needs you back [home]? I’m 10 or 20 years older than you and have two kids...”

The implication was clear: I abandoned my family for the company, so you should too. If you don’t like it?

“If you are not satisfied with your job, you can resign. I will approve it immediately.”

She even threatened to use her industry influence to destroy the careers of those who crossed her, implying that a bad review from her would make them unemployable in the tight-knit Chinese tech sector. The videos were likely intended to style Qu as a hard-nosed executive who fully embraced the gung-ho working culture that has long prevailed in China’s technology industry, but they backfired spectacularly.

The Backlash

The reaction was nuclear. And I don’t use that word lightly.

The videos exploded on Weibo and Douyin. In an era where the Chinese youth unemployment rate is skyrocketing and the “lying flat” (tang ping) movement is gaining traction as a rejection of the rat race, Qu Jing became the avatar of everything wrong with Chinese capitalism.

The public didn’t see a “strong leader.” They saw a monster.

“Inhumane”: Users flooded the comments calling her working practices exploitative and “unbearable”.

Boycott Threats: Calls to boycott Baidu products began to trend. The brand damage was immediate. Some users even called for a boycott of the company’s products over its toxic working environment.

The Irony: People pointed out the irony that the Vice President in charge of Public Relations had single-handedly engineered the biggest PR crisis Baidu had faced in years. One netizen noted, “It’s quite a feat for a vice president in charge of the company’s public relations to create such a PR disaster.”

This was a revelation of the disconnect between Baidu’s upper management and the reality of the modern workforce. Baidu is trying to pivot to AI, a field that requires creativity, deep thinking, and retention of top talent. You don’t get breakthrough LLM research from people you are threatening to fire because they miss their husbands.

The Cleanup Operation

Baidu panicked. The company, already facing a declining search engine share and seeking an AI-centric transformation, looked to calm public outrage in the wake of Qu’s comments.

Qu Jing apologized on her WeChat Moments, a classic non-apology apology:

She claimed the videos “do not represent Baidu’s stance” and admitted she didn’t seek company permission before posting them. “My original idea was to do my job well, but I was too hasty and used inappropriate methods,” she wrote.

Hasty? Posting four highly edited, scripted videos is not “hasty.” It’s calculated stupidity.

Part II: “Delete A” and Document 79

If the Qu Jing story makes you want to short Baidu stock to zero, this next section is why you might want to mortgage your house to buy it. Baidu is operating in a rigged casino, and for once, the house is rigging it in their favor.

Document 79

In September 2022, the Chinese government issued a directive so sensitive that high-ranking officials were only shown the paper and not allowed to make copies. It is formally known as Document 79. Informally, in the halls of State-Owned Enterprises (SOEs), it is known as the “Delete A” directive.

“Delete A” stands for “Delete America”.

The order is simple and non-negotiable: State-owned companies in strategic sectors, finance, energy, telecommunications, transport, must replace all foreign IT software and hardware in their systems by 2027.

This is not a suggestion. It is a national security mandate.

The Scale of the Purge

We are talking about a total decoupling of the Chinese state economy from Western tech.

Hardware: Dell, Cisco, and IBM are being taken out of server rooms across the country. Dell’s market share in China has already almost halved in the past five years.

Software: Microsoft Windows and Office are the primary targets. The goal is to replace them with Linux-based alternatives like UOS (Unity Operating System).

Databases: Oracle is being shown the door. One of the last bastions of foreign tech profitability in the country is crumbling.

This directive expands a drive that is muscling U.S. technology out of the country. A Morgan Stanley poll of 135 CIOs in China found that many expected the share of computers powered by Windows to fall significantly over the next three years.

Why This Matters for Baidu

Baidu is positioned as one of the few domestic players capable of filling this void, mainly in the cloud and AI infrastructure layers.

Operating Systems & Cloud: As Microsoft is pushed out, Baidu Cloud is one of the few “safe” domestic alternatives that can scale to the level required by massive SOEs. Baidu is actively investing in its cloud infrastructure to absorb the funds released from failed deals like YY Live.

Office Software: Baidu has its own suite of collaborative tools (Baidu Hi, Baidu Drive) that are being retooled to capture the enterprise market fleeing Microsoft 365.

The “National Team” Status: Unlike ByteDance or Alibaba, Baidu has always maintained a closer, more subservient relationship with Beijing. They are often seen as the “State’s Search Engine.” In a “Delete A” world, being the teacher’s pet is a license to print money.

Billions of dollars of annual IT spend is forcibly changing hands from US firms to Chinese firms.

Baidu doesn’t have to be the best product in the world. It just has to be the best product that isn’t American. That is a very different, and much easier, bar to clear.

Part III: Moore Threads, Biren, and Chip Wars

The US government has effectively banned the export of advanced Nvidia chips (H100, A100) to China. This has created a vacuum that nature, and Chinese speculative capital, abhors. The result is a frenzy of investment into domestic GPU makers that borders on mass hysteria.

The “China’s Nvidia” Hype Train

Enter Moore Threads and Biren Technology. You might not have heard of them two years ago. Now, they are being valued like they discovered cold fusion.

Moore Threads: Founded by a former Nvidia China executive, this company is the current darling of the “patriotic capital” movement.

The IPO Madness: When Moore Threads listed on the Shanghai STAR Market, the IPO was oversubscribed 4,000 times. Retail investors were so desperate that the final allotment rate dropped to just 0.036%.

The Valuation: The company hit a market cap of nearly 282 billion yuan (approx $40 billion USD), making it the fifth most valuable company on the STAR Market.

The Financials (The Joke): They have lost 5 billion yuan in the last three years. They are not profitable. The IPO was priced at 123 times sales. Not earnings. Sales…

Biren Technology: Another contender, Biren listed in Hong Kong under “Chapter 18C,” a rule designed for pre-revenue tech firms.

Performance: Revenue of ~$59 million RMB in the first half of 2025. Loss of 1.6 billion RMB, widening by over 80% from the previous year.

The Market Reaction: It nearly doubled on its debut. Investors don’t care about the losses. They care that Biren has a chip (the BR100/BR200) that claims to rival Nvidia’s Ampere architecture.

Part IV: EV Story – Jiyue vs. Xiaomi

Baidu decided, like everyone else in China, that it needed to make cars. They partnered with Geely (who owns Volvo, Lotus, Zeekr) to create the Jiyue brand.

The Tech: It’s legit. Built on Geely’s SEA architecture (same as the excellent Zeekr 001). Baidu provides the brains: Apollo L4 autonomous driving, dual Nvidia Orin X chips providing 508 TOPS of computing power, and a “Simsmo” intelligent cockpit.

So why is it failing?

Xiaomi…

While Baidu was busy engineering a “robot car,” Xiaomi (the phone maker) was busy building a “dream car.” The Xiaomi SU7 launched and obliterated the market. It targeted the Porsche Taycan and Tesla Model 3. It sold out its entire 2024 production capacity in 24 hours.

The Comparison:

Xiaomi SU7: Positioned to “humiliate Porsche” with 1200 km - 643 km range and blistering speed. Lei Jun (Xiaomi CEO) is a rockstar who understands fan culture.

Jiyue 07: It has a “good chassis” and an intelligent cockpit. A blogger who sold their Jiyue 01 praised the cockpit as “top three” in the industry but noted the massive disconnect between product quality and brand heat.

Nobody cares about your “intelligent cockpit” if your brand has zero heat. The irony is that Jiyue actually has the better chassis heritage (Geely’s 18 billion yuan investment SEA architecture) , but Xiaomi won the “heart” of the consumer.

The “Shareholder Revolt”

The sales for Jiyue have been “mediocre” and the losses “serious.” Rumors are swirling that shareholders (Geely/Baidu) are “no longer willing to continue investing”. There is talk of restructuring. The “Robocar” dream is hitting the wall of consumer reality.

Baidu has the best self-driving tech in China, hands down. Jiyue CEO Xia Yiping even livestreamed a 24-hour, 2000km smart driving challenge where the system handled 99.5% of the driving through fog, rain, and complex urban roads. Unless Baidu can pivot Jiyue into a pure robotaxi fleet (Apollo Go) and stop trying to sell sedans to hipsters, this venture is a capital incinerator. This is a great activist play.

Part V: YY Live Acquisition Termination

Finally, we must address the $3.6 billion elephant in the room that vanished.

In 2020, Baidu announced it would buy YY Live (the domestic livestreaming business of JOYY Inc.) for $3.6 billion. At the time, it seemed like a desperate move to buy traffic. Livestreaming was hot. Baidu needed engagement.

The Freeze: The deal sat in regulatory purgatory for three years. The State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR) never signed off.

The Kill: In January 2024, Baidu formally terminated the deal. They cited “unmet conditions,” specifically that the closing was subject to regulatory approvals that had not been obtained by the long stop date of December 31, 2023. JOYY was furious. They threatened legal action, claiming the sale was “substantially completed” back in February 2021.

The Interpretation: Did Baidu fail to close, or did they choose to fail? I argue the latter. In 2020, livestreaming was the future. By 2024, livestreaming had become a regulatory minefield (crackdowns on “vulgarity” and tipping) and a low-margin business. Baidu had also launched “Baidu Youxuan,” its own e-commerce brand integrating livestreaming, making a dedicated platform like YY less critical.

Baidu realized: “Wait, we can just keep our $3.6 billion and spend it on AI instead.”

In fact, the $1.6 billion Baidu had already deposited into escrow was released back to them. Baidu explicitly stated it plans to invest such released funds into its cloud business and AI. This was a lucky escape. Baidu accidentally dodged a bullet because the government was too slow to approve the gun sale. Now, they have an extra $3.6B (gross) in liquidity to burn on chips and LLMs. It looks like incompetence, but it ended up being accidental brilliance.

The Bear Case

1. “Delete A” Is Not a Baidu Monopoly

Document 79 benefits everyone. ByteDance, Tencent, Alibaba, Huawei, and fifty other vendors are all fighting for the same displaced spend. Baidu has no exclusive claim to government IT budgets.

Worse: “Delete A” affects enterprise procurement. Baidu’s core business is consumer advertising. Policy tailwinds in one segment do not offset structural collapse in another.

Online marketing revenue: down 18% year-over-year in Q3 2025.

Douyin is the new front door to the Chinese internet. WeChat owns transactions. Baidu search is what you use when you have to, not when you want to. Management admits AI search monetisation “has yet to scale.”

May 2024: VP of Public Relations creates the company’s worst PR crisis in years. 60% of her team quits within months.

November 2024: Jiyue, a JV with Geely backed by billions, implodes. Employees protest unpaid wages. Rumours of a 7 billion RMB black hole. Allegations of procurement fraud. The CFO had already fled to the UK.

One incident is bad luck. Two incidents in six months is a pattern. If Baidu cannot prevent catastrophic failures in high-profile ventures with sophisticated partners, why trust them with AI monetisation?

4. Kunlunxin Is Not Nvidia

Kunlunxin chips are optimised for inference, not training. They work best for “government, telecom, and state-owned cloud users, where stable supply and lower cost matter more than top performance.”

Beijing is deliberately fragmenting chip procurement across Moore Threads, Biren, Cambricon, Huawei Ascend, and others. The state does not want a single winner.

Baidu’s 59% stake at a $3B valuation equals $1.8B. Even if it doubles, that is $3.6B in value creation. Meaningful. But not enough to offset an 18% annual decline in a $15B+ advertising business.

One shouldn’t dismiss rivals too quickly

ByteDance has native AI integration across Douyin. 700 million daily users. Baidu cannot match that distribution.

Alibaba’s Qwen models compete directly with Ernie. Tencent is everywhere. Huawei’s Ascend 910C reportedly matches Nvidia’s H100.

6. The “Utility” Thesis Is Actually Bearish

Utilities trade at low multiples for a reason: low growth, heavy regulation, capital intensity, political risk. If Baidu’s future is as a state-adjacent infrastructure provider, the correct valuation is a discount, not a premium.

You are not buying a tech company. You are buying a regulated quasi-SOE with a search engine attached.
