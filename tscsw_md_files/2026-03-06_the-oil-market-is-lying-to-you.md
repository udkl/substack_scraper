Why does this matter for the equity trade right now? Because Sinokor’s positioning is not a passive response to the Hormuz disruption. It is an active factor in how rates resolve. If Sinokor, which was already driving VLCC rates to $200,000+ per day before the conflict, continues to hold back tonnage from the spot market (a rational strategy for an operator that has priced in a prolonged disruption), the rate signal for the rest of the VLCC fleet amplifies beyond what the physical disruption alone would justify. Conversely, if Sinokor floods the market with spot supply on the assumption that the disruption resolves quickly, rates compress and the trade reverses. The Sinokor positioning is therefore both the primary upside catalyst and the primary downside risk in the tanker trade. Watching Sinokor’s vessel deployment data through sources like Signal Group and Kpler is more important than watching headline VLCC rates.

Now here is the equity map across Tier 2 and Tier 3, because the transmission mechanisms are different.

**Under Tier 2 (base case, 45%), the tanker trade has two components.**

The first is pure rate capture. Brent at $80-95 and Hormuz running at reduced capacity for weeks means VLCC rates sustain above $200,000 per day. The operators with the most spot market exposure, rather than time charter cover, capture this in real earnings. The names with the highest spot market exposure in the current fleet are Frontline (41 VLCCs, predominantly spot-oriented), DHT Holdings (22 VLCCs at year-end 2025, expanding to 26 by mid-2026 as four newbuildings deliver, well-known for spot exposure and a history of aggressive dividend payouts when rates spike), and International Seaways (a smaller fleet but with meaningful spot exposure and the added optionality of its product tanker segment as product dislocations compound the crude rerouting). All three moved on Monday and all three have room to run further if rates sustain. The key metric to watch for each: the percentage of fleet on spot versus time charter for Q2 2026. Spot-oriented operators have unlimited upside at $200,000+ rates. Time-chartered operators have already sold their optionality.

The second component is war risk premium. Operators willing to call the Gulf at a premium earn surcharges, but any vessel that takes a hit is a balance sheet event, not a P&L event. The right names here are the operators with the best risk management track records (Frontline’s 2019 operational history, DHT’s transparent routing disclosure), not the ones with the most aggressive premium marketing.

**Under Tier 3 (tail risk, now 30%), the tanker trade transforms.**

At Tier 3, rates do not sustain at $200,000 per day. They spike to $400,000 or beyond, which is where the 2020 oil price war briefly sent them. The names that benefit most from a true Tier 3 rate environment are not the large diversified operators (Frontline, Euronav) but the smaller, leaner spot operators with high operational leverage. DHT becomes the most interesting name in a Tier 3 scenario: 22 VLCCs with four newbuildings delivering through June, minimal debt, historically disciplined about dividend payouts that track spot earnings almost directly. At $400,000 per day rates, DHT’s per-share earnings power over a single quarter approaches levels the market has not priced. Nordic American Tankers (Suezmax focused, not VLCC) is more leveraged to a full Hormuz closure because Suezmax vessels are the natural alternative for cargoes seeking to leave the Gulf via Cape of Good Hope rerouting when VLCCs cannot transit. NAT’s 20% overnight move was not irrational: it was the market pricing exactly this rerouting premium.

One structural point most coverage has missed. The approximately 200 million barrels of Iranian crude floating at sea (Kpler data, as of February 27) provides Chinese refiners with roughly four months of buffer. This is bearish for crude prices in the short term (Chinese spot demand does not spike) but separately bullish for tanker rates: every barrel in floating storage is a vessel not in the spot market. The 200 million barrel buffer absorbs tanker capacity rather than releasing it. You can be short-term bearish on Brent and structurally bullish on tanker rates simultaneously, and that is the correct framing for the next 60 days.

The critical risk to all of the above: the Trump escort announcement. If US Navy escorts become a credible and sustained operational reality (not just an announcement), the war risk premium for Hormuz transit compresses, spot rates normalize from elevated levels, and the tanker trade gives back the premium. The 20% overnight NAT move was largely given back by Monday’s close, which suggests the market has already started pricing some probability of credible escort operations. Watch whether any commercial tanker actually transits under Navy escort in the next 72 hours. If the first convoy goes through without incident, the Tier 3 tail in tankers compresses materially.

## **The E&P Filter: Hedge Book as the Only Variable That Matters**

Exxon Mobil rose 4.1% in pre-market trading Monday, and Chevron was seen up 3.9%. These are integrated majors with diversified earnings streams, so the oil price move is only one of several inputs to their valuation. For pure-play E&Ps, the analysis is simpler and more brutal: at $80-95 Brent under Tier 2, the earnings accretion from the oil price move is not shared equally. It is filtered, almost entirely, through the hedge book. An E&P that locked in $65 Brent for 60% of its 2026 production has already sold the upside

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Ga3k!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fefd82fe8-407a-4e03-afc7-1b87964ae3a1_1405x738.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Ga3k!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fefd82fe8-407a-4e03-afc7-1b87964ae3a1_1405x738.png)

An E&P that is 70% unhedged at current levels is printing money at $85 Brent. The spread between these two cases is not marginal. It is the difference between a 20% upside and a 60% upside. The hedge book is the only variable that matters. Everything else is noise.

The consolidation wave (Exxon/Pioneer, Chevron/Hess, COP/Marathon, Diamondback/Endeavor, Devon/Coterra at $58 billion) has concentrated the E&P universe into four super-independents, all operating under explicit “value over volume” mandates. The structural consequence for this analysis: consolidation has pushed hedge ratios down. Large, investment-grade E&Ps with strong balance sheets have less incentive to hedge aggressively, and the fiscal 2025 10-K filings confirm this pattern.

Diamondback’s Q4 2025 filing (February 25, 2026) shows an unhedged realized oil price of $58.00 per barrel versus a hedged price of $57.07, a differential of only $0.93, indicating an extremely light hedge book entering 2026. EOG’s derivative cash settlements ran between $21 million and $38 million per quarter through 2025 against quarterly operating cash flow exceeding $2.5 billion, confirming that derivatives are a rounding error in EOG’s earnings profile. ConocoPhillips has historically maintained a minimal hedging philosophy, relying on balance sheet strength and geographic diversification rather than financial instruments. Devon and Coterra each maintained separate hedging programs pre-merger, and the combined hedge book will not be disclosed until the first post-close earnings release, likely in Q3 2026.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mq_O!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe972a295-ba8f-400c-b15b-88d97c028f19_1405x767.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mq_O!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe972a295-ba8f-400c-b15b-88d97c028f19_1405x767.png)

The pattern is clear: the Big Four are entering this disruption largely unhedged. In the current environment, the leverage in an oil price spike therefore sits with the larger, less-hedged operators. That is a structural shift from the pre-consolidation shale era, where the most leveraged names had the most upside but also the most downside. The Big Four have the upside without (most of) the downside.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!N69V!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fddb8790b-2e76-4fc5-9b6a-17b5635dd710_1405x773.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!N69V!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fddb8790b-2e76-4fc5-9b6a-17b5635dd710_1405x773.png)

Here is how I map the Big Four across Tier 2.

**ConocoPhillips (COP)** is the name you buy when you want the upside but also want to sleep. Historically minimal hedging philosophy, highest credit rating in the independent E&P universe, lowest production costs, most geographically diversified asset base. Under Tier 2, COP captures $80-95 Brent on a large, low-cost base with virtually no hedge book drag. Here is the irony nobody is talking about: COP has meaningful Qatar exposure, and Qatar just went offline. The “defensive” E&P has a non-obvious offensive risk sitting inside its international earnings mix. That does not make it a bad trade. It makes it a trade you need to understand before you size.

**Diamondback Energy (FANG)** is the purest expression of “I want to own the Permian at $85 Brent with nothing in the way.” Post-Endeavor, approximately 840,000 net acres across Midland and Delaware with production above 500,000 boe/d. The hedge book is effectively nonexistent: Q4 2025 filing shows an unhedged realized oil price of $58.00 versus a hedged price of $57.07. That is a $0.93 differential. Diamondback’s management looked at the forward curve and said: we do not need protection. At $85 Brent, every incremental dollar flows to buybacks and special dividends. FANG is the cleanest Big Four leverage to this specific disruption.

**EOG Resources (EOG)** is the one you own across cycles, not because of any single quarter. Premium drilling program targets 30%+ direct after-tax returns at $40 oil, which tells you everything about why the hedge book is light: when your breakeven is $40, hedging at $65 is giving away money you do not need to give away. Derivative cash settlements ran $21-38 million per quarter through 2025 against quarterly operating cash flow exceeding $2.5 billion. That is not a hedge book. That is a rounding error. The part the market is underpricing right now: EOG carries meaningful natural gas exposure, and with QatarEnergy offline and the JKM-Henry Hub spread blown wide open, EOG’s gas volumes are quietly repricing upward while everyone focuses on oil. Oil upside plus embedded gas optionality. That is EOG at $85 Brent.

**Devon Energy / Devon-Coterra (DVN)** is the one I would be most cautious about sizing near-term. The $58 billion merger closing in Q2 2026 means the combined hedge book will reflect two separate programs, and deal-close hedges in large upstream mergers tend to be conservative. Asset quality is undeniable: 27% of combined locations at or below $40 breakeven, Mizuho estimates approximately 21 years of inventory. But how much of Q2’s windfall reaches earnings versus getting absorbed by deal-close hedges? Watch the first combined earnings call for the H2 2026 hedge percentage before sizing aggressively.

The most interesting E&P expression for Tier 2 is not the Big Four. It is the mid-cap names with light hedge books and concentrated Permian exposure. The analytical filter is straightforward: companies that entered 2026 with less than 40% of production hedged above $70, with low leverage and no pending deals.

I have now run the screen against Q4 2025 10-K filings. Two names illustrate both sides of the hedge book filter.

Permian Resources (PR) passes the filter cleanly. Management’s stated hedging targets are 30%, 20%, 10% for years one, two, and three out, and on the Q4 2025 earnings call they confirmed the 2026 book sits below those targets. As of mid-2025, PR had 29,500 bbl/d of oil hedged for full-year 2026 at a weighted average fixed price of $66.12 per barrel. Against approximately 190,000 bbl/d of oil production, that is roughly 15-16% hedged for 2026, one of the lightest books in the Permian mid-cap universe. Leverage sits at 0.8x, approaching investment grade. Concentrated Delaware Basin exposure, no pending transformational deal, and a management team that explicitly described their hedging philosophy as “opportunistic, not programmatic.” At $85 Brent, PR captures the full price on approximately 85% of production. That is as clean an expression of the hedge book filter as exists in the public E&P universe right now.

Matador Resources (MTDR) is what the wrong side of the filter looks like. Matador hedged approximately 50% of its projected 2026 oil production using costless collars at a weighted average floor of approximately $53 per barrel and a ceiling of approximately $66 per barrel. At $85 Brent, half of Matador’s production caps out at $66 realisations. The company is well-run, the Delaware Basin assets are high-quality, but the hedge book is doing exactly what it was designed to do: protecting the balance sheet at the cost of upside capture. PR at $85 Brent captures the full move on 85% of volumes. Matador captures it on 50%, with a $19 per barrel give-back on the hedged portion. That spread, compounded across a full quarter, is the difference between a stock that re-rates and a stock that merely reports good earnings.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!cTzt!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5b292fb7-2551-4f22-9107-7d883de19765_1405x649.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!cTzt!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5b292fb7-2551-4f22-9107-7d883de19765_1405x649.png)

One structural counterweight. The EIA’s January 2026 STEO forecasts US crude production at approximately 13.6 million barrels per day, essentially flat with the 2025 record. But that was compiled before Epic Fury, and the price spike is triggering producer hedging at $70+, exactly the conditions that incentivise marginal production increases. More US barrels hedged at $70+ means more supply in H2 2026, capping the duration of any Brent floor above $80. The earnings torque is real in Q2, but the production response is already being locked in for Q3 and Q4.

One genuinely contrarian point worth sitting with before sizing any E&P position. If you own energy stocks, you do not want an oil price spike. I know that sounds wrong. Sit with it. Price spikes force government action: the US and China release SPR reserves, demand gets hit, and investors, critically, refuse to capitalise geopolitically-driven price spikes into long-term valuation multiples. We have a clean historical analogue. In 2022, when Russia’s invasion of Ukraine sent Brent above $120, E&P stocks underperformed the oil price move by roughly 30% on a 12-month basis. The market looked at $120 Brent and said: that is not a new floor, that is a spike, and I am not paying 6x cash flow for a spike. The same logic applies now. Current prices, $80-85 Brent, are actually the ideal scenario for E&P equity holders: high enough to drive exceptional free cash flow, low enough to avoid triggering the political and demand-destruction responses that cap the trade. A spike to $100+ is good for E&P earnings for one quarter, then potentially bad for E&P equities for two years of valuation multiple compression and demand destruction. Tier 3 E&P positioning therefore needs to be sized as an earnings event hedge, not as a structural long.

For Tier 3 E&P expression: the answer is not individual stocks. It is XLE (the Energy Select Sector SPDR).

XLE saw significant inflows in the first hour of Monday’s session.

At Tier 3 ($100+ Brent), the ETF captures the entire sector reprice without requiring you to pick individual hedge books in real time. The Tier 3 E&P trade is a sector trade, not a stock trade. Size it accordingly.

## **The LNG Trade: The Disruption the Oil Market Missed**

This section was not in the original Epic Fury note, and its absence was the most substantive gap in the framework. It requires its own treatment now.

QatarEnergy is the world’s largest LNG producer, accounting for approximately 20% of global LNG exports with annual production capacity of roughly 77 million metric tonnes (Reuters, OilPrice), operating 14 liquefaction trains at Ras Laffan.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5NRv!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F59b3b6dc-dd66-4ee5-9cc2-1d1e6fa49c88_1406x594.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5NRv!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F59b3b6dc-dd66-4ee5-9cc2-1d1e6fa49c88_1406x594.png)

On March 2, Iranian drone strikes hit the Ras Laffan Industrial City and Mesaieed Industrial City, the twin pillars of Qatari LNG output. QatarEnergy declared force majeure and ceased all LNG production. The same day, Dutch TTF benchmark gas futures surged more than 50% and Asian JKM prices jumped approximately 39%. Bloomberg Terminal data tells the story more precisely: the JKM front-month contract (JGL1) was trading at 1,659 cents per MMBtu on February 27. By March 3, it had hit 2,433. On March 4, it peaked at 3,165 ($31.65 per MMBtu), a 91% spike from pre-conflict levels. It settled at 2,736 on March 5. The one-year average was $17.54. This is a two-standard-deviation event in LNG pricing.

More revealing than the outright price is the JKM-TTF spread (AJTTM1 on Bloomberg). Through most of January and February, this spread was negative, meaning European gas was trading at a premium to Asian LNG. On January 23, the spread hit -$4.10 per MMBtu. Then on March 2 it flipped positive at +$1.33. On March 3 it detonated to +$6.68. It sits at approximately +$4.12 as of March 5. That is a $10.78 swing from trough to peak. The interpretation is unambiguous: the Qatar shutdown redirected global LNG scarcity from Europe to Asia in a single trading session. Asian buyers, who were the primary offtakers of Qatari LNG, are now competing against European buyers for a structurally smaller pool of available cargoes. The premium has flipped hemispheres.

Meanwhile, Henry Hub (NG1) sits at $2.95, essentially unmoved. US domestic gas supply is not disrupted.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!xbq5!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F516e30fb-5f1c-4dbc-9d68-71ee4fb54eee_1405x780.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!xbq5!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F516e30fb-5f1c-4dbc-9d68-71ee4fb54eee_1405x780.png)

European natural gas, which had been tracking toward seasonal normalization, repriced as a structurally different commodity overnight.

The LNG trade is not a derivative of the oil trade. It is a separate disruption with a separate duration logic. Restarting an LNG liquefaction train after a forced shutdown is not the same as reopening a shipping lane. The thermodynamics of cooling and inspecting a liquefaction complex mean the restart process alone takes at minimum two weeks, with full capacity restoration potentially requiring four to six weeks even if no additional damage is assessed. Rystad estimates that a four-to-five week full interruption translates to a loss of approximately 11.2 million metric tonnes for the full year 2026. For context, that is a structural supply loss, not a voyage delay.

The equity expression of this disruption runs through two distinct groups.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!yV_y!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F323e9dcf-9e21-414d-a94b-de62c37992be_1406x712.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!yV_y!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F323e9dcf-9e21-414d-a94b-de62c37992be_1406x712.png)

The primary beneficiaries are US LNG exporters. Cheniere Energy (LNG) gained approximately 6% on Monday and Venture Global (VG) surged nearly 20%. The mechanism is direct: with Qatar offline, US Gulf Coast terminals operating at 90-95% capacity utilization can redirect spot cargoes toward the highest-priced markets in the world. Cheniere’s ~90% long-term contracted model limits spot upside but its Corpus Christi expansion adds incremental volume at exceptional pricing. Venture Global, with more spot exposure, is the higher-beta expression. The critical constraint: US export capacity is near full utilization. The upside is capped by infrastructure, not demand.

The upstream gas beneficiaries are worth identifying separately. EQT Corporation, the largest Appalachian natural gas producer, and Antero Resources are both structurally exposed to Henry Hub price uplift as US LNG export demand pulls Appalachian basin gas harder. These are not geopolitical plays in the traditional sense. They are domestic production beneficiaries of an international supply shock that raises the floor for all US gas pricing. The pricing dislocation is staring at you from the screen. Henry Hub at $2.95, JKM at $27.36, TTF surging 50% in two sessions. US domestic gas is not disrupted. International gas is in crisis. The JKM-Henry Hub spread blowing out while Henry Hub stays anchored is telling you exactly where the arbitrage sits: US exporters are printing money on a widening intercontinental spread at near-full capacity utilisation.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!C-os!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8c764953-bcf8-4566-a3a4-34122d33ad45_1405x728.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!C-os!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8c764953-bcf8-4566-a3a4-34122d33ad45_1405x728.png)

The single most important variable for the LNG equity trade is not Hormuz transit volume. It is the Ras Laffan damage assessment. If the drone strike caused only superficial infrastructure damage, Qatar restarts within two to three weeks and the TTF spike partially reverses. If the liquefaction trains themselves sustained material damage, Qatar is offline for months and the structural reallocation of global LNG flows has just become permanent for 2026. Watch for independent engineering assessments of Ras Laffan, not just QatarEnergy’s public statements, which will be conservative on timing for commercial and political reasons.

## **What Loses: Airlines, Consumer, and the Rerouting Tax**

I want to be honest about why the “what loses” section matters beyond the obvious short thesis. Most equity notes in geopolitical disruption events cover the obvious shorts as a box-checking exercise. The airlines are down, hotels are down, cruise lines are down, everyone knows. The analytically useful work is in identifying where the damage is uneven, where the market has either overcorrected or not corrected enough, and where the duration of the disruption changes the calculus.

**Airlines: Three Separate Businesses Facing the Same Problem**

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!tBZg!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1fe370a8-7cef-4c94-8211-8af84e2bb9f3_1404x689.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!tBZg!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1fe370a8-7cef-4c94-8211-8af84e2bb9f3_1404x689.png)

United Airlines tumbled 8.4% on Friday pre-market, Delta was down 6.6%, American Airlines shed 6.2%, and Southwest slipped 3.3%. The US Global Jets ETF fell 4% on the day. By Monday, the moves had partly normalized: US carriers ended down approximately 2-4% at the close as oil pulled back from its intraday highs. The market is treating the airlines as a homogeneous sector. They are not.

The key analytical distinction is fuel hedging. Most US carriers abandoned hedging over the last decade, including Southwest, once the industry’s most celebrated hedger. Delta, United, and American are all unhedged or minimally hedged. That decision, justified by a decade of stable oil markets, is now being repriced.

Jefferies estimates (with the same caveat on sell-side positioning incentives that applies throughout this note) that each 5% change in fuel costs translates to a 5% to 10% impact on Delta’s and United’s 2026 earnings per share. For American, it represents approximately a 35% impact in either direction.

This differential is the most important number in the airline analysis. American’s sensitivity is not merely “higher than peers.” It is categorically different. A 10% fuel cost increase, which is a conservative assumption at current Brent levels versus where airline fuel costs were priced for 2026, does not move the dial for Delta and United in a way that threatens their business models. For American, it does.

Delta’s latest filing implies a 10% fuel price jump adds roughly $1 billion to its 2026 expense base, against 2025 net income of approximately $3.5 billion. Material but survivable. American’s leverage is structurally different: thinner net income, more stretched balance sheet, and fuel cost sensitivity roughly 3.5x higher on a percentage basis.

The second airline variable is airspace disruption. Major Gulf hubs including Dubai, Doha, and Abu Dhabi have suspended operations for several days, canceling over 12,300 flights across seven airports and leaving hundreds of thousands of passengers stranded. Airlines are being forced to reroute through narrow corridors, including the Caucasus route, increasing operational complexity, fuel burn, and costs. US airlines have “minimal” direct exposure to Middle East travel, with the region accounting for less than 1% of planned first-quarter capacity for American, United, and Delta, according to Jefferies. The airspace disruption therefore hurts US carriers primarily through rerouting costs, not through direct route cancellation. The hurt to European carriers, particularly Gulf-dependent hubs like IAG and Lufthansa, is more direct.

The asymmetric trade in airlines: American is the most vulnerable, Delta is the most resilient, and neither the short nor the long is as clean as the Monday moves suggested. American’s 35% EPS sensitivity to fuel costs means that at sustained $85+ Brent, the stock is pricing in a scenario that could compress already-thin earnings to zero or negative. That is a legitimate short thesis on duration, not on the first week of the conflict. Delta, with its superior fuel cost structure, stronger balance sheet, and diversified revenue streams (particularly its co-brand credit card with American Express, which is a high-margin, fuel-insensitive revenue source), is the most defensible US airline under Tier 2. The spread trade (long Delta, short American) has been discussed in airline circles before: the current context may be the cleanest entry point in years.

Ryanair CEO Michael O’Leary confirmed the airline is hedged for the next 12 months at approximately $67 per barrel, and its stock fell only 2.2% despite the broader airline selloff. This is the cleanest illustration of the hedging divide. Ryanair made a specific financial decision: to pay for certainty rather than hope for stability. That decision looks extraordinarily prescient right now. No US major carrier made the same decision.

InterContinental Hotels Group fell as much as 6.2% in London. French peer Accor plunged 11%. Royal Caribbean Cruises dropped 3% and Carnival Corp. lost more than 7%. These are the obvious moves. The more analytically interesting signal is in what they imply about duration. Hotel and cruise line stocks do not trade down 7-11% on a one-week geopolitical event. They trade down that much when the market believes the disruption persists long enough to affect forward bookings, not just current operations.

Carnival and Royal Caribbean have essentially no physical exposure to the Middle East. Their sell-off is driven by bunker fuel costs and consumer confidence. Under Tier 2, the bunker cost impact is manageable. The consumer confidence channel is the one that matters: if $85+ Brent flows through to $3.50-4.00 gasoline within 60 days, hotel and cruise line shorts extend rather than bounce.

The CF Industries (CF) move is worth flagging as a non-obvious read-through. CF Industries soared 8.3% on Monday. Iran is a major exporter of urea and nitrogen fertilizers, and the supply disruption benefits CF, which produces competing product. This is the kind of second-order trade that most equity allocators miss in the first 48 hours, and the earnings support is structural as long as Iranian exports remain offline.

CF is not the only second-order read-through. QatarEnergy’s shutdown cascaded downstream beyond LNG. The company subsequently announced the halt of additional downstream products including urea, polymers, methanol, and aluminum. That last item deserves its own note. Aluminum prices on the London Metal Exchange rallied as much as 3.8% following the announcement. QatarEnergy holds a 50% stake in Qatalum alongside joint-venture partner Norsk Hydro, making Norsk Hydro the direct equity expression of the Qatar aluminum disruption. The Hydro angle is not a crowded trade; almost no equity coverage has connected the QatarEnergy downstream cascade to Norsk Hydro’s production exposure. That spread between where the news is and where the equity is tends to close.

The unifying logic across CF, Norsk Hydro, and the LNG upstream names is simple: Iran and Qatar together account for significant global market share in urea, aluminum, LNG, and petrochemicals. The disruption is not an oil story. It is a Middle Eastern industrial output story that is being filtered through an oil price lens by most analysts. The second-order commodity trades exist precisely because of that analytical filter.

**Freight: The Compounding Rerouting Cost**

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!I1Mp!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3cf39658-f3ee-4502-8cf3-57b4fa93075c_1403x707.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!I1Mp!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3cf39658-f3ee-4502-8cf3-57b4fa93075c_1403x707.png)

The closing of Middle East airspace also threatens the margins of freight carriers such as FedEx, UPS, and DHL, as longer transit times drive up fuel costs. The rerouting of vessels around Africa instead of through Suez (already a default for some operators since the Houthi campaign) compounds this. About 170 container ships reported delays as owners halted sailings and rerouted Europe-bound vessels around Africa. The freight carriers face a double hit: higher jet fuel costs for air freight, and longer sea freight routes for ocean-going cargo. Neither move is catastrophic independently, but they are additive, and the longer the disruption persists, the more the market will start pricing structural rerouting costs rather than temporary disruption premiums.

## **Tier 3 Hedges: How to Express the Tail Without Owning It**

I said in the original piece that Tier 3 requires options, not outright positions. I stand by that. Here is the more precise framework.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!lNS2!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe92db6fa-3a06-4a9e-96b0-7a0575b5394c_1404x679.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!lNS2!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe92db6fa-3a06-4a9e-96b0-7a0575b5394c_1404x679.png)

Tier 3 ($100+ Brent, dual chokepoint, weeks of Hormuz closure) is not the base case. At a 30% probability, it is the tail. The error most allocators make in tail scenarios is to either ignore them entirely (because the base case does not include them) or to size as if they are certainties (because the narrative is compelling). Neither is correct.

The correct expression: out-of-the-money Brent calls for Q2 and Q3 2026 expiry. The OVX (CBOE Crude Oil Volatility Index) was at 58.82 at the time of the original piece. It has since moved to the mid-70s, roughly 30% higher. Implied volatility is expensive in absolute terms. Here is what that means in concrete numbers.

A $100-strike June 2026 Brent call, at roughly 70% annualized implied vol (which approximates the current Brent smile given OVX in the 73-78 range), costs approximately $5.50 per barrel. That call breaks even at expiry around $105-106 Brent. A $110-strike June call costs roughly $3.50 per barrel and breaks even around $113-114. These are approximate figures derived from Black-Scholes using current spot (~$81), three months to expiry, and the prevailing vol surface; readers should verify against live ICE Brent options pricing before executing.

The break-even numbers tell you exactly what you are paying for. The $100-strike call requires Brent to reach the lower end of the Tier 3 range just to recover the premium. It does not make money at $100; it makes money at $106+. The $110-strike call is cheaper but requires a deeper move into Tier 3 to pay off. In both cases, the premium is the maximum loss and the payoff above the break-even is non-linear, which is the definition of the convexity you are buying. If Tier 3 does not materialise, the premium is lost in full. If it does, and Brent reaches $115-120 on a sustained dual-chokepoint scenario, the $100 call is worth $15-20 per barrel against a $5.50 cost, a 3-4x return on premium. That is the risk-reward. Size it as a hedge, not as a position.

For equities specifically, the Tier 3 expression that is currently most attractive is the tanker sector through short-dated options rather than outright positions. Here is why: if the Trump escort announcement is credible and Hormuz reopens within two weeks, the tanker stocks give back 30-40% of their post-strike moves rapidly. Owning outright tanker stock at Monday’s elevated prices is a crowded trade with asymmetric downside if Tier 1 resolution arrives unexpectedly. Owning call options on DHT or Frontline for April and May expiry gives you the same Tier 3 upside (which would see these stocks double from current levels on sustained $400,000+ day VLCC rates) without the full downside exposure to a rapid de-escalation.

The NAT trade is somewhat different. As a Suezmax operator, NAT benefits from a Cape of Good Hope rerouting scenario that does not require Hormuz to be open. The rerouting demand for Suezmax vessels exists regardless of whether the Strait reopens, as long as the conflict creates enough risk aversion to make the longer route economically rational. This is a lower-delta, more durable trade than pure VLCC exposure and it is less dependent on the Tier 3 binary.

For consumer shorts in Tier 3: American Airlines is the cleanest equity expression. Its 35% EPS sensitivity to fuel costs means that at $100+ Brent, with no hedge book, the earnings case for the stock approaches zero. The position sizes itself: this is not a high-conviction short at current prices because the stock has already moved down 6-8% from the pre-conflict level. It is a short if the conflict persists for three to four weeks and Brent sustains above $90, at which point American’s Q2 guidance withdrawal becomes a near-certainty.

## **The Five Numbers That Determine Which Scenario Wins**

I am going to close with the same structure I used in the original piece: the specific data points that will tell you whether we are tracking toward Tier 1, Tier 2, or Tier 3. Not the narrative. The numbers.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!g7M4!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd853bfda-4a66-4248-8dc9-47d29dd99793_1404x724.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!g7M4!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd853bfda-4a66-4248-8dc9-47d29dd99793_1404x724.png)

**1. Daily Hormuz tanker transits (Kpler, Vortexa,** **JMIC).** The transit data has deteriorated materially since the original framework was published. On February 28, approximately 100 ships transited in total, with 56 tankers observed by 1800 UTC, followed by a sharp collapse. On March 1, JMIC recorded three oil tankers, an approximately 94% fall from the prior day and an 86% drop against the 2026 daily average of 19.8 million barrels per day per Kpler. On March 2, traffic was near-zero. On March 3, JMIC recorded zero tanker transits and only four vessels of any type. On March 4, IRGC formally declared full control of the Strait, and Kpler’s principal freight analyst confirmed that tanker traffic had fallen 90% from pre-conflict levels, with some residual movement occurring under AIS blackout. As of March 5, P&I insurance has been formally cancelled by major clubs including Gard, Skuld, NorthStandard, and the London P&I Club, and the Strait is functionally closed in commercial terms regardless of its formal legal status. The goalposts for the five-number framework have therefore shifted. The Tier 1 recovery signal is now 30+ tankers per day sustained for 72 hours, not just a single day. The current data puts us well inside Tier 2 entrenching toward Tier 3, not at the ambiguous boundary between them.

**2. Kharg Island damage assessment (satellite imagery).** This is the single most important variable in the oil supply analysis and it has not been definitively resolved. My provisional assessment of Kharg Island as non-operational requires a specific correction. EU Copernicus Sentinel satellite imagery published by Bloomberg, timed at 10:47 a.m. local time on March 2, shows a VLCC moored at a loading jetty on the eastern side of the island, two days after the initial strikes. Loading operations were confirmed operational as of that date. No satellite imagery has been published covering Kharg Island since March 2, and no independent engineering assessment of the terminal’s current structural status has been released. The honest assessment as of March 5: the terminal was loading on March 2, its status after subsequent strike activity is unknown, and the “non-operational” framing I used was premature. These are two different supply scenarios with different duration profiles. If loading infrastructure is intact, the supply disruption is measured in days. If subsea pipelines or booster stations were struck, it extends for months. I do not have direct access to Planet Labs or commercial satellite feeds; this assessment is based on force majeure declarations, QatarEnergy’s response, and secondary reporting. It should be treated as a working hypothesis. Watch for credible satellite imagery from CSIS, Planet Labs, or Reuters’ geospatial team.

**3. American Airlines fuel guidance revision.** The first time American updates its full-year fuel cost guidance will tell you what its management believes about the duration of the disruption. If they revise upward and narrow the revenue guidance band, they are not expecting a rapid resolution. The timing of that disclosure (likely at the next earnings call unless there is a pre-announcement) is a market event in its own right.

**4. VLCC rates (TD3C benchmark, MEG to China).**Pre-conflict rates were $206,141 per day. On Monday open, the benchmark hit $423,736, an all-time record and a 94% single-session move, which already settled the Tier 2 question. The rates have since retraced as the Trump escort announcement created uncertainty about physical access. What you want to watch now is not whether rates break $250,000. It is whether they hold above $300,000 on the retracement. A floor above $300,000 means the physical disruption is outrunning the political response. A collapse back below $200,000 means the escort announcement has genuine operational credibility and the tanker trade gives back most of its premium.

**5. a) Iran negotiation posture.** As of March 5, Iran’s security chief Ali Larijani has publicly stated: “We will not negotiate with the United States,” directly contradicting reports that Iran’s new post-Khamenei leadership was seeking talks. Trump told The Atlantic that Iran wanted to negotiate and he had agreed to talk, but Larijani’s public statement has not been walked back. Polymarket’s prediction market, with $12.4 million in trading volume, currently prices a ceasefire by June 30 at approximately 68% and by May 31 at 64%. The divergence between Trump’s stated expectation and Larijani’s public position is the single most important variable for Tier probability weighting right now. A negotiated off-ramp in March would collapse the Tier 2 and 3 probabilities rapidly. The absence of one hardens Tier 2 as the structural base case for the remainder of Q2.

**5. b) China spot crude purchasing.** The 200 million barrels of Iranian crude floating at sea gives China approximately four months of buffer. As long as Chinese refiners are drawing down floating storage rather than buying spot crude, the oil price upside is capped. Watch Kpler’s weekly Chinese import data. The week China starts buying spot crude aggressively (rather than drawing floating inventory) is the week the $85 Brent floor becomes a $90 floor.

## **What Could Make Us Wrong**

Every framework has a kill switch. Here are the three things that collapse this one.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ktZ0!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff7a17c89-9bbb-42e9-bc44-ca97b6eed10c_1403x679.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ktZ0!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff7a17c89-9bbb-42e9-bc44-ca97b6eed10c_1403x679.png)

First, Trump’s escort operations achieve credible sustained passage within 72 hours. Not an announcement, but an actual commercial tanker transiting under escort without incident, followed by a second and a third. If the first convoy goes through clean, war risk premiums compress, VLCC rates retrace below $200,000, and the tanker trade gives back most of its premium in a week.

Second, Iran agrees to negotiate before March 15. Larijani’s public refusal notwithstanding, if a backchannel produces a ceasefire framework, the Tier 2 and Tier 3 probabilities collapse simultaneously. Polymarket has ceasefire by June 30 at approximately 68%. If that timeline compresses to March, every long position in this note reverses.

Third, China draws its 200 million barrels of floating Iranian crude while simultaneously refusing to bid spot crude. This does not collapse the thesis in the way the first two kill switches do. It caps the upside. Oil stays range-bound because Chinese demand never materialises in the spot market, and the tanker rate thesis softens as floating storage vessels discharge and re-enter the active fleet. This is not a Tier 1 resolution; it is a Tier 2 that fails to escalate and slowly bleeds premium. The distinction matters for position sizing: the first two kill switches require immediate exits, the third requires patience and a willingness to let positions decay rather than reverse.

The first two kill switches are sufficient individually to invalidate the Tier 2 base case outright. The third erodes it. All three are currently possible. Position sizing should reflect that.
