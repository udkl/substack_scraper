## **Financial Analysis**

To understand why the market’s reaction to Q3 was boring, you have to appreciate the obsession retail investors and algorithmic traders have with “The Beat.” Wesdome reported an Earnings Per Share (EPS) of CAD 0.58 against a forecast of CAD 0.60.

In the binary world of headline trading, that 3.33% miss is a red flag. It triggers sell programs. It causes the casual observer to think, “Oh, they missed. Next.”

But this is a lazy, surface-level interpretation that misses the big change in the company’s capitalization. If you ignore the operational noise for a second and look strictly at the P&L and Balance Sheet, Wesdome looks less like a struggling miner and more like a printing press.

### **Top-Line Explosion**

Revenue hit **CAD 230 million**, beating forecasts by nearly 9%. That is a 57% jump year-over-year. In mining, where guys are usually happy to flatline production and pray for gold price hikes, this is volume-driven growth amplified by pricing.

This revenue figure isn’t just a function of the gold price being high; it’s a function of Eagle River pushing high-grade ore through the mill at record rates.

The quality of this revenue is high. Sold ounces were 47,400 oz, up 10% year-over-year. This confirms that the revenue beat was driven by actual metal leaving the gate.

Wesdome is converting 65 cents of every dollar of revenue into operating cash flow before tax and depreciation. Eagle River is the only reason the stock hasn’t collapsed under the weight of Kiena’s drama. This mine is a beast, an anomaly in an industry where grades usually degrade over time. Eagle River has been in production since 1995, and yet it is currently delivering some of the best grades in its history.

### **Kiena - The Detractor**

If Eagle River is the golden child, Kiena is the teenager crashing the family car into the garage door. Repeatedly.

* **Production:** 16,169 ounces. Down **25% year-over-year**.
* **Grade:** 10.2 g/t. Down **22% year-over-year**.
* **Throughput:** 50,147 tonnes, down 3%.

#### **The Anatomy of Failure**

The Kiena drop wasn’t a single catastrophic event; it was a cascade of failures, some mechanical, some human.

1. The Hoist Failure:

In July 2025, Kiena suffered a “longer than planned hoist shutdown”.4 In a deep underground mine like Kiena (where the shaft goes down 920 meters 9), the hoist is the umbilical cord. It brings ore up; it takes men and materials down. If the hoist stops, the mine stops. The shutdown resulted in “more than two weeks without mill feed”.11 For a continuous operation, losing 14 days of production is devastating to quarterly numbers. It disrupts the rhythm of the stope cycle, delays backfill, and creates a bottleneck that takes weeks to clear even after the hoist is fixed.

2. The Presqu’île Disaster:

The Presqu’île Zone is a satellite deposit accessible via a ramp from the surface. The strategic plan was for Presqu’île to provide “supplemental feed”—lower grade but easy tonnage to keep the mill full while the high-grade Kiena Deep ore was mined carefully.

It didn’t work. The quarter saw “contractor execution issues and underperformance at Presqu’île”.2 In plain English: the contractors hired to mine this zone didn’t hit their targets. They moved less rock than promised. This forced the mill to run under capacity or wait for feed, destroying efficiency.

3. Inconsistency:

Management’s commentary is littered with euphemisms like “inconsistent execution” and “limited operational flexibility”.4 This is code for “we are mining hand-to-mouth.” When you have limited flexibility, it means you don’t have enough prepared stopes (working faces) ready to go. If one stope encounters bad ground or equipment failure, you have nowhere else to send the scoop trams. You just sit and wait. Kiena is currently “stope-constrained,” leaving it vulnerable to even minor disruptions.

1. **Kiena Deep:** The high-grade core (10.1 g/t reserve grade).
2. **Presqu’île:** The bulk tonnage ramp zone.
3. **136-Level:** A reintroduced horizon to provide flexibility.

## **Management**

You don’t buy mining stocks for the rocks; you buy them for the people who know how to get the rocks out of the ground without going bankrupt. Wesdome has assembled a team that looks less like a standard mid-tier board and more like a turnaround special ops unit. The recent personnel moves are the strongest signal in this entire analysis.

### **Philip Yee - Kirkland Lake DNA**

This is the alpha. **Philip Yee** was appointed CFO effective September 29, 2025. To the uninitiated, a CFO appointment sounds boring. To those who followed the gold sector from 2016 to 2020, this is a siren.

The Resume:

Philip Yee was the Executive Vice President and CFO of Kirkland Lake Gold (KL) from 2016 to 2018. During his tenure (and the subsequent years where his systems were in place), Kirkland Lake Gold was the best performing gold stock on the planet. They took the Fosterville mine in Australia and the Macassa mine in Ontario (high-grade, deep underground assets) and turned them into cash flow monsters through ruthless cost control and operational optimization. Before that, he was CFO at Lake Shore Gold 6, another success story in the Timmins camp.

The Move:

Here is the nuance: Yee was already on the Wesdome Board of Directors. He had a bird’s-eye view of the company. He saw the mess at Kiena, he saw the cash generation at Eagle River, and he decided to step down from the Board governance role into the operational C-suite role.

This is rare. Board members usually enjoy their quarterly meetings and stock options. They don’t jump into the trenches unless:

1. They see a massive financial upside they can unlock personally.
2. The situation is so critical that it requires their specific skillset to fix.

The Implication:

Yee is a “wartime” CFO. He is not here to manage a steady state. He is here to impose “Kirkland Lake discipline” on Kiena. He knows exactly what high-grade underground mining should cost.

He knows how to negotiate with contractors. He knows how to structure the balance sheet for M&A. His presence suggests that Wesdome is preparing for a major transition, either a rigorous turnaround of Kiena to prep for a sale, or an aggressive acquisition strategy using their cash pile.

## **Val-D’Or and Labour War**

You can have the best gold and the best management, but if you can’t get guys to go underground, you are dead. Wesdome is fighting a war in Val-d’Or, Quebec, and they are currently the underdog.

### **Agnico Eagle Major Issue**

The Val-d’Or region is effectively a company town, and that company is **Agnico Eagle**. Agnico owns the Canadian Malartic (Canada’s largest gold mine), Goldex, and LaRonde mines, all within a short drive of Wesdome’s Kiena.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!AO7F!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa3906618-d233-4347-ade6-d8121a338b05_1946x1086.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!AO7F!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa3906618-d233-4347-ade6-d8121a338b05_1946x1086.png)

* **The Problem:** Agnico is the “employer of choice.” They offer stability, massive benefits, and a “family” culture that they actively cultivate. They aim to hire 100% of their workforce locally.
* **The Impact on Wesdome:** This is the root cause of the “contractor execution issues” at Kiena. The tier-1 miners, the best jumbo operators, the best scoop drivers, they all work for Agnico. Wesdome is left fighting for the scraps or relying on third-party contractors.
* **The Mercenary Risk:** Contractors are mercenaries. In a tight labor market, the best contractor crews get sent to the biggest, most consistent clients (Agnico). The “B-teams” get sent to the smaller, more sporadic clients (Wesdome). This explains why Presqu’île underperformed. The contractors simply didn’t deliver the tons.

### **The Demographic Cliff**

The broader context is even uglier for small producers.

* **Disinterest:** **66% of youth** aged 15 to 30 have absolutely no interest in working in the mining industry.
* **Shortage:** The industry needs to recruit **191,000 workers** in the next decade just to replace retirees and meet baseline demand. In an expansionary scenario, that number hits 256,000.
* **Wage Inflation:** This scarcity drives up costs. Production costs per tonne at Kiena jumped to **$506** in Q3 2025, up significantly from $426 the previous year. That isn’t just general inflation; that is “wage inflation”, paying more to get less efficient labor.

Until Wesdome can stabilize its own workforce and reduce reliance on contractors, Kiena’s costs will remain volatile. They are trying to compete with a whale in a swimming pool that is rapidly draining of water.

Wesdome operates in a vacuum of its own operational challenges, but the external environment provides a massive tailwind that might just save them from themselves.

## **History Lesson - Kiena’s History**

To understand why the market is so skeptical of Kiena, you have to look at its history. This mine has broken hearts before.

* **1981:** First ore mined.
* **1984-2003:** Passed between Falconbridge, Campbell Red Lake, and McWatters Mines. It was a “trading card” asset, bought, sold, and eventually closed when prices got low.
* **2003:** Wesdome buys the Kiena complex.
* **2013:** Operations suspended. The mine was put on care and maintenance because it wasn’t profitable.
* **2016:** The Discovery. The **Kiena Deep A Zone** was found. This changed everything. It wasn’t just old remnants; it was a new, high-grade core.
* **2021:** Pre-Feasibility Study (PFS) for the restart.
* **2024:** First ore from Kiena Deep.

The Trauma:

Investors remember the 2013 shutdown. They remember the false starts. The “hoist failure” in 2025 triggers PTSD. It reinforces the narrative that Kiena is a “tired” asset with old bones (infrastructure from 1981) that will always struggle to deliver consistent ounces. Management’s job is to prove that the 2016 Deep Zone discovery is a “new mine” hiding inside an old shell, not just a scavenging operation.
