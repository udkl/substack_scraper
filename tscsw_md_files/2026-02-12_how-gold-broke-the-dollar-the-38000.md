## **Part 1: How Gold Broke the GDP Model**

Let me go back six months to set the stage, because the Q1 2025 GDP fiasco explains why October’s gold export spike matters so much.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FUqW!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F88dd387a-4997-473a-ab4d-326125bc56e7_1405x528.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FUqW!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F88dd387a-4997-473a-ab4d-326125bc56e7_1405x528.png)

The BEA’s advance estimate for Q1 2025 GDP came in at negative 0.3%. The headline number was bad enough, but the backstory was worse. In late February, when January trade data dropped, gold imports single-handedly swung the Atlanta Fed’s GDPNow model from +2.3% to -1.5% overnight. A 3.3 percentage point distortion from one commodity. Not oil, not semiconductors, not agricultural products. Gold. A metal that sits in vaults and does nothing.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!hLOz!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa1e03d0e-1450-4dbe-be56-5a23da60c7d8_1405x782.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!hLOz!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa1e03d0e-1450-4dbe-be56-5a23da60c7d8_1405x782.png)

The dislocation was so severe that by March 6, the Atlanta Fed was forced to create an entirely separate “gold-adjusted” GDPNow model, which ran roughly 2 percentage points higher than the standard version. I’ve been following macro data for years and I have never seen a statistical agency essentially admit that one line item broke their model. The Atlanta Fed literally had to kill its old model and build a new one. That’s how disruptive the gold flows were.

Total imports (including substantial tariff front-running) subtracted 5.0 percentage points from actual GDP that quarter. But the gold component was the analytically interesting part. America was hoovering up physical gold at a pace that distorted national accounts. The conventional explanation was that traders were moving gold to COMEX vaults to settle futures contracts at elevated premiums. Fine. That’s probably true.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Yf8G!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F15c1f05d-deb7-4781-8a67-357ddbf41d38_1405x787.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Yf8G!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F15c1f05d-deb7-4781-8a67-357ddbf41d38_1405x787.png)

But then, by autumn, the flow reversed. Gold started moving out. And where it went tells you something the arbitrage story doesn’t.

The October BEA trade data confirmed exports rose $7.1 billion month-over-month, with gold dominating the increase. November exports then fell $11.1 billion, and while the gold-specific commodity breakdown is less granular, the Swiss customs data fills in the picture. The gold was being refined in Switzerland and shipped to China, India, Turkey, and the UAE. It wasn’t returning to the London vaults it came from. It was being absorbed permanently into Eastern central bank and private reserves.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!N0I4!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd8017630-bd9b-4f3c-83e8-44219d9ec74a_1405x662.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!N0I4!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd8017630-bd9b-4f3c-83e8-44219d9ec74a_1405x662.png)

For context: the last time gold dominated American exports was during the California Gold Rush of 1848-1857, when California alone produced roughly 76 tonnes annually, about 2% of GDP. In the modern era, post-Bretton Woods, post-Nixon shock, post-financialization, there is simply no precedent for this.

That absence of precedent should make you uncomfortable. It makes me uncomfortable, and I’ve been bullish on gold for a while.

## **Part 2: The Policy Flip Nobody Is Talking About**

For decades, the prevailing wisdom among gold analysts (and conspiracy theorists, if we’re being honest) was that the US government actively suppressed the gold price. The logic was straightforward: a rising gold price is a falling confidence vote in the dollar. Since the entire post-1971 monetary architecture depends on global confidence in dollar-denominated assets, particularly Treasuries, the US had every incentive to keep gold boring, irrelevant, a relic.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!_LgA!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb462be73-67f2-4ed6-a398-16353acc3209_1405x591.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!_LgA!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb462be73-67f2-4ed6-a398-16353acc3209_1405x591.png)

I used to find this argument partially compelling. I don’t anymore. Not because the historical suppression thesis was necessarily wrong, but because the incentive structure has inverted completely.

Think about what both the Biden and Trump administrations have explicitly stated they want. They want a weaker dollar, to make American exports competitive and reduce the trade deficit. They want reshoring of manufacturing, which requires making foreign production relatively more expensive. They want reduced foreign ownership of US assets, because dependence on foreign capital creates leverage for adversaries. And they want a rebuilt defense industrial base.

On that last point, let me share a data point that I think is underappreciated. CNN reported in July 2025 that the US used approximately 25% of its entire THAAD missile interceptor stockpile during the Israel-Iran conflict, burning through more than 100 interceptors at $12.7 million each over just 12 days. At current production rates of 11-12 new interceptors per year, replenishing that inventory could take 3 to 8 years. A quarter of your missile defense capacity, consumed in less than two weeks, with replacement measured in years.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!lq2w!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F163eef4b-02dc-4842-8beb-dd11ae98d50e_1405x720.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!lq2w!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F163eef4b-02dc-4842-8beb-dd11ae98d50e_1405x720.png)

You cannot run a defense industrial base when your currency is so strong that domestic manufacturing is uncompetitive. You cannot reshore production when every factory in America costs 5x what the same factory costs in China. You cannot rebuild military stockpiles when your industrial base has been hollowed out by four decades of financialization. Every single one of these problems is ameliorated by a weaker dollar. And a weaker dollar, by definition, means a higher gold price.

I want to be precise here. I’m not claiming there’s a smoke-filled room where Treasury officials are coordinating gold policy. I don’t need conspiracy. I need aligned incentives. And the incentives are screaming.

On January 20, 2026, US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer delivered a keynote at Davos titled “From Hamilton to Today: Trade and U.S. Economic Strategy.” The full text is on USTR.gov, and I’d encourage you to read it. He invoked Alexander Hamilton’s Report on Manufactures, argued for an “American System” of tariffs, and called for a “new global economic order founded on fairness, balance, and national interests.”

I’ll be transparent about something here: Greer did not mention gold. He did not use the phrase “neutral reserve asset.” And I don’t want to be the kind of analyst who reads his own thesis into someone else’s speech and calls it evidence. That would be intellectually dishonest.

What I can say is that the Hamiltonian framework Greer described, a system where industrial policy and strategic trade take priority over free capital flows, is fundamentally incompatible with the current dollar reserve architecture as it exists today. Hamilton built a system where the US exported real goods and maintained a sound currency backed by specie. The modern version of that vision, one that analysts like Luke Gromen have been mapping for years, would logically require some neutral settlement layer between great powers who no longer trust each other’s sovereign paper.

Gold is the most obvious candidate for that role. It may not be the one policymakers choose. They may attempt digital alternatives, bilateral currency arrangements, SDR baskets, or some hybrid system nobody has proposed yet. But the direction of Greer’s stated policy, away from financialisation and toward industrial production, is at minimum consistent with an environment where gold’s monetary role increases. I’ll let readers judge for themselves whether “consistent with” is strong enough to act on. For what it’s worth, I think it is, but I want the reasoning visible rather than buried in assertion.

And the institutional machinery is already being prepared.

In August 2025, the Federal Reserve published a FEDS Note titled “Official Reserve Revaluations: The International Experience.” Let that title sink in. The Fed’s own research staff analyzed five countries that have revalued their official gold reserves and explicitly modeled how the United States could revalue from the statutory price of $42.22 per ounce to the market price. You don’t write that paper unless someone important is asking the question. You don’t publish it on the Fed’s website unless someone wants the idea in circulation.

The mechanics are well understood. The US holds 8,133.46 tonnes of gold, or 261.5 million fine troy ounces, currently carried on Treasury’s books at the statutory price of $42.22 per ounce for a laughable book value of $11 billion. At today’s market price of approximately $5,050 per ounce, that same gold is worth $1.32 trillion. Fortune reported the reserves first crossed $1 trillion in market value around September 2025, when gold was only at $3,825. It’s since gained another 32%.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Upmk!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9e360d2f-fff8-44af-a117-982c287687f0_1405x786.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Upmk!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9e360d2f-fff8-44af-a117-982c287687f0_1405x786.png)

If Congress were to raise the statutory price (which requires legislation, not executive action, though S. 954 was introduced in March 2025 to do effectively this for a Bitcoin reserve, so the legislative template exists), Treasury could issue new gold certificates to the Fed at the higher valuation. The Fed would credit Treasury’s account. Result: $1.3 trillion in spending capacity without issuing a single new Treasury bond. No tax increase, no borrowing, no inflation in the conventional sense. Just the recognition of value that already exists.

Now compare that $1.32 trillion to the $9.36 trillion in foreign-held Treasury debt as of November 2025. The ratio, US gold market value as a percentage of foreign-held Treasuries, currently sits at approximately 14-15%.

This ratio has a history that is enormously instructive.

In 1989, when the Berlin Wall fell: approximately 25%. Through the 1950s and 1960s, when the Bretton Woods system still held: 40% to well over 100%. At the 1980 peak, when gold hit $850 and foreign Treasury holdings were modest: roughly 125-175%. US gold was worth more than all foreign-held Treasuries combined.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!w5XE!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb53e4c53-3bec-46fb-bbde-32a2004ea96b_1405x782.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!w5XE!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb53e4c53-3bec-46fb-bbde-32a2004ea96b_1405x782.png)

The current 14-15% represents a significant recovery from the late-1990s trough of roughly 8-10%, but it’s still historically depressed by any reasonable standard. Every major monetary reset in the past century has been accompanied by a dramatic repricing of gold relative to sovereign obligations. We appear to be in the early innings of the next one.

And as if to underline the point with a sledgehammer: on February 9, 2026, two days ago as I write this, Bloomberg reported that Chinese regulators verbally directed banks to limit new purchases and reduce existing positions of US Treasuries. China’s official holdings of $682.6 billion are already at their lowest level since 2008, down from a peak of $1.32 trillion in 2013. In the same month, the PBOC extended its gold purchases for a 15th consecutive month.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!a4eF!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F10350a62-fc91-434c-a2f6-9f258bb8d16c_1405x789.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!a4eF!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F10350a62-fc91-434c-a2f6-9f258bb8d16c_1405x789.png)

Selling Treasuries. Buying gold. For 15 months straight. And they just told their banks to accelerate.

The direction of travel could not be clearer.

# Part 3: The Gromen Equation, and Where It Breaks

If you follow macro Twitter at all, you’ve encountered Luke Gromen. Founder of Forest for the Trees, one of the most widely followed macro strategists in the gold and commodity space, and the person who has done more than perhaps anyone else to map the structural case for gold as a neutral reserve asset in a deglobalising world. I’ve drawn on his work throughout this piece, and readers of TSCS will know we published a detailed synopsis and critique of his “Mother of All Crises” framework last year.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!xsb7!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5d6a8fd0-87ef-4c97-9a58-f053cb4e768e_1405x609.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!xsb7!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5d6a8fd0-87ef-4c97-9a58-f053cb4e768e_1405x609.png)

Gromen has a number that makes people’s eyes roll. I want to walk through it carefully, because what it captures is important even where the specific math oversimplifies.

## **The Framework**

China ran a trade surplus of $992 billion in 2024 and approximately $1.189 trillion in 2025, a roughly 20% year-over-year increase. That’s the largest trade surplus any country has ever run in human history, including in real terms. Chinese gross gold imports hit 1,447 tonnes in 2023, shattering the previous record of 1,427 tonnes set in 2018, with 2024 imports estimated at approximately 1,225 tonnes. Net imports for 2024 (adjusting for re-exports through Hong Kong) are estimated at approximately 975 tonnes, though Chinese customs data makes precise verification difficult by design.

Gromen's provocation is simple. Divide the 2025 surplus by estimated 2024 net gold imports: $1.189 trillion divided by 975 tonnes, converted to troy ounces (31,339,335 oz), gives you approximately $37,900 per ounce. Call it $38,000.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!iNMi!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F06f983e1-5641-4ea1-81a6-8469c38b4ea3_1405x662.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!iNMi!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F06f983e1-5641-4ea1-81a6-8469c38b4ea3_1405x662.png)

That is the implied price at which China’s trade surplus with the world would be fully settled in gold terms. In his words: “The dollar is so overvalued against gold relative to what we make useful in the real world. And that’s the issue.”

His broader argument is that this number captures the magnitude of the dollar’s overvaluation relative to what America actually produces versus what China actually produces, denominated in the one monetary asset that both sides can agree on as neutral. When he says “all roads lead to gold,” he means it literally: whether you get inflation (currency debasement) or deflation (sovereign credit risk forcing eventual monetisation), gold is the only major monetary asset with no counterparty risk. The endpoint is the same. Only the path differs.

And there is real data backing the directional claim. The sensitivity table is, I think, more useful than the single headline number:

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!fSm8!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fcc70b5c5-8d29-4872-8f98-dfcafed04269_1405x761.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!fSm8!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fcc70b5c5-8d29-4872-8f98-dfcafed04269_1405x761.png)

Even in the most favorable scenario for the status quo, where China’s surplus halves and gold imports stay constant, the implied equilibrium price is nearly 4x today’s level. And the surplus isn’t halving. It grew 20% last year. Chinese firms pivoted away from the US (exports down 20%) toward Africa (+26%), ASEAN (+13%), and the EU (+8%). The surplus is becoming more diversified, not smaller.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!tWeX!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F31edb195-bf24-4354-b431-06ff2a2298ed_1405x720.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!tWeX!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F31edb195-bf24-4354-b431-06ff2a2298ed_1405x720.png)

The physical evidence supports the direction of travel. The dollar fell 10.7% in the first half of 2025, the worst first-half decline in over 50 years, with Bloomberg noting it was the most significant since 1973. The DXY currently sits around 97, down roughly 10% over 12 months. Non-monetary gold was America’s single largest export for the first time in modern history in October and November 2025. The gold is moving West to East through Swiss refineries, being recast into kilobars, and it isn’t coming back. China’s cost advantage in physical production (83% cheaper nuclear power, per Johns Hopkins/Harvard data published in Nature’s research family in 2025) is real and structural, not merely a labor arbitrage.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!z7I-!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F059ca4d7-e864-4c76-b661-3cab16c56244_1405x787.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!z7I-!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F059ca4d7-e864-4c76-b661-3cab16c56244_1405x787.png)

Gromen’s conclusion: “I think gold is probably going 15 to 25,000 over the next 10 years.” More importantly, he argues the US government’s incentive structure has flipped entirely. It is now, in his framing, “a matter of national security for the United States for the price of gold to go up, not down.”

# Where the Framework Breaks

I find Gromen’s directional thesis compelling. I think the structural case for gold is the strongest it has been in decades, for many of the reasons he articulates. But intellectual honesty requires identifying where his framework overreaches, and there are several places where it does.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!uPiK!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F16d6d91c-7957-4ddb-ab9f-7d4a5c0f2736_1405x765.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!uPiK!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F16d6d91c-7957-4ddb-ab9f-7d4a5c0f2736_1405x765.png)

## 1. The $38,000 is a ratio, not an equilibrium.

Dividing China’s trade surplus by its gold imports produces a number, but there is no economic mechanism that requires or even suggests trade surpluses should be fully settled in gold. China’s surplus is also being recycled into copper stockpiles, strategic petroleum reserves, euro and yen-denominated assets, Belt and Road infrastructure, and domestic investment. Gold is one destination for surplus capital, not the only one. The $38,000 figure implicitly assumes 100% gold settlement of the entire surplus, which no one, including Gromen, actually expects to happen. It functions as a rhetorical anchor, not an analytical price target, and readers should treat it accordingly.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5Yp8!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4e23bea0-671f-4f05-ad93-b6da0646b76a_1405x788.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5Yp8!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4e23bea0-671f-4f05-ad93-b6da0646b76a_1405x788.png)

## 2. Correlation is doing a lot of heavy lifting.

This is the most important methodological critique, and it applies not just to Gromen but to the entire gold macro narrative, including my own argument in this piece.

The core claim is: China is selling Treasuries AND buying gold, therefore China is replacing Treasuries with gold as its reserve asset. The “therefore” is doing enormous work. China is indeed reducing Treasury holdings ($682.6 billion, the lowest since 2008, down from $1.32 trillion in 2013). China is indeed buying gold (15 consecutive months of PBOC purchases). But these two facts don’t necessarily share a causal link.

China’s Treasury reduction is heavily driven by currency intervention mechanics. When the PBOC sells Treasuries, it’s often to defend the yuan in FX markets, not to fund gold purchases. The gold buying is a separate strategic decision driven by sanctions risk (after the West froze $300 billion of Russian reserves in 2022), reserve diversification, and domestic financial stability objectives (Chinese banks need better assets on their balance sheets given the real estate crisis). Both things are happening simultaneously, but “simultaneously” is not “because of.”

Gromen himself, to his credit, acknowledges this indirectly when he says “forget what I think, de facto the United States settled part of its trade deficit in October and November in gold.” The word “de facto” is doing the work of an entire causal argument. The US exported gold and ran a trade deficit. Those are two facts. Whether one settles the other, or whether they’re coincident flows driven by different mechanisms (COMEX arbitrage unwinds, Swiss refinery demand, Eastern central bank accumulation), is genuinely uncertain.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!qppX!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8ff68fea-4552-4d70-b17e-427c152bfca9_1405x741.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!qppX!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8ff68fea-4552-4d70-b17e-427c152bfca9_1405x741.png)

I lean toward Gromen’s interpretation. The direction, volume, and persistence of the flows are hard to explain purely through arbitrage mechanics. Gold that gets refined into kilobars in Switzerland and shipped to Shanghai doesn’t look like a basis trade unwind. It looks like settlement. But I want readers to understand that this is an interpretation of correlated data, not a proven causal chain. The distinction matters for sizing your conviction and your position.

## 3. The “national security requires higher gold” argument proves too much.

Gromen argues that the US government now wants a higher gold price because it needs a weaker dollar for reshoring, defense independence, and debt management. He’s almost certainly right about the policy direction. But the argument, taken at face value, implies the US government is effectively coordinating the destruction of its own reserve currency status. Governments rarely do this voluntarily. They do it by accident, or they do it when forced by crisis, or they do it incrementally while loudly denying it’s happening. The idea that both the Biden and Trump administrations arrived at a conscious “let gold rip” strategy gives policymakers more credit for coherent long-term planning than any government in history has demonstrated.

More likely, what we’re observing is aligned incentives producing a gold-favorable environment without anyone explicitly choosing it. That’s a weaker claim than Gromen makes, but it’s a more honest one, and importantly, it leads to the same investment conclusion. You don’t need a conspiracy or even a coordinated strategy. You just need a world where every major policy objective (weaker dollar, reshoring, reduced foreign ownership of US assets, rebuilt defense base) happens to point in the same direction as a rising gold price.

## 4. The framework has no falsification condition.

This is what separates a thesis from a religion. Gromen’s “all roads lead to gold” argument is compelling precisely because it’s comprehensive: inflation favors gold, deflation triggers sovereign credit risk which forces monetisation which favors gold, dollar strength is unsustainable so it favors gold eventually, dollar weakness obviously favors gold. Every scenario resolves to the same conclusion.

But an argument where every possible outcome confirms your thesis is an argument that can never be proven wrong. And an argument that can never be proven wrong should be held with appropriate epistemic humility, not because it’s necessarily incorrect, but because you’ve constructed it in a way that prevents you from ever discovering if it is.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!lIyX!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F05ab744f-472e-425b-b50d-f5254c60f6a2_1405x784.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!lIyX!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F05ab744f-472e-425b-b50d-f5254c60f6a2_1405x784.png)

For what it’s worth, here is my falsification condition: if the US manages a genuine fiscal consolidation (deficit below 3% of GDP sustained for three or more years) while maintaining positive real GDP growth above 2.5%, the structural gold thesis weakens considerably. I don’t think this is likely. But I want to name the condition under which I’d reassess, because any honest analyst should.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!AA5k!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0a1f9e6b-ad48-4f83-8962-df05e661c41d_1405x782.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!AA5k!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0a1f9e6b-ad48-4f83-8962-df05e661c41d_1405x782.png)

## What I Actually Think

Gromen’s directional call is right. The structural case for gold is overwhelming. The trade flows, the central bank buying, the FEDS Notes on revaluation, the Hamiltonian policy rhetoric, the deterioration of Western sovereign balance sheets, the Cayman Islands basis trade fragility, all of it points the same way.

Where I part company is on magnitude and certainty. I think gold is going meaningfully higher from here, probably $10,000-15,000 over the next decade in my expected value calculation. But I hold that view knowing that the analytical framework supporting it has real gaps, that correlation is not causation, that the $38,000 number is a provocation rather than a target, and that the “all roads lead to gold” construction is unfalsifiable by design.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-TOa!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2b0adf0a-9bb7-451e-8efb-fdbc00cd1557_1405x486.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-TOa!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2b0adf0a-9bb7-451e-8efb-fdbc00cd1557_1405x486.png)

The irony is almost too perfect. The country that abandoned gold in 1971 is now exporting it as its most important trade good. The dollar’s confession, written in the language of trade flows, is that it already knows what comes next. But confessions can be misread, and the prudent investor will size their position for the probability-weighted outcome, not for the most dramatic one.

## **Part 4: The Japan Accelerant**

If the China-gold-trade story is the slow-burning structural thesis, Japan is the potential accelerant that could compress a decade of adjustment into a few violent quarters.

I need to walk through some bond market mechanics here because the signal flashing from Japanese government bonds is one I’ve only previously seen in emerging market crises. Most Western investors are completely unprepared for what it implies, partly because the idea that Japan, the world’s third-largest economy and its largest net creditor, could trigger a global financial dislocation strikes people as absurd.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Y3Rb!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F783cd660-022b-4b43-9231-f6e2ab31628e_1402x728.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Y3Rb!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F783cd660-022b-4b43-9231-f6e2ab31628e_1402x728.png)

## The Chart That Should Keep You Up at Night

The yield spread between 10-year US Treasuries and 10-year Japanese Government Bonds has narrowed dramatically, from roughly 350 basis points in mid-2024 to approximately 190-200 basis points today. The 10-year UST yields around 4.15%, the 10-year JGB around 2.24%.

Under normal circumstances, in any economics textbook covering developed markets, a narrowing UST-JGB spread is bullish for the yen. The logic is straightforward: it reduces the carry trade incentive to borrow in yen and invest in dollars, which should reduce selling pressure on the yen and strengthen it.

But the yen isn’t strengthening. It’s weakening. USD/JPY sat near 153 in October 2025 despite the spread breaking below levels that historically corresponded to a much stronger yen. This divergence between the bond spread and the currency, which had held reliably for at least five to six years prior, only started breaking down in the third quarter of 2025.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!kVRT!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F103b9ce7-4417-4935-b277-209c38d2124c_1405x789.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!kVRT!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F103b9ce7-4417-4935-b277-209c38d2124c_1405x789.png)

Now, I want to be careful here because there are less alarming interpretations of this divergence. It’s possible that Japanese investors are hedging their currency exposure differently than they used to, changing the transmission mechanism between rate differentials and FX. It’s possible that the BOJ’s forward guidance is being priced asymmetrically across bond and currency markets. It’s possible that structural shifts in Japan’s current account (rising energy import costs, declining goods surplus) are weakening the yen independently of rate differentials.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!y4kO!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F96a88674-5124-4d30-9813-276701a726fe_1403x764.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!y4kO!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F96a88674-5124-4d30-9813-276701a726fe_1403x764.png)

But there’s a more troubling interpretation, and it’s the one that rhymes with historical precedent from every emerging market crisis I’ve studied. Higher government bond yields driving a weaker currency is completely contradictory to what developed market textbooks predict. But go to Brazil, Argentina, Turkey, or any country that has experienced a sovereign debt crisis, and they’ll recognise the pattern immediately. Higher yields reflect deteriorating fiscal credibility, not tighter monetary policy. The market demands more compensation not because of inflation expectations alone, but because it’s starting to price in the possibility that the sovereign cannot sustainably service its debt.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!JKgC!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fbc51c5aa-fdc5-461a-b983-c69f0a05d7a4_1405x789.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!JKgC!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fbc51c5aa-fdc5-461a-b983-c69f0a05d7a4_1405x789.png)

The uncomfortable question is whether this is now happening in Japan.

## The Doom Loop Mechanics

Japan’s general government gross debt stands at approximately 230-237% of GDP, the highest among developed nations by a staggering margin. The Bank of Japan raised rates to 0.75% in December 2025, and new PM Sanae Takaichi, who won a landslide lower house election in February 2026, has signalled willingness to accept higher rates as part of her fiscal agenda.

The mechanical problem is straightforward and self-reinforcing. Every basis point increase in JGB yields makes the debt service burden heavier. The heavier debt service worsens the fiscal outlook. The worsening fiscal outlook pressures the yen. A weaker yen drives imported inflation (Japan imports the vast majority of its energy and a significant share of its food). Imported inflation forces the BOJ to consider more hikes to maintain credibility. More hikes raise debt service costs further.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!bLVQ!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F89131ad4-46e8-4d98-88bc-2b48ea33e46a_1402x785.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!bLVQ!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F89131ad4-46e8-4d98-88bc-2b48ea33e46a_1402x785.png)

This is the classic emerging market doom loop. The reason it hasn’t applied to Japan historically is twofold: first, Japan ran persistent current account surpluses, generating natural demand for yen; second, the vast majority of JGBs were held domestically, meaning the government was essentially borrowing from its own citizens’ savings. Both of these buffers are eroding. Japan’s goods trade balance has deteriorated substantially since 2022 as energy costs rose. And the domestic savings pool is shrinking as the population ages and retirees draw down assets.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-wQL!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0ea354ee-912f-4b85-bdef-aecc150f3c2d_1402x785.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-wQL!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0ea354ee-912f-4b85-bdef-aecc150f3c2d_1402x785.png)

If you overlay the two-year JGB yield over the last five years against gold, as Gromen has highlighted to clients, they track almost identically. Japanese savers are watching their government bond yields rise for the wrong reasons (fiscal stress rather than economic strength) and responding rationally: buy gold.

## The $3.66 Trillion Piggy Bank

Now here’s the part that connects directly to American markets and the gold thesis.

Japan’s Net International Investment Position, the difference between what Japanese entities own abroad and what foreigners own in Japan, reached 533 trillion yen at the end of 2024. Against nominal GDP of approximately 608 trillion yen, that’s roughly 88% of GDP. Japan’s NIIP hit an all-time high of $3.66 trillion in Q3 2025, making it the world’s largest net creditor by a massive, historically unprecedented margin.

This is simultaneously Japan’s greatest strength and the rest of the world’s greatest vulnerability.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!17yS!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F203732b8-226c-4ab4-a01a-f436d5e93e55_1405x789.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!17yS!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F203732b8-226c-4ab4-a01a-f436d5e93e55_1405x789.png)

Where is that money invested? Overwhelmingly in US Treasuries and US equities. Japan holds $1.2 trillion in Treasuries alone, making it the single largest foreign holder. Japanese life insurers, pension funds, and the Government Pension Investment Fund (the world’s largest pension fund) hold hundreds of billions more in US corporate bonds and equities.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Gn-r!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F24c60bd1-a6b8-4aa5-8451-d228332c06a8_1405x315.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Gn-r!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F24c60bd1-a6b8-4aa5-8451-d228332c06a8_1405x315.png)

Unlike Argentina or Turkey or Brazil, countries that entered their sovereign crises with negative NIIPs and no foreign assets to fall back on, Japan has an enormous piggy bank it can break in an emergency. And breaking it looks like: sell Treasuries, sell US stocks, repatriate the proceeds to meet domestic obligations. Pension payments as the population ages. Insurance claims. Government bond purchases to prevent a JGB market dislocation. Bank recapitalisations if yen weakness triggers corporate distress.

If Japan is forced to sell even a fraction of its $3.66 trillion in net foreign assets, the transmission to US markets is direct and violent. Treasury supply increases (as Japanese institutions sell). Treasury demand simultaneously decreases (because the marginal buyer is stepping away). Equity prices face selling pressure. And the dollar weakens against the yen, which is precisely the opposite of what current positioning expects.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!gKne!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe9844973-5389-4822-a40c-eaea08982d55_1403x788.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!gKne!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe9844973-5389-4822-a40c-eaea08982d55_1403x788.png)

The policy choice Japan faces, ultimately, is whether to save the bond market or save the currency. They cannot do both. And every precedent in monetary history suggests they will choose the bond market: cap yields with printed money, accept the currency depreciation, and break the piggy bank of foreign assets to manage the transition. That means selling Treasuries. That means handing the baton of the crisis to the Americans.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!bWNV!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa61eeb13-88e1-41d9-89e6-2c27299e8459_1405x789.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!bWNV!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa61eeb13-88e1-41d9-89e6-2c27299e8459_1405x789.png)

## The April 2025 Preview

We got a preview of this transmission mechanism in April 2025, though the trigger was different. On April 2, Trump announced sweeping tariffs. Bonds initially rallied, with the 10-year falling to 3.86% by April 4. Then everything reversed. The 10-year spiked 60 basis points to roughly 4.5%. The 30-year surged past 5%, marking the biggest three-day increase in decades. Stocks, bonds, and the dollar all fell simultaneously. That’s not a developed market pattern. That’s what happens in Turkey, in Argentina, in countries where sovereign credit is the risk.

The tariff pause was announced on April 9, just seven days after Liberation Day, and was widely interpreted as having been forced by the bond market stress. Seven days. That’s how quickly things can unravel when confidence cracks.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!palf!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4a988513-7ab3-47fd-be65-23d88f7bc667_1403x790.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!palf!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4a988513-7ab3-47fd-be65-23d88f7bc667_1403x790.png)

That April episode was primarily a liquidity scare driven by positioning, particularly around the roughly $800 billion basis trade. The NY Fed’s post-crisis analysis found that actual basis trade positions remained “notably stable” (unlike March 2020), with stress concentrated in swap spreads and market-maker liquidity. A genuine Japanese repatriation event would be orders of magnitude more significant, because it would involve selling of underlying assets, not derivative unwinds.

I want to flag the honest uncertainty here: this is a scenario, not a prediction. Japan’s fiscal deterioration could continue for years at a slow burn without triggering acute liquidation. The BOJ has enormous experience in managing its bond market. Japanese institutional investors are conservative by nature and unlikely to dump foreign holdings precipitously without extreme domestic stress. The probability I assign to this accelerated scenario (25-35%, as outlined in Part 5) reflects genuine uncertainty about timing and severity.

But the directional risk is clear. And here’s the detail that ties Japan back to China and thus back to gold: China supplies approximately 23% of Japan’s total imports. Among product categories where Japan has high import dependence, over two-thirds are sourced from China. In a world where Beijing is already imposing dual-use export controls on Japan covering rare earths, gallium, and germanium, Japan’s economic vulnerability to Chinese leverage is acute and growing. Any escalation of Sino-Japanese tensions would compound the fiscal pressure, accelerate the repatriation imperative, and drive both Japanese and global capital toward the only monetary asset with zero counterparty risk.

That asset is gold. It has been gold for 5,000 years. It will be gold for the next 5,000.

## **Part 5: The Regime Map**

Let me be clear about what I’m arguing and what I’m not.

I’m not telling you to sell everything and buy gold. I’m not predicting the collapse of the dollar. I’m not saying the S&P 500 will crash in nominal terms. What I am arguing is that we are living through a regime change in the global monetary system, and that most investors, particularly the 60/40 portfolio crowd, are positioned for a regime that no longer exists.

Here’s the evidence, stated as plainly as I can.

Gold has outperformed the S&P 500 total return on most timeframes that matter. The approximate numbers as of February 2026:

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!tc4X!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fad238279-b469-4fe0-93b2-3e58dd08a33e_1405x789.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!tc4X!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fad238279-b469-4fe0-93b2-3e58dd08a33e_1405x789.png)

A few honest caveats before anyone accuses me of cherry-picking (because without them, I would be). The 25-year figure benefits enormously from starting near gold’s multi-decade trough around $260 in 2001 and the S&P’s dot-com peak. If you start the clock at gold’s 2011 peak of roughly $1,900, the S&P 500 total return crushed gold for the next decade, tripling while gold went essentially sideways through 2019. Gold spent nine years doing nothing while equities compounded relentlessly.

That matters, because it tells you something about regime dependence. Gold doesn’t outperform in all environments. It outperforms when the monetary regime is shifting, when sovereign credit is deteriorating, when the rules of the game are being rewritten. The question isn’t whether gold always beats stocks. It doesn’t. The question is whether we’re in one of those regime-shift periods right now. I believe we are, and the data I’ve laid out in the preceding sections is my case for why. But I want readers to understand that this outperformance is conditional, not guaranteed, and that a stabilisation of the current system (my Scenario 5) could easily produce another decade where equities dominate.

The bull case isn’t “gold always wins.” It’s “gold wins in environments like this one, and environments like this one are rare.”

And yet the average institutional gold allocation is approximately 1-2%. Academic research using 13-F filings found an average exposure of 1.7% among institutions that hold gold at all, and only about 30% of institutions hold any. Across the full investor universe, the effective allocation is probably under 1%.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!SxHU!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F99c36315-f683-4fe1-83b2-063f6444432f_1402x789.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!SxHU!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F99c36315-f683-4fe1-83b2-063f6444432f_1402x789.png)

This is a positioning mismatch so extreme it almost defies explanation. An asset class that has outperformed the benchmark on literally every meaningful timeframe gets a rounding error allocation. Why? Because the entire financial advisory industry was built during a 40-year bond bull market (1982-2020) that taught everyone the same Pavlovian lesson: bonds hedge equity risk. Gold is for cranks.

That lesson is now wrong. And the April 2025 episode proved it. When stocks, bonds, and the dollar all fell simultaneously, every 60/40 portfolio in the world was a correlated bet on the same outcome: stable sovereign credit. There was no hedge. The only asset that performed was gold.

The deeper structural problem is even more troubling than a single crisis episode. The Federal Reserve’s own October 2025 FEDS Note, “The Cross-Border Trail of the Treasury Basis Trade,” revealed that TIC data undercounts Cayman Islands-domiciled hedge fund Treasury holdings by approximately $1.4 trillion. After adjustment, these hedge funds held roughly $1.85 trillion in Treasuries (versus $423 billion reported), making them the single largest foreign holder, above Japan, above China, above every sovereign government. Between January 2022 and December 2024, these funds absorbed 37% of total net issuance of notes and bonds.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!CahK!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9337adab-ea0c-40cc-8afe-ae650a651046_1402x789.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!CahK!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9337adab-ea0c-40cc-8afe-ae650a651046_1402x789.png)

Let me say that more bluntly: over a third of all new Treasury supply in the past three years was absorbed by leveraged hedge funds domiciled in a Caribbean tax haven. These are positions that can be unwound in days if spreads move against them. This is the “stable foreign demand” that anchors the entire Treasury market?

Gold has no counterparty. Gold requires no rollover. Gold cannot be frozen by sanctions (ask the Russian central bank about their $300 billion in frozen Western reserves). Gold cannot be unwound by a risk manager in the Caymans. The BIS estimates roughly $86-89 trillion in dollar-denominated non-financial debt globally, and every dollar of that debt depends on the continued willingness of creditors to roll it over. Gold is the only monetary asset in the world that is simultaneously no one’s liability and accepted as settlement by everyone.

## **The Probability Table**

I’ll offer a probability-weighted framework rather than a price target, because intellectual honesty requires acknowledging uncertainty even when you have strong conviction.

**Scenario 1: Orderly rebalancing (30-40% probability). Gold: $7,000-12,000.** Gradual trade settlement shift. Managed dollar decline. Central banks continue accumulating at current pace. No acute crisis. Gold doubles from here over 3-5 years. This is the “boring bull market” scenario, and honestly, it’s the one I hope for.

**Scenario 2: Accelerated crisis, Japan triggers (25-35% probability). Gold: $12,000-25,000.** Japan’s fiscal situation forces NIIP liquidation. Treasury market dysfunction returns, worse than April. The Fed intervenes with yield curve control or outright monetization. Gold reprices to reflect the true credit risk of sovereign bonds. The gold-to-foreign-Treasury-debt ratio returns to the 1989 Berlin Wall level of 25%, implying gold around $11,000-13,000.

**Scenario 3: Full sovereign debt crisis (10-15% probability). Gold: $25,000-50,000+.** Loss of confidence in Western sovereign credit broadly. Multiple major economies simultaneously face funding crises. The ratio returns to its long-term historical average of 40-60% (gold at $17,000-20,000) or the 1980 panic level of 125%+ ($45,000-50,000). The US NIIP, already at -90% of GDP versus +5% in 1984, a deterioration without precedent for a reserve currency nation, makes this scenario less outlandish than consensus would have you believe.

**Scenario 4: Deflationary bust, no monetization (5-10% probability). Gold: $8,000-15,000.** Credit contraction, asset prices fall. But gold still outperforms because the risk being priced is sovereign counterparty risk, not inflation. Gold’s role as a zero-liability asset drives demand even in deflation.

**Scenario 5: Status quo holds (15-20% probability). Gold: $4,000-6,000.** Temporary stabilisation. Dollar strengthens. Gold consolidates or corrects. This is your bear case, and even it implies gold roughly flat to modestly lower from current levels. The analyst consensus has caught up: Goldman Sachs targets $5,400 by December 2026, SocGen and Deutsche Bank see $6,000.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!oY6p!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe936be4b-409c-4f2e-b84f-5e2145131554_1862x1046.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!oY6p!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe936be4b-409c-4f2e-b84f-5e2145131554_1862x1046.png)

I want to flag something about this framework that a fair-minded reader will have already noticed: every scenario I’ve outlined has gold at or above roughly current levels. My bear case is gold roughly flat. That should make you appropriately skeptical, because any probability framework where the asset in question literally cannot lose is either describing a genuinely extraordinary asymmetry or revealing the author’s bias. Probably some of both.

The scenario I’ve arguably underweighted, or perhaps excluded entirely, is a genuine deflationary shock with sustained dollar strength, something like 2008 compressed into a shorter timeframe, where gold corrects 20-30% to the $3,500-4,000 range before the policy response kicks in. I don’t think this is the most likely path, and I think any correction would be short-lived for the structural reasons I’ve outlined, but I’d be dishonest to pretend the downside from here is zero. Gold fell 45% from its 2011 peak and didn’t recover for nine years. The structural setup is different now, as I’ve argued, but “different” doesn’t mean “immune to drawdowns.”

With that caveat stated openly:

The expected value across these scenarios comes out to approximately $10,000-15,000 per ounce. Against a current price of $5,050. Against an average institutional allocation of less than 2%.

I don’t know which scenario plays out. Nobody does. But I know that the skew, the asymmetry between the upside and downside from here, is the most attractive I’ve seen in any major asset class in my career.

## **The Part Where I Address the Skeptics**

I want to take the strongest counterarguments seriously, because the gold community does itself no favours when it dismisses every objection as ignorance or conspiracy.

**“The gold flows are temporary COMEX arbitrage, not structural.”** Maybe. But arbitrage doesn’t explain why the gold is being refined into kilobars in Switzerland and shipped to Shanghai. Arbitrage flows return to origin. Settlement flows go one way. West to East via Switzerland to China is not the signature of a basis trade unwind. It’s the signature of trade settlement.

**“The US government doesn’t coordinate gold policy.”** Correct, and I’m not claiming it does. I’m claiming that the incentive structure of every major policy objective, weaker dollar, reshoring, defence independence, debt management, points in the same direction. You don’t need a conspiracy when you have aligned interests.

**“China could weaponise its gold accumulation.”** It could, but that would be self-defeating. China needs a higher gold price at least as much as America does. Chinese banks are sitting on trillions in non-performing real estate loans. A higher gold price improves the asset side of their balance sheets. The PBOC’s reserves are more robust with gold at $5,000 than at $2,000. It’s mutual interest dressed up as competition.

**“Gold has no yield, making it inferior in a high-rate environment.”** True, gold generates no income. But this objection assumes sovereign bonds are risk-free, and the entire argument of this piece is that they’re not. If sovereign bonds themselves are the risk, then the yield they offer is compensation for counterparty exposure to the most indebted entities in human history. Real yields on gold, measured in purchasing power preservation, have outperformed Treasuries on almost every meaningful timeframe. The “yield” argument is a circular reasoning trap: it assumes the conclusion (bonds are safe) to argue against the alternative (gold as a safer asset).

**“This is just another version of ‘this time is different.’”** Agreed. It isn’t different. That’s my entire point. The 50-year experiment of a pure fiat reserve currency with no gold backing (1973-2023) was the anomaly in 5,000 years of monetary history. What we’re witnessing isn’t a departure from historical norms. It’s a reversion to them. Gold served as the foundation of international trade settlement for millennia. It did so because it solved a problem that no paper currency has ever permanently solved: how do you settle accounts between parties who don’t fully trust each other?

The answer, as it turns out, hasn’t changed.
