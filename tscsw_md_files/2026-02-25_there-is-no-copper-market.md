## **The Inventory Paradox Is Not a Paradox. It Is a Map.**

Start with the data point everyone is arguing about. Combined global exchange inventories hit 1.02 million tonnes, a 23-year high. COMEX alone holds a record 534,405 tonnes. LME inventories have risen for 27 consecutive days, the longest streak since 2009. On the surface, this looks like textbook oversupply.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!HD0N!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F342b5bd0-cbee-4e3e-a71b-b053ac38cde4_1405x777.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!HD0N!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F342b5bd0-cbee-4e3e-a71b-b053ac38cde4_1405x777.png)

It is not.

What the “record stockpiles” crowd is missing: COMEX inventories have surged 5x in twelve months. Not because demand collapsed. Because traders are front-running a tariff wall. The 50% Section 232 tariff on semi-finished copper products (pipes, wires, rods, sheets, tubes) and intensive copper derivatives (cables, connectors) has been in force since August 1, 2025. Critically, the proclamation explicitly exempts ores, concentrates, cathodes, anodes, and scrap from the 50% rate. On February 20, the Supreme Court struck down IEEPA tariffs in a 6-3 ruling, but left Section 232 fully intact. Trump immediately pivoted to Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, proclaiming a 10% global tariff on February 20, effective February 24, then raising it to the statutory maximum of 15% the following day. The Section 122 authority carries a 150-day sunset, expiring July 24 unless Congress extends it.

But the Section 122 tariff is a broad, temporary replacement for the voided IEEPA regime.

The date that matters for copper specifically is June 30.

After that report, Trump may impose a phased 15% tariff on refined copper (including but not limited to cathodes) starting January 1, 2027, rising to 30% in 2028. The 50% Section 232 tariff on semi-finished copper products has been in force since August 1, 2025, and was entirely unaffected by the IEEPA ruling.This single binary outcome, tariffs enacted or tariffs abandoned, determines whether 500,000+ tonnes of copper stay trapped in U.S. warehouses or flood back into global markets.

## **Three Exchanges, Three Opposing Bets**

To be clear: COMEX, LME, and SHFE have always carried divergent positioning and regional dynamics. What is unprecedented is not the existence of fragmentation, it is the magnitude. A spread that moved from under $50 to nearly $2,900 and back to $200 in twelve months is not a difference in degree. It is a difference that behaves like a difference in kind. And it is creating a structural fragility that nobody is discussing with sufficient rigour.

**COMEX:** Western speculators and physical traders hoarding copper ahead of the tariff decision. Net long positioning in the 80th historical percentile. Warehouses are full. The bet is simple: if cathode tariffs hit in 2027, domestic copper becomes a premium product, and anyone holding physical inventory in a COMEX warehouse prints money.

**LME:** The traditional global benchmark has been drained. LME stocks fell as low as roughly 100,000 tonnes in late 2025 before recovering to approximately 184,000 tonnes by mid-February 2026, still well below the 271,000 tonnes held a year earlier. Backwardation is screaming tightness. The physical market outside the U.S. is signalling scarcity, not surplus. Western speculators are historically long here too, but the physical signal is genuine.

**SHFE:** Chinese commercials are at their widest net short since 2021. This is the contrarian data point worth watching. The world’s largest copper consumer, the country responsible for roughly 55% of global refined copper demand, has its commercial hedgers positioned for lower prices. Post-Lunar New Year demand has been soft. The stimulus hopes that powered the Q4 rally have not yet materialised in physical offtake.

Three exchanges. Three opposing narratives. One commodity.

A resolution in either direction triggers violent repricing across all three. ## **The Supply Side: What the Models Miss**

### **El Teniente’s Permanent Scar**

Bloomberg reported on February 20 that Codelco’s El Teniente, Chile’s largest underground mine, will see output stagnant at roughly 300,000 tonnes per year through the end of the decade. This is permanently down from 356,000 tonnes in 2024, following the July 2025 rockburst that killed six workers.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Th6q!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F302bc4b8-c79e-4047-9a32-e6a93a66ea10_1405x786.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Th6q!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F302bc4b8-c79e-4047-9a32-e6a93a66ea10_1405x786.png)

That is a roughly 56,000-tonne annual loss extending through the end of the decade, more than 15% of El Teniente's 2024 output.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!0PML!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff30adabd-2f73-494c-91b3-af0c0fbecc63_1405x749.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!0PML!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff30adabd-2f73-494c-91b3-af0c0fbecc63_1405x749.png)

Not a one-quarter disruption. Not a maintenance shutdown. A permanent reduction in output from one of the world’s most important copper mines, extending through at least 2030. Critically, this loss is not a future event; it is already in the current supply-demand balance, reducing available tonnes today and for every quarter through the end of the decade. And the situation may be worsening: in February 2026, Codelco fired three senior executives after an internal audit found concealment of technical data from regulators following an earlier 2023 rockburst at the same mine, raising questions about whether the geotechnical risks were systematically underreported. Major commodity desks have had time to adjust headline supply figures since mid-2025, and some likely have. But the compounding effect of the executive firings, the concealment of geotechnical data, and the possibility that the production ceiling is lower than 300,000 tonnes has not, to my knowledge, been stress-tested in published sell-side models. If I am wrong on that, the bullish case weakens marginally. But the pattern at El Teniente, a permanent loss revised downward after governance failures, is the kind of slow-moving supply destruction that models tend to underweight rather than overweight.

### **Water Is the Hard Cap**

Something the macro tourists never discuss: water scarcity in Chile is not an environmental talking point. It is a production constraint with arithmetic consequences.

BHP's Cerro Colorado and Anglo's Los Bronces have collectively seen severe water-driven production impacts, with Los Bronces plant throughput cut by up to 44% and copper output falling roughly 28% at its trough, with Cerro Colorado's decline far more severe (the mine effectively entered care and maintenance in late 2023 after its environmental permit expired, compounded by court-ordered groundwater extraction bans and the broader drought). Chile’s reservoir capacity is down to roughly 30%. By 2031, 47% of mining water will need to come from desalinated seawater pumped to 2,000 to 4,000 metres altitude, adding $500 million or more per project to capex. Capstone's Mantoverde was forced to fully halt sulphide operations in January 2026 after a labour strike disrupted its desalination plant, reducing total output to roughly 50 to 75% of normal capacity before a new three-year agreement was reached in February.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!A-YD!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0f274fa9-a338-499d-b081-0187b7be43d1_1405x752.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!A-YD!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0f274fa9-a338-499d-b081-0187b7be43d1_1405x752.png)

This matters for the price-elastic supply response that bears rely on. The standard argument is: high copper prices incentivise new supply, which eventually cures high prices. Ed Morse’s framework. But what if the marginal tonne of Chilean supply requires a $500 million desalination plant, environmental permits that take years, and a pipeline running up the side of a mountain? The supply response function has changed. It is slower, more capital-intensive, and more fragile than the models assume.

# Beyond the 12-Month Trade

## Leaching Technology: The Wild Card Nobody Models

The supply-side analysis above focuses on constraints binding today and through 2027. But the most intellectually interesting variable in the copper market operates on a longer timeline, and almost zero equity research addresses it properly.

Rio Tinto’s Nuton technology and Jetti Resources are commercialising bio-heap leaching processes that extract copper from previously uneconomic sulfide ores and mine waste. The numbers are startling: Nuton achieves up to 85% copper recovery from primary sulfides, versus 25 to 35% for conventional methods. It produces 99.99% pure cathode on-site without smelting, completely bypassing the TC/RC crisis (more on that below). Nuton signed Amazon Web Services as its exclusive launch customer for data centre copper supply, with Rio Tinto confirming discussions with additional offtakers are underway.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!b34z!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F43734881-75a3-472e-bf3b-791febddaa41_1405x780.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!b34z!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F43734881-75a3-472e-bf3b-791febddaa41_1405x780.png)

The kicker: Freeport estimates 19 billion pounds of currently unrecoverable copper sitting at Morenci alone. Rio estimates 100 million tonnes of copper in global mine waste piles. Arizona Sonoran Copper is testing Nuton at its Cactus project and reporting 61 to 82% extraction rates.

If these technologies scale, and that is still an “if,” they fundamentally change the supply deficit narrative. Not by eliminating it, but by creating a new source of supply that existing models do not capture.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!vvKw!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F37bac858-5c54-4ae5-b382-f711c08365d4_1405x787.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!vvKw!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F37bac858-5c54-4ae5-b382-f711c08365d4_1405x787.png)

The timeline is uncertain, and that is the key qualifier. Commercial scale-up of bio-heap leaching on primary sulfides is a three-to-seven-year proposition at best. Nuton’s Amazon deal is a bespoke offtake, not a proof of repeatable industrial throughput. This is why leaching sits in an appendix rather than in the core thesis: for the 12-to-24-month trade anchored to June 30, it is a footnote. For the 2030+ supply picture, it could be the most important variable in the entire copper market. Almost nobody in equity research is pricing it in, on either the bull or bear side.The companies with the most direct exposure to this technology are Rio Tinto (Nuton developer), Freeport-McMoRan (Morenci waste pile, Jetti investor), BHP (Jetti investor, Escondida sulphide potential), and Arizona Sonoran Copper (testing Nuton at Cactus). Whether and when this translates into investable conviction depends on commercial scale-up data that does not yet exist. This section is informational context for the long-term supply picture, not an actionable recommendation.

## **The Demand Side: The Vector Nobody Counted**

### **Defence Spending as a Price-Insensitive Buyer**

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!b5Pl!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe266966d-9d8c-4dd6-af1d-6da83c84c4c4_1405x464.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!b5Pl!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe266966d-9d8c-4dd6-af1d-6da83c84c4c4_1405x464.png)

In January 2026, S&P Global published “Copper in the Age of AI,” the first major institutional study to formally designate defence as a discrete copper demand vector alongside AI, energy transition, and core economic consumption. The headline finding: combined demand from AI/data centres and global defence spending is projected to add approximately 4 million metric tonnes of annual copper consumption by 2040, with each vector roughly tripling from current levels. Defence spending itself could double to $6 trillion by 2040 amid rising international tensions. Carlos Pascual, S&P’s Senior Vice President of Geopolitics and International Affairs (and former U.S. Ambassador to Ukraine), stated plainly that copper demand in the defence sector is “structurally inelastic.”

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!WP7k!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fea934fa4-b6a5-4995-84e1-15b912352128_1405x787.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!WP7k!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fea934fa4-b6a5-4995-84e1-15b912352128_1405x787.png)

That S&P designation matters because it establishes institutional consensus where previously there was only fragmentary evidence. The most detailed bottom-up estimate of military copper consumption comes from Simon Hunt Strategic Services, cited by the Modern War Institute at West Point, which pegged global military copper usage at 2.19 million tonnes in 2021, roughly 9% of global refined output, growing at approximately 14% per year through 2026.

That estimate deserves scepticism: the 14% growth rate implies a near-doubling in five years, which would be extraordinary for any industrial commodity demand vector, and nobody has independently verified Hunt’s 2.19 million tonne figure at the granular level. S&P Global’s designation validates defence as a category, not Hunt’s specific numbers.

What we can say with higher confidence is directional: S&P projects combined AI and defence demand adding approximately 4 million tonnes by 2040, global military spending surged 9.4% in 2024 to $2.718 trillion, and the procurement commitments detailed below are funded and contractually underway. If defence accounts for even a quarter of S&P’s combined figure, that is 1 million tonnes of incremental demand by 2040 from a buyer class that does not appear in most copper supply-demand models. The precise tonnage remains genuinely uncertain. The structural characteristic does not: this demand is additive and non-cyclical.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!R0s9!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F95d8f901-3adc-4463-9688-f91b83accd44_1405x775.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!R0s9!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F95d8f901-3adc-4463-9688-f91b83accd44_1405x775.png)

The granular data points reinforce the direction. Global military spending hit $2.718 trillion in 2024, up 9.4%, the steepest one-year surge since the Cold War. This is not a forecast that rests on a single source or a stale baseline. It is a convergence: S&P Global’s institutional modelling, Hunt’s operational estimate (however imperfect), NATO’s stated procurement timelines, and verifiable production ramp data all point in the same direction. Defence is an emerging fifth demand vector, after construction, EVs, grid, and renewables. Its near-term tonnage is modest relative to construction or EVs.

What makes it structurally significant is the characteristic of the demand: non-cyclical, price-inelastic, and growing on funded procurement timelines that operate independently of the copper price. Most copper supply-demand models still exclude it entirely. That omission, not the uncertainty around the precise tonnage, is the analytical gap that matters.

## **The Bottleneck Behind the Bottleneck: Zero TC/RCs**

If you only remember one data point from this piece, make it this one.

The 2026 benchmark treatment charge / refining charge for copper concentrate was set at $0 per tonne. Zero. The 2024 benchmark was $80; the 2025 benchmark had already collapsed to $21.25, and the historical range spans roughly $50 to $107. Spot TCs have gone negative, meaning smelters are effectively paying miners for concentrate, subsidising feedstock acquisition at a loss just to keep plants running.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!_iv-!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4ded02dd-39f3-48d9-bc32-6c05f456806b_1405x789.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!_iv-!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4ded02dd-39f3-48d9-bc32-6c05f456806b_1405x789.png)

This is the clearest signal of physical copper scarcity at the mine level. There is not enough concentrate to feed existing smelter capacity. Chinese smelters, unable to cover operating costs at zero or negative TCs, are planning over 10% output cuts. This creates a second bottleneck beyond mining itself: even if you mine the copper, you may not be able to get it refined, because the smelters that would process your concentrate are curtailing capacity or demanding onerous terms to stay operational.

The equity implication is direct. Miners with integrated smelting capacity, Freeport's Gresik smelter in Indonesia, Ivanhoe's new Kamoa-Kakula smelter (Africa's largest at 500,000 tonnes per annum, first anodes produced December 29, 2025), have a structural margin advantage because they bypass the third-party smelting bottleneck entirely. Non-integrated miners face the risk that custom smelters curtail capacity at zero or negative TCs, leaving their concentrate without a processing route regardless of the terms. Ivanhoe’s smelter halves logistics costs and is designed to generate up to 700,000 tonnes per year of sulphuric acid (per Ivanhoe’s January 2026 disclosure, spot prices in Kolwezi have reached $700 per tonne following Zambia’s September 2025 acid export ban, though management expects realised prices to average $350 to $400 over the medium term). That margin expansion is not in consensus numbers.

## Quantifying the Gap: An Adjusted Structural Balance

Most published copper supply-demand balances disagree on the headline number. Goldman sees a surplus; JPMorgan sees a 330,000-tonne deficit. But both start from the same baseline methodology: forecast mine supply, forecast demand from construction, EVs, grid, and renewables, and subtract one from the other. The disagreement is largely about Chinese demand assumptions and inventory cycle timing.

Neither, to my knowledge, adjusts for the three structural variables outlined in this piece. So here is a simple exercise: take the consensus range and layer on the factors that most models either exclude or underweight.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!0tnX!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F160c5295-c6b3-4627-9978-09a2139fc4d1_1405x726.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!0tnX!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F160c5295-c6b3-4627-9978-09a2139fc4d1_1405x726.png)

Start with the midpoint of the Goldman-JPMorgan range, which implies a roughly balanced market to modest deficit of around negative 100,000 to negative 150,000 tonnes for 2026. A caveat before adjusting: some of these factors, particularly El Teniente’s headline production downgrade, are partially captured in existing sell-side models. The adjustments below therefore represent the incremental risk beyond what consensus has already incorporated, not the total magnitude of each factor. Where overlap is likely, I note it. Now adjust.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!bxXp!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc92edc6c-abf7-4f82-8d6d-17ab38dd3927_1405x773.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!bxXp!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc92edc6c-abf7-4f82-8d6d-17ab38dd3927_1405x773.png)

El Teniente governance risk: the headline downgrade is likely in most models, but the incremental downside from executive firings and concealed geotechnical data is not. I estimate 10,000 to 30,000 tonnes of additional risk beyond what consensus has captured.

Chilean water-stressed capacity at risk (partially correlated with the El Teniente adjustment above, as both reflect Chilean institutional and environmental governance failures; the ranges below are calibrated to avoid double-counting): BHP's Cerro Colorado is effectively in care and maintenance.

Capstone’s Mantoverde ran at roughly 55% capacity during the January 2026 strike. Los Bronces has seen cumulative output reductions of up to 44%. Aggregating across Chile’s water-stressed operations, I estimate 30,000 to 60,000 tonnes of annualised production that is either already offline or operationally fragile, meaning it can drop out of the balance in any given quarter due to drought, permit revocation, or labour disputes at desalination infrastructure. This is not a forecast of permanent loss; it is a measure of supply that consensus treats as firm but that operates with structurally higher variance than the models assume.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Qxon!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8e9f9a37-e528-49b2-89d8-733941c07015_1405x773.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Qxon!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8e9f9a37-e528-49b2-89d8-733941c07015_1405x773.png)

Defence demand not in consensus models: 50,000 to 75,000 tonnes of incremental annual demand by 2027, scaling toward 1 million tonnes by 2040. Small relative to the 26-million-tonne global market, but additive to a balance that is already tight, and absent from both the Goldman and JPMorgan frameworks.

TC/RC-implied refining constraint: if the zero-TC environment drives even a 3 to 5% net reduction in Chinese refined output, that implies 350,000 to 650,000 tonnes of refined copper that the market expects but may not receive. Some concentrate will find alternative processing, and smelter cuts may partially reverse, so this carries the widest confidence interval of any adjustment.

Adjusted structural balance excluding the refining constraint: starting from a consensus midpoint of negative 100,000 to negative 150,000 tonnes, the supply-side and defence adjustments alone produce a range of roughly negative 190,000 to negative 315,000 tonnes for 2026. This is the higher-confidence estimate, built on variables with tighter confidence intervals. Including the refining constraint widens the range to roughly negative 200,000 to negative 450,000 tonnes, but the TC/RC adjustment carries a confidence interval wide enough (350,000 to 650,000 tonnes of at-risk refined output) that it should be treated as a scenario overlay rather than a base-case input.

Even the narrower range, excluding refining risk, implies a market tighter than any single published sell-side estimate I have seen that does not incorporate defence demand. The wider range, including refining, represents the tail risk if Chinese smelter cuts materialise at scale.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!fgJu!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3967176c-7072-45f0-9cd3-f22477f063f8_1405x782.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!fgJu!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3967176c-7072-45f0-9cd3-f22477f063f8_1405x782.png)

This is not a forecast. It is a framework for stress-testing consensus against variables that consensus excludes. If any of the adjustments above are wrong, the reader can remove them and recalculate. That is the point: the methodology is transparent, and the inputs are individually falsifiable. The output is only as good as the weakest assumption. But the exercise itself, asking “what does the balance look like when you include the factors nobody models?”, is one I have not seen performed elsewhere.

## **The Framework: How to Think About This**

Here is how I would structure the thinking.

**The near-term catalyst** is June 30. The Commerce Secretary’s report determines the trajectory of U.S. copper tariffs. This is binary and datable. If you are managing risk around copper equities, this is your event.

**The medium-term structure** is the fragmentation I outlined above. It resolves within 12 months. The resolution creates asymmetric outcomes for producers with geographic exposure to the “right” side of the tariff wall

**The long-term thesis** is unchanged and strengthening. Electrification, AI infrastructure, grid expansion, defence spending, andIndian industrialisation (where copper demand surged 16% in FY23 while domestic smelting capacity remains structurally constrained following the 2018 Sterlite closure, a dynamic we will address in a dedicated piece) are five demand vectors running simultaneously against a supply side constrained by water scarcity, grade decline, permitting timelines, and a smelting bottleneck at zero TCs. Sprott’s BloombergNEF figure of a $122 billion investment gap needed by 2035 is, if anything, conservative once you add defence demand.

**The contrarian risk** is Shapiro’s positioning argument. He is right that speculative longs are crowded. He is right that crowded positioning shifts risk/reward. The question is whether the structural fundamentals are strong enough to absorb a positioning washout and still resolve higher. I think they are, but the path between here and there may include a meaningful drawdown if the June 30 report is dovish on tariffs or if Chinese demand fails to recover.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!dTjT!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F83f85f62-08ff-4cf2-96d8-7242168dc127_1405x784.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!dTjT!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F83f85f62-08ff-4cf2-96d8-7242168dc127_1405x784.png)

Before turning to the leaching wildcard, here is how I would frame the three scenarios and their relative likelihood.

Scenario 1: Tariffs enacted on cathodes (phased 15% from January 2027). I weight this at approximately 40%, making it the most likely single outcome but not a majority probability. The reasoning: the Commerce Department has been tracking toward tariff implementation since the Section 232 investigation was opened. The administration’s revealed preference, demonstrated by the 50% tariff on semi-finished products already in force since August 2025, is for copper protectionism. Abandoning cathode tariffs would require the Commerce report to find domestic supply adequate, which is difficult to argue when the U.S. imports roughly half its refined copper consumption. Against that, the administration has shown willingness to defer tariff timelines when broader trade negotiations are in play (the Section 122 pivot being the most recent example). If enacted, LME copper reprices to $10,500 to $11,500 per tonne within six months as non-U.S. physical tightness intensifies. COMEX premium persists and widens. FCX, Teck, and Ivanhoe are the primary beneficiaries. FCX re-rates on domestic strategic value with production scaling 60% by 2030.

Scenario 2: Tariffs abandoned or indefinitely deferred. I weight this at roughly 25%. The Commerce report finds domestic supply adequate or the administration deprioritises copper in favour of broader trade negotiations. The trapped COMEX inventory re-enters global circulation. LME backwardation collapses. Copper pulls back to $8,500 to $9,200. SHFE shorts are vindicated. This is the positioning washout Shapiro warns about, and it would be painful for leveraged longs. But I would view it as a buying opportunity against the structural deficit, not a thesis invalidation.

Scenario 3: Delayed or ambiguous outcome. I weight this at roughly 35%, and it may be underappreciated. The Commerce report is published but non-committal, or the tariff timeline is extended. This is consistent with the administration’s pattern of using tariff threats as negotiating leverage rather than implementing them on stated schedules. Fragmentation persists. Copper trades in a volatile $9,000 to $10,500 range while the market waits for resolution. This is the worst scenario for equity positioning because it extends the uncertainty without resolving it.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!nevk!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Faee44e93-72e3-4d6c-8a2c-6cb400ea854a_1405x782.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!nevk!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Faee44e93-72e3-4d6c-8a2c-6cb400ea854a_1405x782.png)

These are point estimates on inherently political outcomes, so treat them as centres of gravity rather than precise calibrations. A reasonable case exists for weighting the delayed scenario higher (closer to 40%) and tariff enactment lower (closer to 35%), given the administration's revealed preference for using tariff threats as negotiating leverage rather than implementing them on stated schedules. I weight enactment slightly higher because the Section 232 investigation was specifically designed to produce a tariff recommendation, and because abandoning cathode tariffs while maintaining the 50% on semi-finished products would be internally inconsistent. But I hold this weighting loosely.

What matters more than the exact weights is the shape of the distribution: it is bimodal, not normal. The probability-weighted expected LME copper price lands somewhere around $9,700 to $10,200 over the next twelve months, but the average understates the tails. Position for the tails.

## The Reverse Thesis: What Makes This Wrong

Any honest framework requires a falsification criterion. Here is what would cause me to abandon or substantially downgrade the structural bull case, not just the near-term trade, but the multi-year deficit thesis itself.

Chinese demand structurally disappoints. The bull case assumes Chinese copper consumption, currently roughly 55% of global demand, at least holds steady and eventually recovers as stimulus feeds through. But there is a scenario where China’s property sector never recovers to pre-2022 construction intensity, EV copper loading plateaus as manufacturers optimise designs, and the stimulus impulse remains targeted at sectors with lower copper intensity (services, consumer subsidies) rather than infrastructure. If Chinese refined copper demand contracts by 3 to 5% annually for two or more consecutive years, the structural deficit closes regardless of supply-side constraints. The SHFE commercial short positioning may be telling us exactly this. I am watching monthly SHFE warrant cancellations and Chinese copper semis output as the leading indicators. If both remain flat or declining through Q3 2026, the demand leg of the thesis is in trouble.

Substitution accelerates beyond historical rates. Aluminium is already replacing copper in certain power cable applications, and fibre optics displace copper in data transmission. Historically, substitution has been slow (1 to 2% of addressable demand per decade) because copper’s conductivity advantage is large enough that the cost of redesigning systems exceeds the material savings. But if copper sustains $11,000+ per tonne for multiple years, the economics of substitution shift. The threshold I am watching is aluminium-to-copper price ratio: if it falls below 3.5x on a sustained basis (currently around 4.2x), the redesign economics begin to pencil for high-voltage applications. This is a slow-burn risk, not a 2026 event, but it is the mechanism by which a sustained high price can destroy its own demand base.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!GEYT!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fddf32766-e994-42e7-8a2f-2fd413bd69bc_1405x712.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!GEYT!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fddf32766-e994-42e7-8a2f-2fd413bd69bc_1405x712.png)

Supply response from jurisdictions outside Chile. The thesis leans heavily on Chilean supply constraints (water, El Teniente, permitting). But copper is mined globally. If DRC production ramps faster than expected (Kamoa Phase 4, additional Chinese-backed greenfields in the Copperbelt), or if Indonesian output from Grasberg’s block cave exceeds guidance, or if Mongolian concentrate from Oyu Tolgoi scales ahead of schedule, the supply-side deficit narrows without Chilean constraints needing to ease. The aggregate production guidance from these three jurisdictions for 2026 to 2028 is the metric to track. If combined output exceeds consensus by more than 200,000 tonnes annually, the deficit thesis weakens materially.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!05k-!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fefb22086-df74-4d17-8d93-289514237723_1405x780.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!05k-!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fefb22086-df74-4d17-8d93-289514237723_1405x780.png)

Leaching technology scales faster than the appendix assumes. I framed Nuton and Jetti as three-to-seven-year propositions. If Arizona Sonoran’s Cactus results in 2026 show commercial-scale recovery rates above 75% at costs below $2.00 per pound, and if Freeport announces a full-scale Morenci leach deployment before 2028, the 2030+ supply picture changes fundamentally. This is bullish for FCX and RIO specifically but bearish for the copper price and for the deficit narrative that underpins the entire thesis.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!YbSO!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdca091ef-1d1f-41e2-85c6-120c9fbc168a_1405x782.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!YbSO!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdca091ef-1d1f-41e2-85c6-120c9fbc168a_1405x782.png)

The kill signal is not any one of these in isolation. It is two or more occurring simultaneously. Chinese demand structurally declining while non-Chilean supply ramps would close the deficit from both sides. Substitution accelerating while leaching scales would mean demand destruction meeting supply creation. Any single factor can be absorbed by the remaining tightness in the balance. Two concurrent factors cannot. I will revisit this reverse thesis explicitly in each subsequent copper update, and if the evidence shifts, I will say so.

## **Conclusion**

The copper market in February 2026 is not complicated because the fundamentals are unclear. It is complicated because the market structure has fractured into three distinct arenas with opposing positioning, and most analysis still treats “the copper price” as a single number with a single narrative.

It is not. It is three prices, three narratives, and a June 30 catalyst that determines which one wins.

The mainstream debate, JPMorgan deficit versus Goldman surplus, Kobeissi inventory bears versus Tavi Costa structural bulls, is a debate about a market that no longer exists in singular form. The real edge is in understanding the fragmentation itself, and positioning for the resolution.

I think this resolves higher. Not because the positioning is clean (it is not), and not because the path will be smooth (it will not be). A dovish Commerce report on June 30 triggers the positioning washout discussed above, and anyone managing copper equity exposure needs to size for it.

But the positioning risk and the structural thesis operate on different timeframes, and conflating them is the mistake most commentary makes. A positioning flush does not reverse permanent mine-level supply losses, build desalination infrastructure, produce concentrate for smelters bidding zero TCs, or slow a defence procurement cycle that most copper equities still do not reflect in forward valuations. The supply constraints outlined above are physical, not speculative, and they survive any near-term drawdown intact.

The near-term risk is genuine. The medium-term fragmentation resolves within twelve months. The long-term deficit is arithmetic. And the probability-weighted resolution, across all three scenarios, points to a clearing price above where we sit today. June 30 determines the speed. The direction is already set.
