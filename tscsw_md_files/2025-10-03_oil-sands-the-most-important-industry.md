### **Why do incentives rule the oil sands?**

Most people think winning is about being the strongest or the fastest.

But in any complex game, the real winners are the ones who understand the rules *behind* the rules.

They see the invisible forces that shape the outcome before anyone else does. It’s not about fighting the current; it’s about knowing which way it’s flowing and using it to your advantage.

This is the power of understanding **Incentives**.

Think about a professional cyclist in the Tour de France. The amateur sees someone pedaling furiously.

The expert sees a master of physics, drafting behind teammates to save precious energy, knowing exactly when to break away. They aren't just playing the game; they're playing the *incentives*, the reward for conserving energy, the prize for a breakaway, the penalty for a miscalculation.

When you apply this lens to a colossal, multi-decade industry like the Canadian Oil Sands, everything starts to make more sense.

The game isn't just about pulling oil out of the ground. It's a marathon where the rules, the terrain, and even the finish line keep changing.

Imagine the oil sands industry as an ultramarathon runner. In the early miles, the incentive was simple: run fast, build a lead. The prize was huge, and the track was clear.

But now, the runner is deep into the race.

The sun is beating down (climate pressure), the muscles are aching (high costs), and the path ahead is full of steep hills (regulation) and unexpected turns (geopolitics).

The old incentive (just run) is no longer enough.

Now, the incentives are about survival, efficiency, and finding a smarter way to finish the race. Some are trying to invent new running shoes mid-stride; others are just trying to make it to the next water station.

### **Case for Canadian oil sands**

Every meaningful shift in how a sector is valued begins when the cash it generates becomes steadier and stronger than the market expects.

The Canadian oil sands are at that point today.

What once was known mainly as a story of big spending that revolved around massive mines, decade-long buildouts, and heavy up-front costs, has matured into something more reliable. These projects now resemble large-scale industrial plants with decades of life ahead of them, slow natural decline, and operating discipline that was hardened during the 2014–2015 price crash from the U.S. shale boom and global demand slowdown.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!SbMF!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F599a6727-663b-46d8-ab22-4b182fc4c27f_1600x900.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!SbMF!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F599a6727-663b-46d8-ab22-4b182fc4c27f_1600x900.png)

The logic is simple.

In commodity markets, investors tend to mark down future earnings because production levels, prices, and costs can swing widely. B

ut three big changes are reducing that uncertainty for Canada’s oil sands.

**First,** the Trans Mountain Pipeline Expansion (TMX) is now running, giving producers a safety valve that helps keep price discounts from blowing out.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!rr11!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa92f3bdd-2533-4f0b-85fe-a424449b6196_1600x893.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!rr11!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa92f3bdd-2533-4f0b-85fe-a424449b6196_1600x893.png)

**Second,** in a world unsettled by conflict and supply risks, buyers are willing to pay more for oil from stable, democratic countries where contracts are honored.

**Third,** access to the Pacific coast means Canada is no longer tied almost entirely to one customer in the U.S. Instead, it can sell into a wider set of markets. With more buyers competing for supply, producers do not need luck or perfect timing to improve returns. They just need to move their barrels to market.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!cjo_!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F13ec01cd-a681-409a-8bba-dfce45e8f05e_1600x877.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!cjo_!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F13ec01cd-a681-409a-8bba-dfce45e8f05e_1600x877.png)

What happened after 2014 reshaped the industry.

Faced with a painful price crash, oil sands companies rebuilt their operations to be far tougher on costs.

Today, they can keep going at oil prices in the low $40s per barrel because they have simplified operations, removed bottlenecks, added more efficient power systems, and leaned on automation.

The focus has shifted from chasing big new projects to carefully maintaining what already exists.

That means when prices are stronger and steadier, most of the extra revenue turns directly into free cash. This is the heart of the bullish case for oil sands.

Cash flows are now more reliable, most of that cash goes back to shareholders through dividends and buybacks, and we believe the market has yet to fully price in this new reality.

These catalysts don’t just matter technically, they reshape the incentives driving producers and buyers alike

### **Summary of Key Catalysts**

The core of this thesis rests on three transformative pillars:

a) **The first catalyst is the TMX** that started on May 1, 2024, adding about 590,000 barrels a day of new capacity to Canada’s Pacific Coast. That brings the total system to nearly 900,000 barrels a day. More than just another pipeline, it is a major piece of infrastructure that steadies price swings by giving producers extra capacity to move oil even when other pipelines are full. Instead of barrels backing up in Alberta and being forced to sell at steep discounts, producers now have a direct route to the Pacific Coast. From there, tankers can reach refineries in Asia and on the U.S. West Coast, giving Canadian oil access to more buyers and more stable prices.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!hTtZ!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc068342c-d651-4aeb-9506-9433cb18f746_1600x895.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!hTtZ!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc068342c-d651-4aeb-9506-9433cb18f746_1600x895.png)

b) **The second catalyst is global politics.** Buyers now value oil from countries that are safe and reliable. The war in Ukraine and renewed instability in the Middle East (including attacks on shipping routes) have forced consuming nations such as Europe, Asia, North America to rethink energy security as a priority, not a background concern. Canada’s proposition underpinned by stable government, clear rules, and very long reserve life, make its oil more attractive and more valuable. The world has fewer swing suppliers today. U.S. shale growth has slowed, OPEC spare capacity is thinner, and sanctions have sidelined large chunks of Russian oil. That makes any disruption in unstable regions hit harder.

c) **The third catalyst is commercial diversification.** Before TMX, Canadian oil was almost entirely tied to U.S. refiners. Now, with Pacific access, shipments are already heading to Asia, especially China, as well as to the U.S. West Coast. This reduces reliance on one customer and creates competition among buyers, which pushes prices higher.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!O3OW!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6409e502-c3e3-4b7e-a998-2f5b404cd92e_1600x898.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!O3OW!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6409e502-c3e3-4b7e-a998-2f5b404cd92e_1600x898.png)

Finally, what some see as a risk, possible U.S. tariffs, actually makes diversification more important.

If one market can suddenly change the rules, having multiple outlets is insurance, not a luxury.

TMX provides that insurance in the form of steel in the ground.

## **The TMX Catalyst: Unlocking a Landlocked Resource**

### **A History of the WCS–WTI Differential**

For more than ten years, the biggest challenge for the Canadian oil sands has been the steep and often unpredictable price discount applied to its crude.

This gap, called the WCS–WTI differential, has cut deeply into revenues and made earnings volatile for investors.

The differential arises from two fundamental factors.

First is a quality discount. WCS is a heavy, sour crude, which is harder and more costly to refine into products like gasoline compared to the lighter, sweeter WTI.

Second, transportation: Canadian oil has to travel long distances by pipeline to reach major markets, especially the U.S. Gulf Coast. In normal conditions, these factors mean a discount of about US$11–15 per barrel.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!kb_w!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F44e0bc7d-6af9-468d-a29a-9eaa328aeb11_1600x867.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!kb_w!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F44e0bc7d-6af9-468d-a29a-9eaa328aeb11_1600x867.png)

But in reality, the discount has often been much worse.

Production growth regularly outpaced new pipeline capacity, leaving barrels stranded in Alberta when the system was full. In those cases, producers had to turn to rail, which can add another US$5–10 per barrel in costs. Because market prices are set by the cost of moving the marginal barrel, this forced the entire WCS price lower.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Kx3Y!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F98f8348a-0aab-4293-904d-0a338d54458b_1600x893.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Kx3Y!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F98f8348a-0aab-4293-904d-0a338d54458b_1600x893.png)

At its worst in late 2018, the gap between WCS and WTI blew out to US$43 per barrel, wiping billions of dollars from the Canadian economy. Episodes like this made oil sands cash flows look riskier than those of peers in other regions.

### **TMX: Operational and Economic Impact Analysis**

The TMX is a landmark project that solves the pipeline bottleneck which had troubled the oil sands industry for years. After more than a decade of regulatory delays and construction hurdles, the C$34 billion expansion finally began operating on May 1, 2024, opening a new chapter for Canadian oil exports.

The project involved building a second line alongside the existing Trans Mountain pipeline, stretching from Edmonton, Alberta, to Burnaby, British Columbia.

**With this expansion, capacity nearly tripled from 300,000 to 890,000 barrels a day**

A massive increase, equal to about 13% more total pipeline space leaving Western Canada.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!HORb!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4f905d12-71a4-4233-8d32-3d0446f58629_1600x903.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!HORb!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4f905d12-71a4-4233-8d32-3d0446f58629_1600x903.png)

The impact was immediate.

The WCS-WTI differential, which historically widened significantly in the fall due to seasonal refinery maintenance in the U.S. Midwest, defied this trend in late 2024.

Instead, the gap narrowed, a clear sign that barrels were finding an alternative outlet to the Pacific Coast.

Alberta Central estimated this improvement boosted producer revenues by about C$10 billion in 2024 alone. While short-term market hiccups can still cause swings, the long-term improvement is clear.

The bigger story is stability.

**Before TMX, pipelines often ran at full capacity, meaning any disruption, like a refinery shutdown, could cause the price gap to “blow out” to extreme levels.**

TMX now provides a buffer of spare capacity, acting as insurance against those damaging spikes. Even as oil sands output grows, this cushion is expected to last for years, creating a steadier environment for producers.

**For investors, this matters because value is tied to how predictable future cash flows are**.

The pre-TMX era was marked by wild swings in earnings due to transport limits. TMX reduces that risk, capping the price gap and making revenues more reliable. That means investors can apply a lower risk discount, which should lift valuations across the sector. As one analyst put it, the biggest benefit of TMX is not just smaller price gaps. It is the much lower chance of another disastrous blowout.

### **WTI–WCS Outlook & Market Repricing**

We expect the price gap between Western Canadian Select (WCS) and West Texas Intermediate (WTI) to stay narrower and more stable than in the past.

This matters because a smaller, steadier discount lifts revenues, improves margins, and creates more reliable free cash flow for producers.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!r37Y!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F094c9a07-a67c-497d-b275-3bb659d6a109_1600x900.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!r37Y!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F094c9a07-a67c-497d-b275-3bb659d6a109_1600x900.png)

The industry’s cost structure has also been reshaped since the 2014–15 downturn.

Most large operators can now break even with oil prices in the **$40–43 per barrel WTI range**.

S&P Global’s 2025 outlook estimates average half-cycle costs at around **$27 per barrel** WTI, with a range of $18–45 depending on the project type. That means companies are positioned to stay profitable even if prices fall well below recent averages.

With TMX online, cash flows are not only higher but steadier.

Producers no longer live with the risk of “differential blowouts.” This stability lowers the risk premium investors normally attach to oil sands cash flows.

In valuation terms, it means not just stronger earnings but also a higher multiple, since investors are willing to pay more for cash that is predictable.

Taken together, this creates the basis for a sector re-rating: a mix of higher realized prices, leaner cost structures, and dependable free cash flow that the market has not yet fully recognized.

### **A Global Energy Powerhouse**

The Canadian oil sands are one of the cornerstones of global energy because of both their size and the unusual way they are open to private investors.

**Canada is the world’s fourth-largest oil producer and holds the fourth-largest proven reserves, behind only Venezuela, Saudi Arabia, and Iran.**

Nearly all of Canada’s reserves, about 159 of ~163 billion barrels, are in the oil sands, putting the country firmly in the top tier of energy powers.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!GIAD!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F68409c95-0898-4c10-896a-4ded7d1c3a98_1600x900.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!GIAD!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F68409c95-0898-4c10-896a-4ded7d1c3a98_1600x900.png)

What makes these reserves stand out is how they are governed.

Most of the world’s oil is locked up by state-owned companies, which limits outside investment.

Canada is different.

Its oil sands are developed under a stable, market-based system that allows global investors to participate. In fact, it is estimated that the oil sands make up more than half of all oil reserves in the world that private investors can access, a rarity in today’s energy landscape.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!vsJa!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd1da7bb6-9871-4625-8c0c-de5c2404f4cc_1600x897.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!vsJa!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd1da7bb6-9871-4625-8c0c-de5c2404f4cc_1600x897.png)

The way these resources behave adds another advantage.

Shale wells and conventional oil fields decline quickly and need constant reinvestment to keep production flat.

Oil sands projects, once built, are more like industrial plants. They run for decades with steady output and only modest reinvestment. For example, Canadian Natural Resources’ major mines and upgraders still have more than 40 years of reserves left.

This means **production is stable and predictable, and a much larger share of operating cash can flow straight to debt repayment, dividends, or share buybacks, instead of constantly being ploughed back into drilling.**

For investors, that combination of stability, visibility, and long asset life offers a rare mix of lower risk and higher, more dependable returns.

### **Market Size & Forecast**

The Canadian oil sands are part of a large and steadily growing global market for unconventional oil.

Unconventional oil refers to petroleum extracted using unconventional methods, such as hydraulic fracturing (fracking), oil sands, and oil shale production, which differ from traditional drilling techniques.

By 2024, the market for unconventional oil was valued at about US$355 billion. Looking ahead, it is projected to exceed US$500 billion by the early 2030s, growing at more than 4% CAGR.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!OwNY!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5a91b4ca-2168-48a8-b95a-269a8ae82003_1600x862.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!OwNY!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5a91b4ca-2168-48a8-b95a-269a8ae82003_1600x862.png)

North America, particularly Alberta’s oil sands, remains the largest contributor to this market.

In 2024, Canadian oil sands output averaged 3.3 million barrels per day, and outlooks from S&P Global suggest volumes will climb toward 3.7 million barrels per day by 2035.

While Asia-Pacific is expected to show faster demand growth in the coming years, North America will remain the anchor of global oil sands production and investment.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-Rft!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ffc32cdd9-8530-481f-85b9-402bbc1051c5_1600x894.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-Rft!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ffc32cdd9-8530-481f-85b9-402bbc1051c5_1600x894.png)

### **Segmentation Analysis**

Breaking the market into its key segments helps explain what is driving growth and how the industry is changing.

* **By Extraction Method:** Oil sands are developed in two ways: surface mining for deposits near the surface, and in-situ recovery for deeper reserves. Mining was the original method, but in-situ now dominates since about 80% of reserves are too deep to mine.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!xj53!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fcefe4627-77b8-4992-9979-e725566e4467_1600x894.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!xj53!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fcefe4627-77b8-4992-9979-e725566e4467_1600x894.png)

In 2024, oil sands output continued to grow, with most of the increase coming from in-situ projects. In-situ is expected to remain the fastest-growing method because it disturbs less land, can be expanded in smaller steps, and applies to a much larger share of the total resource.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!QMtR!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff5919482-af3c-4f17-bc49-45853c2567a5_1600x889.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!QMtR!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff5919482-af3c-4f17-bc49-45853c2567a5_1600x889.png)

* **By Product Type:** The industry mainly produces two types of oil: raw bitumen (blended into Dilbit for shipping) and Synthetic Crude Oil (SCO), which comes from upgrading facilities. In 2024, Canadian oil sands output included about 125 million cubic metres of bitumen and just under 70 million cubic metres of SCO, shifting the mix to roughly 64% Dilbit and 36% SCO. This marks a clear move toward Dilbit as in-situ projects expand and no new upgrader capacity is added.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!aY53!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F81094862-f02d-46df-8cfe-15e0808fc894_1600x887.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!aY53!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F81094862-f02d-46df-8cfe-15e0808fc894_1600x887.png)

* **By Application:** Most oil sands output ends up in the transportation sector, refined into gasoline, diesel, and jet fuel. Other uses include industrial fuels, petrochemical feedstocks (for plastics and other materials), and, to a smaller extent, power generation. Over time, the demand for transportation fuels will remain the biggest factor shaping the industry’s growth.

### **Bitumen vs. Synthetic Crude Oil (SCO)**

Not all oil sands output is the same.

The industry mainly produces two types of products: Bitumen and Synthetic Crude Oil (SCO).

**Bitumen**In its natural state, bitumen is extremely heavy and sticky, more like cold molasses than flowing oil. Because it cannot move through pipelines on its own, producers blend it with a lighter oil, usually condensate(typically natural gas condensate such as naphtha, a lighter petroleum product with low viscosity), to thin it out. The blended product is called Diluted Bitumen, or Dilbit. This is what most in-situ producers without upgrading facilities sell. However, because Dilbit is lower in quality and more expensive for refineries to process, it usually sells at a steep discount compared to lighter crudes like WTI.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!KfYB!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F33ce29c7-54ce-4e04-adf4-ec298632cc30_1600x906.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!KfYB!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F33ce29c7-54ce-4e04-adf4-ec298632cc30_1600x906.png)

**Synthetic Crude Oil (SCO)**Producers with upgrading facilities can process raw bitumen into a lighter, cleaner oil called Synthetic Crude. Upgraders use heat and chemical reactions to break down the heavy molecules and often add hydrogen, producing a higher-quality oil that more refineries can handle. SCO typically sells for more, trading closer to benchmarks like WTI.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!qyAs!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd9a1bb2f-c3d0-40e3-9ee7-e6047fe814d7_1600x885.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!qyAs!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd9a1bb2f-c3d0-40e3-9ee7-e6047fe814d7_1600x885.png)

**Strategic Trade-Off** Producers in the oil sands face a fundamental choice. They can either invest billions to build and operate an upgrader, capturing the higher price for Synthetic Crude Oil (SCO), or they can sell Diluted Bitumen (Dilbit) at a discount but avoid the enormous capital and operating burden. The year 2020 marked a turning point. The COVID-19 collapse in global demand and the moment when WTI even went negative, exposed just how sensitive different oil sands products were to market stress. At that time, the mix of production was roughly 60% Dilbit and 40% SCO. But what mattered was how companies weathered the storm. SCO producers had a higher-quality product that found buyers more easily, while Dilbit producers benefited from avoiding the huge fixed costs of running upgraders.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!30rU!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F336d8524-bd3c-4136-ba38-6dd3a097dfc9_1600x887.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!30rU!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F336d8524-bd3c-4136-ba38-6dd3a097dfc9_1600x887.png)

Since then, Dilbit’s share has grown, while SCO has slipped below 40% of total output.

Three drivers explain this trend.

**First, the steady rise of in-situ projects has tilted the balance.** In-situ production almost always produces Dilbit, and these projects are cheaper, modular, and easier to expand than mining-and-upgrading complexes.

**Second, the cost of upgraders remains daunting.** They require multi-billion-dollar upfront investments and carry high operating costs, making new builds difficult to justify in a market focused on capital discipline.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!jrUQ!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb943380e-6803-4045-8298-439a96bfe292_1593x891.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!jrUQ!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb943380e-6803-4045-8298-439a96bfe292_1593x891.png)

**Third, refining demand has favoured heavy blends.** A significant portion of U.S. refineries, particularly in the Midwest and Gulf Coast, were designed to run heavy, sour grades like Dilbit. That structural demand has provided a steady market, reducing the pressure to upgrade bitumen into lighter SCO.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5ZLd!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb08bd4ca-4e5e-4716-ad95-fcd03d4f8435_1600x897.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5ZLd!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb08bd4ca-4e5e-4716-ad95-fcd03d4f8435_1600x897.png)

The trade-off is not one-dimensional.

Selling Dilbit locks producers into heavier quality discounts and exposure to transportation costs, but it avoids tying up capital and offers flexibility to ramp output through in-situ growth. Producing SCO secures a higher-quality product that earns closer to benchmark prices and appeals to more refineries globally, but it requires massive capital, steady operations, and carries greater downside risk in weak price environments.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!WLQz!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc7d4e241-02d9-460e-bf9a-8d05090aeb6f_1600x895.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!WLQz!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc7d4e241-02d9-460e-bf9a-8d05090aeb6f_1600x895.png)

This evolving balance between Dilbit and SCO has far-reaching implications. It determines how much cash producers generate per barrel, how volatile those cash flows are, and how exposed they are to shifts in oil price spreads.

Companies with upgraders enjoy a hedge against heavy crude discounts but take on cost and capital intensity; those that stick with Dilbit accept quality discounts but preserve balance sheet flexibility and scalability. Over time, this mix has become one of the defining features of corporate strategy in the oil sands, a lens through which investors can distinguish between operators pursuing premium stability versus those prioritizing leaner growth and lower capital intensity.

### **The Integrated Value Chain**

The Canadian oil sands industry stretches across the entire energy chain, from pulling bitumen out of the ground to delivering gasoline, diesel, and jet fuel to consumers. Understanding this flow is key to seeing how the main players make money.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mkAa!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F82484e95-9d33-4dfa-94c5-54bfcae0290a_1600x889.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mkAa!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F82484e95-9d33-4dfa-94c5-54bfcae0290a_1600x889.png)

#### Upstream

This is where the oil is produced. Bitumen is extracted either by mining deposits close to the surface or, for deeper reserves, by using in-situ projects. These are operations that recover bitumen without digging it up.

In-situ methods use wells drilled into the reservoir to heat the bitumen underground so it can flow to the surface. The most common approach is **Steam-Assisted Gravity Drainage (SAGD),** where two horizontal wells are drilled: steam is injected into the upper well to warm the bitumen, which then drains into the lower well and is pumped up.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5VWj!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1121bb55-b181-4af9-8a0b-f2fa0e587f4e_1600x890.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5VWj!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1121bb55-b181-4af9-8a0b-f2fa0e587f4e_1600x890.png)

Other variations include **Cyclic Steam Stimulation (CSS)** and solvent-assisted techniques. CSS uses a single well that alternates between injecting steam and producing bitumen. Steam is pumped into the reservoir under high pressure, the bitumen is left to soak, and then the well is switched to production mode. This cycle is repeated multiple times and works best in thicker reservoirs, though it tends to use more energy and produces in uneven bursts.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!87yE!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F51a6bae0-f8cd-4c94-96c8-d8691bd7cf01_1600x901.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!87yE!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F51a6bae0-f8cd-4c94-96c8-d8691bd7cf01_1600x901.png)

**Solvent-assisted methods,** by contrast, inject light hydrocarbons such as propane or butane, either alone or alongside steam, to thin the bitumen underground. This reduces the amount of steam needed, lowers fuel use and emissions, and can improve recovery efficiency. The challenge is that solvents are expensive, and large-scale adoption has been slower, though industry pilots are expanding as companies look for ways to cut emissions intensity.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!hYl2!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Feca12cac-15aa-4fbe-86ef-7f6fbcbf2ed2_1600x887.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!hYl2!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Feca12cac-15aa-4fbe-86ef-7f6fbcbf2ed2_1600x887.png)

In-situ projects carry several advantages. They disturb far less land than open-pit mining, can be scaled up in phases rather than through one massive upfront build, and usually require lower initial capital. They also give companies flexibility to expand or optimize production as technology improves. Most in-situ projects produce raw bitumen, which is then blended with condensate to make Dilbit for pipeline transport.

The largest upstream operators, who run both mining and in-situ projects, include Suncor, Canadian Natural Resources, Cenovus, and Imperial.

#### **Midstream**

Once the oil is extracted, it must move, often hundreds of kilometres, from northern Alberta to refineries and export points. This is the job of the midstream sector, which runs the pipelines, storage tanks, and sometimes rail or marine shipping needed to balance supply and demand.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!wOuw!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fff3dec73-bb2b-4f9a-8ac2-e3bed768f734_1815x1019.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!wOuw!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fff3dec73-bb2b-4f9a-8ac2-e3bed768f734_1815x1019.png)

Bitumen is typically blended with condensate into Dilbit so it can flow through pipelines. The midstream system not only carries this crude but also cycles diluent in and out of Alberta, manages quality streams, and balances supply with demand through large storage hubs such as Edmonton and Hardisty. These hubs also set benchmark prices like WCS.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!BScn!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ffb26d1ab-ed0b-4695-bacf-d7f263ed1976_1600x893.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!BScn!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ffb26d1ab-ed0b-4695-bacf-d7f263ed1976_1600x893.png)

Pipeline operators such as Enbridge, TC Energy, and Pembina run the key arteries connecting Canada’s inland production to U.S. and export markets. The system is tightly managed. Shippers nominate space, apportionment occurs when demand exceeds capacity, and tolls are charged for movement. When bottlenecks emerge, rail acts as the swing option, offering flexibility but at higher cost.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!w321!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3e78d25e-13e5-4946-acec-70b98a6eed53_1600x900.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!w321!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3e78d25e-13e5-4946-acec-70b98a6eed53_1600x900.png)

In short, midstream is more than just “pipes.” It blends, stores, measures, and arbitrages barrels so inland production can reach the right market. The adequacy of this system directly shapes price stability, producer cash flows, and government royalties.

**Downstream**

This is where oil is processed into the products people actually use. Bitumen can either be upgraded into a lighter, cleaner crude called Synthetic Crude Oil (SCO) or sent directly to refineries, where it is turned into gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, and other finished products.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ev9V!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F93af3e5d-2c5e-4f0d-bf1a-99e5dd7a68a7_1600x898.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ev9V!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F93af3e5d-2c5e-4f0d-bf1a-99e5dd7a68a7_1600x898.png)

Upgrader facilities are large processing plants purpose-built to transform heavy bitumen into SCO. They do this by applying heat and chemical reactions, such as coking and hydroprocessing, that break down the heavy molecules, remove excess carbon (often stored as petroleum coke), and sometimes add hydrogen to improve quality. The result is a higher-value crude that trades closer to benchmarks like WTI and can be processed by a wider range of refineries.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!c7Au!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fcb8c35a5-cceb-4e45-b2c1-a09cf0a37d8f_1600x899.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!c7Au!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fcb8c35a5-cceb-4e45-b2c1-a09cf0a37d8f_1600x899.png)

Upgraders provide clear advantages.

SCO earns a premium compared to Dilbit and gives producers access to more buyers. But as previously discussed, they also come with big challenges. They cost billions to build, are expensive to run, and become less attractive in weaker oil markets.

For that reason, very few new upgraders have been built in recent years, and many producers have focused instead on expanding in-situ projects and selling Dilbit.

The major integrated players such as Suncor, Imperial, and Cenovus, own both upgrading and refining assets, allowing them to capture value at multiple stages.

This vertical integration acts as a natural hedge.

When crude prices weaken, refining margins often improve, helping to smooth out cash flow across the business cycle.

### **Macroeconomic Bedrock**

The Canadian oil sands are not a peripheral industry.

They are a central pillar of the national economy.

Their size and the reach of their supply chain make them a major source of jobs, investment, and government revenue, which in turn helps secure strong political and public support for keeping the industry running responsibly.

The sector directly and indirectly supports about hundreds of thousands of jobs across Canada, many of them skilled and high-paying, with ripple effects in manufacturing, construction, and professional services.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!x01e!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F95758283-4d00-4780-a651-897646d2ad93_1600x895.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!x01e!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F95758283-4d00-4780-a651-897646d2ad93_1600x895.png)

It is also a major contributor to government budgets. In 2022–2023, oil sands royalties alone delivered **$16.9 billion** to Alberta’s provincial government, about two-thirds of all non-renewable resource revenue. On top of that, producers pay billions in federal and provincial corporate taxes each year. These revenues help fund healthcare, education, and other public services, while underscoring the industry’s role as a key driver of national prosperity.

This deep economic importance provides stability. Governments have a strong incentive to support the industry’s long-term viability, reducing political risk for investors

### **Dawn of Market Diversification**

For the first time, Canada’s oil sands industry has large-scale access to overseas markets. The TMX now ends at the upgraded Westridge Marine Terminal in Burnaby, British Columbia, which can load up to 34 large tankers a month. This connects landlocked Alberta directly to the Pacific Ocean and global buyers.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Fp_z!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F02072ff3-4fc0-408f-8219-fbcfb34cc96f_1600x901.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Fp_z!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F02072ff3-4fc0-408f-8219-fbcfb34cc96f_1600x901.png)

The outcome has been evident from the start. Before TMX, more than 97% of Canadian oil exports went to the United States. Since May 2024, when the line started operating, a significant share has begun moving west. China quickly became a leading customer, buying about **C$2.03 billion** worth of Canadian oil between May and November 2024. Shipping records from May 2024 to June 2025 show that ~**60% of tankers** from Westridge went to Asia, mainly China, while the other **42%** supplied refineries on the U.S. West Coast and in Alaska.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5sC_!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe04900e3-40b2-4e0f-9b39-907237df8201_1600x904.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5sC_!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe04900e3-40b2-4e0f-9b39-907237df8201_1600x904.png)

This marks a historic shift. Canadian producers are no longer almost entirely dependent on the U.S. Midwest and Gulf Coast. They are now tapping into faster-growing Asian markets while still serving U.S. buyers, giving them a more diverse and resilient customer base.

### **The Re-rating of Canadian Crude**

Selling into global markets is not just about spreading risk, it is a direct route to higher earnings.

With TMX now open to the Pacific, Canadian producers can sell their oil at prices set by global supply and demand, rather than being limited by North American pipeline bottlenecks.

Analysts estimate that oil shipped through TMX earns about **US$5–6 more per barrel** than the same oil sent overland to the U.S. Midwest or Gulf Coast. That extra value drops straight to the bottom line, lifting profit margins, generating more free cash flow, and increasing returns to shareholders.

The benefits also extend to the wider economy.

Independent studies project TMX will add **$9.2 billion to Canada’s GDP** and generate **$2.8 billion in tax revenues** between 2024 and 2043.

# **Competition and Industry Structure**

**Porter’s Five Forces Analysis**

Looking at the oil sands industry through Porter’s Five Forces shows an environment that strongly favours existing players. High barriers to entry and weakening buyer power create a competitive landscape where established producers are well positioned.

* **Competitive Rivalry:** The Canadian oil sands industry is an oligopoly, dominated by a handful of large, well-capitalized, and highly sophisticated integrated producers such as Suncor, Canadian Natural Resources (CNQ), Cenovus, and Imperial. Since oil is a global commodity, they cannot set prices or differentiate their product. Instead, competition is about running operations as efficiently and reliably as possible. This has fueled big pushes into automation, cost-cutting, and technology upgrades, such as autonomous haul trucks and advanced robotics, all aimed at being the lowest-cost producer.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!LKhE!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F928dd0f9-d95c-4c2e-a33f-4c3c2cb4f21e_1600x904.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!LKhE!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F928dd0f9-d95c-4c2e-a33f-4c3c2cb4f21e_1600x904.png)

* **Threat of New Entrants:** Starting a new oil sands project is nearly impossible for newcomers. Greenfield projects require staggering upfront capital investment. Mines like Suncor’s Fort Hills cost over $16 billion to build, and even then, projects face years of regulatory review and mandatory Indigenous consultations. Add in the massive scale, decades of operating know-how, and proprietary technologies held by incumbents, and the barriers become insurmountable for most new players.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Ykps!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F92489a4f-db0a-4155-b1a1-c302096be1c0_1600x894.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Ykps!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F92489a4f-db0a-4155-b1a1-c302096be1c0_1600x894.png)



* **Bargaining Power of Buyers:** For years, U.S. refiners held the upper hand because they were almost the only customers for Canadian heavy oil, and pipeline bottlenecks limited alternatives.
* **Bargaining Power of Suppliers:** The supply chain for specialized oilfield services and equipment, such as advanced drilling technologies, heavy haulers, and engineering services, is relatively concentrated among a few global giants like Schlumberger, Halliburton, and various heavy equipment manufacturers. These specialized suppliers possess a degree of pricing power due to their technical expertise and market position. But because oil sands operators are among the biggest customers globally, they can often negotiate favourable long-term contracts, keeping the balance of power relatively even.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!P_e4!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb3ee42f1-6d17-4646-9d81-6f5680a7ee13_1600x891.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!P_e4!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb3ee42f1-6d17-4646-9d81-6f5680a7ee13_1600x891.png)



* **Threat of Substitutes:** The biggest long-term risk is the global shift to cleaner energy. Electric vehicles, biofuels, and renewables will eventually cut oil demand. Still, this will not happen overnight. Most forecasts including from the International Energy Agency expect oil to remain central to the global energy mix through at least 2040. That means oil sands will continue to have a strong market for decades, even as the transition unfolds.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!U18A!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F490ac3c9-4fdf-4aa0-973b-ee63c833be51_1600x899.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!U18A!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F490ac3c9-4fdf-4aa0-973b-ee63c833be51_1600x899.png)

### **Risk Analysis & Mitigating Factors**

A comprehensive investment case requires a rigorous and objective assessment of the risks facing the sector. The outlook for the Canadian oil sands is strong, but investors must weigh challenges tied to the environment, government policy, and swings in oil prices. The good news is that the industry has been tackling these issues directly with large-scale strategies that make it stronger and more resilient.

**ESG & Environmental Headwinds**

The oil sands’ biggest non-financial risk is environmental pressure. Investors, regulators, and the public are relentless in their focus on the industry’s footprint. Key areas of concern include greenhouse gas emissions, water use, tailings ponds, and land disturbance.

The industry is not standing still and responding with collaboration and technology:

* **Pathways Alliance to Net Zero:** Six producers, covering more than 95% of output, have joined forces to achieve net-zero emissions by 2050. This is not mere lip service but a structured, industry-led plan that lays out a credible path to decarbonization.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!_d87!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F71f1a597-4398-4b00-aa23-2c8d9b0ca6e8_1800x911.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!_d87!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F71f1a597-4398-4b00-aa23-2c8d9b0ca6e8_1800x911.png)

* **Technology Gains:** Emissions per barrel are already down 20–30% since the 1990s, largely due to the adoption of more efficient processes like co-generation, which turns waste heat into both steam and electricity. The record shows steady, measurable progress.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!HxDV!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F02dfb77a-cb96-4b5a-ae4c-77be07d933bd_1800x1008.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!HxDV!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F02dfb77a-cb96-4b5a-ae4c-77be07d933bd_1800x1008.png)



* **Carbon Capture, Utilization and Storage (CCS):** The centrepiece of the Pathways plan is a massive CCS hub in Alberta that will capture carbon dioxide from multiple facilities and sequester it deep underground. It is one of the largest and most concrete decarbonization projects anywhere in the industrial world.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!8Jza!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F690a3606-b61b-4a31-8a5c-1aa2a2ceb620_1813x1021.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!8Jza!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F690a3606-b61b-4a31-8a5c-1aa2a2ceb620_1813x1021.png)

* **Water and Land Stewardship:** The industry operates under a strict regulatory framework. Provincial regulations limit total fresh water withdrawals from the Athabasca River, and operators are highly efficient, recycling between 80% and 95% of the water used in their processes. And by law, every acre disturbed by mining must be restored to a self-sustaining ecosystem.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!QeNe!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F129e34b1-9673-4626-9ed6-a6c84b27ea89_1809x1019.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!QeNe!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F129e34b1-9673-4626-9ed6-a6c84b27ea89_1809x1019.png)

### **Regulatory and Political Risk**

Climate policy is a central risk for the oil sands. Canada’s federal government has put in place a rising carbon tax, set to reach $170 per tonne by 2030, and is developing a formal cap on oil and gas emissions. Both measures are intended to curb greenhouse gases but also raise operating costs for producers.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!SSMw!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa936bd7c-55b3-4dbc-9236-6aba0cea3b73_1806x1008.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!SSMw!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa936bd7c-55b3-4dbc-9236-6aba0cea3b73_1806x1008.png)

The sector’s main defence is active investment in decarbonization. Through the Pathways Alliance and related projects, companies are advancing large-scale carbon capture and other technologies to cut absolute emissions. These steps not only support environmental goals but also reduce direct exposure to higher carbon costs and future caps.

The industry’s scale also matters. As a major source of jobs, royalties, and tax revenue, the oil sands hold a significant place in policy discussions. This economic weight helps ensure climate measures are shaped in ways that balance environmental objectives with the sector’s long-term competitiveness.

### **Commodity Price Volatility**

Because oil sands produce a global commodity, the industry is always exposed to swings in world oil prices. Sharp price drops, like those in 2014–15 and 2020 market collapses, cut deeply into profits and forced companies to delay projects, cancel expansions, and shed jobs.

Today the sector is more resilient. **The 2014 price collapse forced a radical and painful restructuring of the sector's entire cost base.** Companies shed non-core assets, cutting overhead, and adopting new technologies to run more efficiently. The result is an industry that is leaner, more disciplined, and able to operate at much lower costs.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!cA0a!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2f0b4a1c-116a-4cf6-9f96-788fb1806acb_1802x1005.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!cA0a!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2f0b4a1c-116a-4cf6-9f96-788fb1806acb_1802x1005.png)

The largest five producers can now cover expenses, maintain production, and return cash to shareholders at oil prices as low as US$40–43 per barrel. This means the industry can stay profitable under a wider range of price scenarios, giving it a stronger buffer against the volatility that once defined it.

## **View**

Combined with the leaner, lower-cost business model built out of the last downturn, the industry is now positioned to generate strong and durable free cash flow.

While risks remain with the global shift toward cleaner energy and the direction of Canadian climate policy, the industry is addressing them head-on through collective efforts like the Pathways Alliance and its large-scale carbon capture projects.

We believe that the market has yet to fully price in the scale of this change and the upside from more predictable cash flows, better pricing from access to new markets, and a disciplined commitment to shareholder returns, remains compelling

### **Key Players: A Game of Incentives**

The Canadian oil sands are the domain of giants, situated in the vast Western Canadian Sedimentary Basin (WCSB), one of the world's most hydrocarbon-rich formations.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!etHh!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9d97a628-2e22-45b6-8c5b-d8a868d11b97_1600x902.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!etHh!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9d97a628-2e22-45b6-8c5b-d8a868d11b97_1600x902.png)

This basin is the reason Canada is the world's fourth-largest oil producer, pumping out 5.6 million barrels per day (MMB/d) in 2022.

The oil sands make up the lion's share of this, about 58% of the total. With 167 billion barrels of recoverable oil, the reserves are mind-bogglingly vast, trailing only Venezuela, Saudi Arabia, and Iran.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!zXn7!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F54bc2495-d92a-44f1-aad9-2dfdcdb2347c_1600x903.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!zXn7!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F54bc2495-d92a-44f1-aad9-2dfdcdb2347c_1600x903.png)

The game here is defined by two sets of rules, or extraction methods, each with its own incentives:

* **Mining:** For the 20% of reserves near the surface, the incentive is to dig. Huge shovels and trucks move earth on a scale that’s hard to comprehend. The bitumen is then separated with hot water. The process is capital-intensive, disturbs a lot of land, and creates massive tailings ponds. The incentive is straightforward: economies of scale.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!2VhF!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6ab65c35-ab27-470d-9814-03cd2cb56216_1600x897.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!2VhF!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6ab65c35-ab27-470d-9814-03cd2cb56216_1600x897.png)



* **In-Situ:** For the other 80% of reserves, you can't dig, so the incentive is to heat. Steam is pumped deep underground to melt the bitumen so it can be pumped up. This method has a smaller footprint but is incredibly energy-intensive, usually burning natural gas to make the steam. The incentive here is technological efficiency.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!BZOk!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F104cd8c0-eb61-45ad-aea1-b79c27c2fa69_1600x895.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!BZOk!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F104cd8c0-eb61-45ad-aea1-b79c27c2fa69_1600x895.png)



These two methods have created an industry dominated by a few major players, each responding to a slightly different set of incentives.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!evA2!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fcc3e39ce-58b0-48de-8cd4-38e87dda6f6b_1600x991.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!evA2!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fcc3e39ce-58b0-48de-8cd4-38e87dda6f6b_1600x991.png)

#### **The Integrated Majors: Hedged and Happy**

The big dogs of the oil sands are integrated, meaning they do everything from extraction to refining to selling gasoline at the pump.

This structure is a powerful incentive in itself.

When crude oil prices are low, they make money on refining. When crude prices are high, they make money on production. It’s a beautiful hedge against the market's wild swings.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!meWm!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc6b52ea5-c416-4b29-89f3-60d44321df5e_1600x894.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!meWm!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc6b52ea5-c416-4b29-89f3-60d44321df5e_1600x894.png)

**Suncor Energy (TSX: SU): First Mover**

Suncor was the first to commercially develop the oil sands back in 1967, so their entire corporate DNA is wrapped up in this resource.

With a market capitalization of $62 billion as of Q2 2025, Suncor is a cornerstone of the sector, operating a diverse portfolio that includes mining (Oil Sands Base Plant, Fort Hills), in-situ production (Firebag, MacKay River), and extensive upgrading facilities.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!oRA5!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc8a2635f-78b9-4c34-b8bb-f37b051c8b15_1600x898.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!oRA5!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc8a2635f-78b9-4c34-b8bb-f37b051c8b15_1600x898.png)

The company is also the largest shareholder in the Syncrude mining joint venture. Their incentive is to sweat these integrated assets for maximum value.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mqG4!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc88f274b-48ea-489b-ade6-6e894007ef48_1600x902.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mqG4!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc88f274b-48ea-489b-ade6-6e894007ef48_1600x902.png)

Their downstream segment, which includes 466,000 barrels per day (kbpd) of refining capacity and the Petro-Canada retail network, provides a crucial buffer against weak crude prices. With a refinery utilization rate of 99% in the first half of 2025, they are masters of this game.

Suncor’s financial strategy is built on disciplined capital allocation, having achieved its net debt target of 8 billion in the third quarter of 2024, which triggered a policy of returning at or near 1007/bbl WTI breakeven reduction in 2024 and record annual production of 234 kbpd at its Firebag in-situ facility that same year.

**Canadian Natural Resources Limited (TSX: CNRL): Cash Generator**

CNRL is the biggest crude oil producer in Canada, a true behemoth.

Their incentive structure is built on a simple, powerful idea: own a vast and uniquely diverse portfolio of long-life, low-decline assets. This includes world-class oil sands mining and upgrading operations (Horizon, Athabasca Oil Sands Project - AOSP), extensive thermal in-situ assets, and a significant conventional oil and natural gas business.

With the second-largest total proved reserves among its global peers (over 5 billion BOE) the company has unparalleled resource depth.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!VtAi!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3665e79d-e52b-471f-9694-41942240b831_1600x899.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!VtAi!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3665e79d-e52b-471f-9694-41942240b831_1600x899.png)

**Imperial Oil (TSX: IMO): Tech Innovator**

As a Canadian affiliate of ExxonMobil, Imperial’s incentive is driven by technology.

They run the massive Kearl mine, the long-running Cold Lake in-situ operation, and hold a significant partnership stake in the Syncrude mine. Their game is to use technology to relentlessly drive down costs, targeting unit cash costs below US 20/bbl at Kearl and US 13/bbl at Cold Lake.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!4fyw!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F623a33f1-0938-470e-a221-64832926bcf5_1600x895.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!4fyw!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F623a33f1-0938-470e-a221-64832926bcf5_1600x895.png)

They are pouring money into next-gen solvent technologies, with their Grand Rapids solvent-assisted SAGD (SA-SAGD) project already exceeding expectations. Construction is also underway on a field pilot for their proprietary Enhanced Bitumen Recovery Technology (EBRT) at their Aspen lease, which is on track for a 2027 start-up.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!VhOd!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdfd1e96d-56e5-463e-a249-b241e78dd02d_1600x890.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!VhOd!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdfd1e96d-56e5-463e-a249-b241e78dd02d_1600x890.png)

EBRT aims to cut steam use by up to 90% and reduce GHG emissions intensity by approximately 60%, directly addressing the industry's core cost and emissions problems. This commitment to both current performance and future innovation has fueled strong shareholder returns, including a 227% dividend increase since 2020.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!E6h-!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F91a2c390-b9d4-487e-af98-f4c264525cf4_1600x898.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!E6h-!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F91a2c390-b9d4-487e-af98-f4c264525cf4_1600x898.png)

**Cenovus Energy (TSX: CVE): The Specialist**

Cenovus made its name as a pioneer of commercial SAGD, the dominant in-situ technology, successfully developing its Foster Creek and Christina Lake projects into industry benchmarks.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!nxoU!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F722847a7-c87e-411b-a46d-96f73e9c2125_1600x865.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!nxoU!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F722847a7-c87e-411b-a46d-96f73e9c2125_1600x865.png)

After merging with Husky Energy in 2021, they became a fully integrated player, giving them the same hedge as Suncor by adding significant downstream refining and upgrading capacity.

Their incentive is disciplined growth and shareholder returns, with a clear target of getting net debt down to approximately $4 billion before accelerating the return of excess free funds flow to shareholders; a target they achieved shortly after Q2 2025.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!xGfg!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0162e907-b358-4b8e-8e89-8da2ae4ea5ea_1908x1077.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!xGfg!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0162e907-b358-4b8e-8e89-8da2ae4ea5ea_1908x1077.png)

Their legacy oil sands assets remain highly competitive, with sustaining capital requirements of $7-9 per barrel and operating costs of $10−12 per barrel. The company continues to advance capital − efficient growth, achieving first oil at its Narrows Lake tie − back project in July 2025 and progressing the West White Rose offshore project. Their August 2025 agreement to acquire MEG Energy in a C7.9 billion deal signals a powerful new incentive in the sector: consolidation to create an "in-situ champion" capable of realizing significant synergies.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!p6CE!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F52e633d6-c8be-4dae-8d09-03f726de8c67_1791x999.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!p6CE!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F52e633d6-c8be-4dae-8d09-03f726de8c67_1791x999.png)

This brings us to a key strategic split in the industry, best understood as **"Harvest vs. Innovate."** It’s not a contradiction; it’s two sides of the same coin.

The integrated majors are incentivized to "harvest" maximum cash from their existing, low-decline mining assets. CNRL makes this explicit, noting their mining assets require far less capital to maintain production than shale wells. This is the cash cow.

At the same time, they are incentivized to "innovate" to secure the future of their in-situ assets, which make up 80% of the total resource. Imperial and Cenovus are pushing hard on solvent technologies that promise to slash costs and emissions.

The incentive structure is brilliant: the low-risk, cash-harvesting assets are funding the high-risk, innovative bets on the industry's future.

#### **Pure-Play Innovators**

While the majors dominate, smaller "pure-play" companies have been crucial. Their incentive is simple: be the absolute best at one thing: in-situ production.

**MEG Energy (TSX: MEG): The Benchmark**

MEG built a reputation as a top-tier in-situ producer with its Christina Lake asset.

Their incentive was to be so efficient that they became the benchmark.

With a steam-oil ratio 40% lower than some peers, they achieved a top-quartile cost structure. They were also smart, securing pipeline contracts to high-value U.S. markets to insulate themselves from price volatility.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!nCX5!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ffc704d0c-6784-488e-806c-4b733d5466c1_1912x1071.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!nCX5!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ffc704d0c-6784-488e-806c-4b733d5466c1_1912x1071.png)

But here’s the thing about being the best: it makes you a target.

MEG’s singular focus and high-quality assets made it the perfect acquisition. After rejecting a hostile bid from Strathcona Resources, they agreed to be acquired by Cenovus in August 2025.

This is the new incentive in the oil sands: scale. The capital required to fund next-generation technologies like MEG's proprietary eMVAPEX solvent process is immense. A larger company like Cenovus is better incentivized to fund that development at a lower cost of capital.

The era of the mid-sized pure-play may be over; the incentive is to consolidate or be consumed.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!6iYA!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ffde8a316-d76f-4925-8f36-d1893abeba22_1600x903.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!6iYA!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ffde8a316-d76f-4925-8f36-d1893abeba22_1600x903.png)

**The Egress Incentive: A Pipeline to Profits**

For over a decade, the biggest negative incentive in the oil sands was the "egress problem."

Producers were getting the oil out of the ground, but they couldn't get it to market.

Production growth consistently outpaced pipeline capacity, leaving the Western Canadian Sedimentary Basin effectively landlocked. This created a supply bottleneck that resulted in the infamous "bitumen bubble"; not a speculative bubble of overvaluation, but a market dislocation caused by oversupply and a lack of export options.

This dislocation manifested as a deep and volatile price discount for Western Canadian Select (WCS) crude relative to the North American benchmark, West Texas Intermediate (WTI). The WCS-WTI differential, which averaged around $15-20 per barrel, blew out to a record of over $50 in late 2018.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!wVY7!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8c554e78-89b7-4025-9fc7-06278d896b80_1600x904.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!wVY7!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8c554e78-89b7-4025-9fc7-06278d896b80_1600x904.png)

The economic carnage was immense; the discount cost the Canadian economy an estimated $15.6 billion in 2018 alone, with daily losses reaching as high as $100 million.

The crisis became so severe that in late 2018, the Alberta government took the drastic step of mandating an 8.7% cut in oil production to support prices.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!PZHL!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0eeb605b-07ce-4338-9e7f-1edb479141ef_2025x1136.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!PZHL!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0eeb605b-07ce-4338-9e7f-1edb479141ef_2025x1136.png)

This struggle for market access ignited a series of high-stakes political and environmental battles over proposed export pipelines. Ambitious projects designed to connect the oil sands to new markets, such as TransCanada's Keystone XL to the U.S. Gulf Coast and the Energy East pipeline to Atlantic Canada, became focal points of intense opposition and were ultimately cancelled after years of regulatory delays and political maneuvering.

Then came the **Trans Mountain Expansion (TMX)**. Its completion in May 2024 was a revolutionary event. By nearly tripling capacity to the Pacific Coast, it fundamentally changed the incentive structure for the entire industry.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Z8z3!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6389c469-6978-4ad4-8988-7f1f54c6d982_1600x900.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Z8z3!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6389c469-6978-4ad4-8988-7f1f54c6d982_1600x900.png)

* **The Price Incentive:** The WCS price discount immediately narrowed and stabilized. In July 2025, it was around $10 per barrel, a world away from the punishing discounts of the past. A better price is a powerful incentive.
* **The Market Incentive:** TMX broke the industry's near-total dependence on the U.S. market. In its first year, 48.1% of marine exports went to non-U.S. destinations, with China becoming the biggest new customer. Access to global, Brent-priced markets created a powerful new incentive for producers.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!dj0u!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7d506d9d-dd18-4e86-9039-985e62d7cbb7_1600x902.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!dj0u!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7d506d9d-dd18-4e86-9039-985e62d7cbb7_1600x902.png)

The completion of TMX de-risked the entire sector.

The central challenge is no longer "Can we sell our oil?" but "How profitably can we sell it on a global stage?" This explains the intense focus on cost. The incentive has shifted from *market access* to *margin optimization*.

#### **The "Buy vs. Build" Incentive**

Understanding oil sands economics requires knowing the difference between building a new project and running an old one.

* **Greenfield (New Builds):** Building a new oil sands project is astronomically expensive. In 2016, it was estimated that a new SAGD project needed a WTI price of $70-80 per barrel to be economic, and a new mine needed $85-100. This creates a massive negative incentive to build from scratch.
* **Brownfield (Existing Assets):** Once built, however, oil sands projects are cash machines. A 2025 analysis found the average breakeven price for existing projects was just US$27 per barrel. This is because they have very low decline rates, unlike shale wells that need constant drilling.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!4iL0!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe7423a65-f4f3-4d6f-95a3-611c5a737555_1600x902.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!4iL0!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe7423a65-f4f3-4d6f-95a3-611c5a737555_1600x902.png)

This two-tiered reality creates a powerful incentive for consolidation. It is far more rational for Cenovus to buy MEG Energy's existing, low-cost production than to risk billions building a new project with a much higher breakeven price. The incentive is to buy, not build.

#### **The Fort McMurray Incentive Cycle**

The industry's operational hub, Fort McMurray, is a living case study of incentives. The city's entire existence is a "boom and bust" cycle driven by the price of oil.

* **The Boom Incentive:** When oil prices are high, the incentive is to build. The city's population exploded by 80% between 2000 and 2010 as workers chased high-paying jobs. The average price of a single-family home soared past $700,000.
* **The Bust Incentive:** When prices crash, the incentive is to cut back. The population fell by nearly 11% between 2015 and 2018. The housing market corrected sharply. In August 2025, the average home price was around $419,000.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!6Kru!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7f8c9d24-0e67-403a-8228-657c0c5819a5_1600x899.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!6Kru!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7f8c9d24-0e67-403a-8228-657c0c5819a5_1600x899.png)

Recent data suggests the city is maturing. The population stabilized with a 1.3% increase between 2016 and 2021. The unemployment rate in the region was 7.1% in July 2025. The wild boom-and-bust incentives are being replaced by the more stable incentives of a mature, operational industry focused on efficiency, not just growth.

### **Global Forces: New Incentives**

The oil sands have solved the egress problem, but now a new set of global forces is creating a gauntlet of powerful, and often conflicting, incentives.

#### **The Climate Stick: Stranded Asset Risk**

The biggest long-term negative incentive is the global energy transition.

As the world moves to lower-carbon energy, oil demand is expected to peak this decade.

For a high-cost producer like Canada, this creates the risk of "stranded assets", massive projects that become worthless because the market for their product disappears.

A June 2025 report quantified this risk under different IEA climate scenarios:

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!77KC!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7a0e4ed1-80e6-43c6-b192-f5d252c08698_1600x904.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!77KC!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7a0e4ed1-80e6-43c6-b192-f5d252c08698_1600x904.png)

If the world gets serious about climate change (the APS and NZE scenarios), a huge portion of future investment gets wiped out, and the entire sector could become a net financial liability.

This is because Canada's undeveloped oil fields have the second-highest median breakeven price in the world, making them uncompetitive in a shrinking market.

This creates a powerful incentive to stop thinking about long-term growth and start maximizing short-to-medium-term cash flow from existing, low-cost assets.

It’s a rational incentive to harvest what you can before the party ends.

#### **The Social License**

Domestically, the industry faces a mix of incentives around its "social license to operate".

* **The Regulatory Stick:** Canada has a shifting view of climate policy, from Alberta's carbon price on industrial emitters (the first in North America) to a proposed federal emissions cap. This regulatory uncertainty acts as a negative incentive, making long-term planning difficult.
* **The Reconciliation:** The relationship with Indigenous communities has shifted from a model of consultation to one of economic partnership. This is a powerful positive incentive. Landmark deals, like the Fort McKay and Mikisew Cree First Nations buying a 49% stake in a Suncor tank farm, are becoming the norm. These partnerships provide communities with revenue and de-risk projects for companies by building powerful alliances. In 2019 alone, the oil sands spent $2.4 billion with Indigenous-owned businesses.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Z8sF!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F77149a95-f013-492b-816a-a92d27c35491_1600x903.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Z8sF!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F77149a95-f013-492b-816a-a92d27c35491_1600x903.png)

#### **Tariff Incentive**

A new, powerful incentive emerged in early 2025: a 10% U.S. tariff on Canadian energy imports. This fundamentally alters the decades-long integration of the North American energy market.

The tariff is a direct financial penalty, making Canadian barrels less competitive in their largest market.

But its timing, coming just as TMX opened, created a powerful "push-pull" dynamic.

The tariff pushes producers away from the U.S., while TMX *pulls* them toward new global markets in Asia.

This gives Canadian producers strategic options they never had before. As the CEO of Cenovus stated, it will prompt a "rebalancing away from the United States".

This U.S. policy, intended to be punitive, may have inadvertently provided the final incentive for Canadian energy to achieve true global market diversification.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mdDF!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F365653b7-a372-4c0d-96b4-a5847d9fb9c9_1600x904.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mdDF!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F365653b7-a372-4c0d-96b4-a5847d9fb9c9_1600x904.png)

In the short run, such a move would hurt Canadian producers.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!gIwD!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F90a67e81-6dc4-4714-8cce-40f3c94656f2_1600x894.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!gIwD!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F90a67e81-6dc4-4714-8cce-40f3c94656f2_1600x894.png)

Because the two countries’ markets are so closely tied, Canadian companies would likely have to absorb most of the cost by accepting lower prices for their oil to remain competitive.

That would widen the price gap between WCS and WTI, cutting into revenues and profits.

But the pain would not stop at Canada’s border.

In practice, the burden would be shared. A U.S. tariff would raise the total cost of moving Canadian heavy crude into American refineries, many of whom rely heavily on Canadian heavy crude. Canadian producers would take lower netbacks while U.S. refiners who cannot quickly switch away from heavy feedstock would face higher effective costs, some of which would flow through to fuel prices and slimmer refining margins.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!M5QJ!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc1bc0b5e-2bf8-40fd-ac3b-1f101fa490bc_1600x902.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!M5QJ!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc1bc0b5e-2bf8-40fd-ac3b-1f101fa490bc_1600x902.png)

This leads to higher gasoline and diesel prices for American consumers, especially in the Midwest, and squeezing profits for U.S. refiners.

Ironically, the risk of tariffs strengthens the long-term case for Canada’s oil sands.

The industry’s biggest weakness has always been its dependence on the U.S., with more than 97% of exports going south. That makes it highly vulnerable to U.S. policy changes and protectionist measures. The threat of tariffs brings this vulnerability into sharp focus and gives producers a strong reason to diversify.

So while tariffs would be a short-term blow, they also act as a catalyst, pushing the industry toward a more diversified, resilient, and ultimately more valuable business model. In this way, the tariff debate reinforces the logic of TMX and strengthens the long-term investment case for the oil sands.

#### **A New Supplier for a New World**

Asia’s demand is rising quickly, especially in countries like China, India, South Korea, and Japan, all of whom should be actively seeking to diversify their energy imports away from traditional suppliers in the Middle East to enhance their own energy security.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!qlcK!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe2b67424-4213-4f98-b2ff-5ca2c173668a_1600x900.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!qlcK!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe2b67424-4213-4f98-b2ff-5ca2c173668a_1600x900.png)

Canada is well placed to serve this need.

From its West Coast, oil can reach Asia in about ~22 days by ship, compared to 3 times as many days from the U.S. Gulf Coast, making Canadian exports faster and cheaper to deliver.

Europe is also part of the longer-term picture. Since Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, European countries have been racing to reduce their dependence on Russian energy. While Canada does not yet have large-scale infrastructure to send crude directly to Europe, its reputation as a stable, democratic supplier makes it a natural candidate.

Discussions are already underway about expanding future oil and LNG export capacity to help fill this gap.

### **Future Outlook: The Innovation Incentive**

Faced with this new world of competing incentives, the industry's survival now depends on one thing: technology. The race is on to re-engineer the entire process to be cheaper and cleaner.

#### **Horizon 1 (Now - 2030): The Solvent Solution**

The most immediate innovation is the shift to solvent-assisted extraction. By mixing light hydrocarbons with steam, producers can slash the amount of natural gas they need to burn, which cuts both costs and GHG emissions.

* **Imperial's EBRT:** Aims to replace up to 90% of steam, potentially cutting GHG intensity by 60%. A pilot project is under construction.
* **Cenovus's SAP:** Aims to reduce emissions intensity by about one-third.
* **MEG's eMVAPEX:** Aims to get steam injection to near-zero levels.

These technologies are the industry's near-term response to the new cost and climate incentives.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!8jvM!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1cecff64-a890-4919-bf86-9c5c8bd9fe4f_1600x906.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!8jvM!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1cecff64-a890-4919-bf86-9c5c8bd9fe4f_1600x906.png)

#### **Horizon 2 (2030 - 2040): The Alliance Bet**

The next horizon is a massive, industry-wide bet: the **Pathways Alliance's** carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) network.

This is a consortium of the six largest producers, representing 95% of production, with a shared goal of net-zero emissions by 2050.

The plan is to build a $20 billion pipeline to capture CO2 from over 20 facilities and store it permanently underground. This is a grand response to the climate incentive, but it's so expensive that it creates a new incentive: the need for massive government co-investment to make it happen.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!bjNE!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6cf64ac6-c91e-450f-972a-a39e1c3cd3dd_1600x902.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!bjNE!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6cf64ac6-c91e-450f-972a-a39e1c3cd3dd_1600x902.png)

#### **Horizon 3 (2035+): The Nuclear Option**

The most transformative, long-term incentive is to completely change the energy source.

**Small modular reactors (SMRs)** could provide non-emitting, high-temperature steam for in-situ operations, virtually eliminating the use of natural gas.

Cenovus is already studying the feasibility of deploying SMRs in the oil sands. This is the ultimate "innovate" strategy, a long-shot bet on a technology that could solve the emissions problem once and for all.

The future of the oil sands is overwhelmingly tied to in-situ production. Mining is a mature operation. The industry's ability to survive into the 2040s and 2050s depends entirely on winning the innovation race for the 80% of the resource that is still buried deep underground.

### **Small Cap Watch List: The Overlooked Players Incentivized for the Future**

While the key players get the headlines, a different set of players is quietly being incentivized to solve the oil sands' biggest problems.

These aren't necessarily the companies pulling the most barrels out of the ground.

Instead, they're the innovators, the toll collectors, and the value-adders who stand to win big by making the entire system cheaper, cleaner, and more efficient.

Here are the companies on our watch list, the ones whose incentives are aligned with the future, not just the present.

#### **The Technology Arbitrageurs**

The biggest prize in the oil sands isn't finding more bitumen; it's finding a cheaper way to get it out and clean it up. A handful of smaller, focused companies are incentivized to do just that.

* **Enlighten Innovations:** Their game is partial upgrading. Enlighten's DSU® technology is designed to take raw bitumen and turn it into low-sulfur marine fuel, a higher-value product. Their incentive is to solve two problems at once: reduce the need for costly diluent and clean up the product, which could cut lifecycle GHG emissions by up to 40%.
* **NanosTech:** This company is trying to move the entire upgrading process underground. Their In-Situ Upgrading Technology (ISUT) uses a nano-catalyst injected directly into the reservoir to upgrade the bitumen before it even comes to the surface. The incentive is massive: potentially cut steam use by 43%, emissions by 35%, and boost the value of every barrel by $5-8. It's a high-risk, high-reward bet on pure science.

#### **Midstream & Infrastructure**

You can bet on a single producer, or you can bet on the company that owns the road everyone has to drive on.

Midstream companies are the ultimate toll collectors, insulated from commodity price swings and incentivized by sheer volume.

* **Gibson Energy (TSX: GEI):** Gibson is a pure-play liquids infrastructure company. They own critical terminals, like the one at Hardisty, and over 500 km of pipelines. Their incentive isn't tied to the price of a barrel, but to the number of barrels that need to be stored, blended, and moved. As long as the oil sands produce, Gibson collects a fee, making them a lower-risk way to play the sector's future.
* **Wolf Midstream:** A private company with a public-sized impact. Wolf operates the Access Pipeline system, a vital artery for moving diluent to the oil sands and bitumen blend back out. But their real future-facing asset is the Alberta Carbon Trunk Line (ACTL), a major piece of CCUS infrastructure. As the industry is forced to decarbonize, Wolf is positioned to be the primary toll collector for captured carbon, a business that is powerfully incentivized by climate policy.

#### **The Value Adders: Petrochemicals**

Why ship raw materials when you can sell finished goods? The biggest long-term incentive for Alberta is to stop exporting bitumen and start exporting high-value products.

* **NOVA Chemicals:** As one of Canada's largest petrochemical producers, NOVA takes feedstocks like ethane and turns them into polyethylene, the building block for countless plastic products. Their incentive is to capture the massive value uplift that comes from chemical processing.
* **Inter Pipeline (a Brookfield company):** Their Heartland Petrochemical Complex is a multi-billion-dollar bet on this exact strategy. It's designed to take locally produced propane and upgrade it into polypropylene, a high-value plastic. This is a direct incentive to diversify Alberta's economy and capture more of the value chain at home, creating a more resilient and profitable downstream industry.

### **Outlook: A Race of Incentives**

The Canadian oil sands industry is at a crossroads, pushed and pulled by a powerful set of competing incentives.

* **Positive Incentives (Tailwinds):** A world-class resource in a stable country. New global market access thanks to TMX. Extremely low operating costs for existing assets. A credible pathway of game-changing technology.
* **Negative Incentives (Headwinds):** The existential threat of the global energy transition and stranded assets. An uncertain domestic climate policy. New geopolitical friction with the U.S..

The confluence of these forces has changed the game.

The oil sands are no longer a growth story. It is a mature industry where the incentive is to optimize, control costs, and return cash to shareholders.

The outlook will be decided by a race: can the incentive to innovate and cut costs win out against the incentive for the world to transition away from oil?

If the industry can execute its three-horizon decarbonization plan, it could secure a future as a supplier of responsibly produced, lower-carbon oil. If it fails, or if the global transition outpaces it, the future looks bleak. The outcome is not random; it will be determined by how the titans of the tundra respond to the powerful incentives that now govern their world.
