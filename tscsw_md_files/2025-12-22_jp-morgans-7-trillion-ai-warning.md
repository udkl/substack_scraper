“The four most expensive words in the English language are: **‘This time it’s different.’**“ — Sir John Templeton

Every bubble begins with a spreadsheet.

Not with a burst of irrational enthusiasm, but with a sober-faced projection in a quiet boardroom. For the AI boom, that projection is the new 58-page J.P. Morgan report outlining future cash flows that defy both economic history and the laws of physics.

We’re digging deep into that report today. And I’d like you to know that I’m taking no shortcuts here. I’m reading every word; my copies are already populated with highlights and scribbles.

The current Artificial Intelligence infrastructure spending spree is perhaps the most refined example yet of this phenomenon.

It is a generational industrial build-out celebrating the weightless elegance of software while depending entirely on the slow, capital-intensive brutality of copper, steel, concrete, and credit.

The market has priced AI as an exponential, anti-fragile technology that operates at the speed of light. However, the investment required to power this revolution is analog, slow, and structurally fragile.

This post will look at the four central cracks in this thesis:

* **Hyper-Capital Intensity:** A $5-7 trillion spend concentrated in just five companies, guaranteeing eventual oversupply.
* **A Debt-Fueled Cycle:** This isn’t a cash-funded revolution; it’s a $1.5 trillion bet from the investment-grade bond market in disguise.
* **The Analog Blockade:** A 7-year wait for power grid connections for infrastructure that takes 2 years to build, creating “dark compute.”
* **The Perpetual Revenue Fantasy:** A $650 billion annual revenue hurdle required *in perpetuity* just to earn a 10% return.

## **Trillion-Dollar Delusions**

The scale of the commitment alone provides the first warning signal, suggesting that we have confused intelligent algorithms with intelligent capital deployment. Global financial analysts estimate that the comprehensive AI infrastructure and data center build-out required over the next five years will consume between $5 trillion and $7 trillion in capital. This is not merely an investment cycle; it is an industrial project of a scope comparable to creating the foundational infrastructure of a small industrialized nation.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!T7Ty!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd538d044-9f5a-47b5-a341-3a4b7cea643d_1600x897.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!T7Ty!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd538d044-9f5a-47b5-a341-3a4b7cea643d_1600x897.png)

This colossal expenditure is overwhelmingly concentrated among the five major hyperscalers (Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Oracle). Their combined capital expenditure (CapEx) has soared from approximately $200 billion in 2022 and is projected to exceed $420 billion by 2026.

This accelerated deployment velocity is a classic, supply-side indicator of a late-cycle boom, virtually guaranteeing eventual oversupply and depressed returns. The capital cycle suggests that such a massive wave of rapid spending tends to destroy aggregate sector profitability, transforming the initial advantage into a collective burden.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!G4lt!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd89e13ca-c7a9-4cf4-93c0-485f7a19f274_1600x901.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!G4lt!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd89e13ca-c7a9-4cf4-93c0-485f7a19f274_1600x901.png)

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!kI_v!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa683ca39-01fe-4f4d-aeeb-2f534d2eba3d_1600x896.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!kI_v!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa683ca39-01fe-4f4d-aeeb-2f534d2eba3d_1600x896.png)

In this frenzy, the tech titans are fundamentally altering their business models.

They are transitioning from the capital-light, high-margin software services that drove their historic valuations to an asset-heavy, utility-like infrastructure model.

They are becoming the Global Crossings and WorldComs of the 21st century, sacrificing high return-on-capital profiles for sheer scale and depreciation risk.

Since the launch of ChatGPT in late 2022, AI-related CapEx growth accounts for a concerning 90% of the S&P 500’s aggregate capital spending growth.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FjQM!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Faec1efe4-82ab-4943-aac8-02fbdfc82f7e_1600x904.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FjQM!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Faec1efe4-82ab-4943-aac8-02fbdfc82f7e_1600x904.png)

The market is rewarding the *intent* to build, but has not yet priced the inevitable margin compression that results when fixed-cost infrastructure competes fiercely for utilization.

Furthermore, this rapid investment creates a ticking time bomb on the balance sheet: accelerated technological obsolescence. The core CapEx component, the specialized GPUs and servers, improves at an exponential rate (Moore’s Law pace), meaning the assets installed today risk becoming technologically obsolete within two or three years.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ous4!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdcd4f197-34f6-4013-a94a-fb5678a730bb_1600x896.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ous4!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdcd4f197-34f6-4013-a94a-fb5678a730bb_1600x896.png)

Yet, the physical data centers carrying these assets are depreciated over much longer traditional accounting horizons. This mismatch between technological reality and accounting expectation creates a form of hidden balance sheet risk, where assets carry inflated book values, setting the stage for potentially massive future write-downs and earnings disappointment.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!N7Lj!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F92955fd0-14f7-4756-8cb5-090b2e94a076_1600x903.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!N7Lj!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F92955fd0-14f7-4756-8cb5-090b2e94a076_1600x903.png)

The result of this financial concentration is acute systemic fragility.

Since November 2022, AI-related stocks have delivered over 70% of the S&P 500’s price returns and nearly 80% of its earnings growth. This concentration suggests the market is not pricing in the dispersed productivity gains of a general-purpose technology, but rather guaranteed, oligopolistic supply-chain control and monopoly rents for the builders.

This singular dependency means that if the underlying capital is misallocated, the systemic market impact is disproportionately large, contradicting the narrative of broad technological stability.

## **AI Doesn’t Eat Electricity, It Eats Credit**

The greatest irony of the AI revolution is that this purported exponential leap in intelligence is critically dependent on leveraging the most established, linear, and least-revolutionary segment of the financial market: investment-grade corporate debt.

AI is not self-funding itself. It’s fundamentally a financing cycle in disguise.

The sheer scale of the build-out is overwhelming the internal cash flows of the tech giants. Michael Burry stated that the largest hyperscalers are using debt to cover up to 94% of their operating cash flow dedicated to AI infrastructure.

This forces the migration away from the historically cash-rich, lean balance sheets that defined Silicon Valley success toward a model reliant on persistent, massive debt issuance.

To meet the gargantuan funding requirements, the J.P. Morgan file estimates that the investment-grade bond market must supply approximately $1.5 trillion in funding over the next five years. This staggering requirement is supplemented by $150 billion in leveraged loans, plus high-yield debt, private credit, and new securitization mechanisms (ABS) for data center assets. The future of artificial intelligence, therefore, rests on the dependable function of the fixed-income market, a structure that moves at the pace of maturity schedules and interest rate policy, not microchip efficiency.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mnee!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F51b75063-6236-4514-b932-efee59d73881_1600x646.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mnee!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F51b75063-6236-4514-b932-efee59d73881_1600x646.png)

The reliance on massive investment-grade (IG) bond issuance transfers the speculative risk inherent in a generational technology pivot to conservative, passive fixed-income investors, such as pension funds and insurance portfolios.

If the CapEx fails to generate the required returns, the credit ratings for these hyperscalers will come under pressure. A wave of potential downgrades, moving this debt from IG to high-yield/junk status, would destabilize the corporate bond market structure, injecting systemic risk far outside the volatile tech equity sphere.

The bond market is already signaling stress.

Recent massive debt issuances by hyperscalers have led to the widening of credit spreads, a clear indicator that investors are demanding higher compensation for bearing the risk of capital misallocation and future utilization uncertainty in this space.

This bearish sentiment (including recommendations to short hyperscaler debt) suggests that the fixed-income market is recognizing the structural leverage and industrial risk that equity investors have chosen to ignore.

The underlying truth is that the current boom is fundamentally less about realizing new intelligence and more about a debt-fueled, late-cycle attempt to refinance existing cloud dominance using the AI narrative as cover.

## **Waiting for the Power Grid**

The most profound vulnerability in the AI CapEx thesis is not financial, but physical. The speed of digital progress is hitting an unavoidable wall made of copper wire, concrete permits, and outdated utility regulation.

The power requirements are shocking in their magnitude. Global data center power demand is forecast to jump between 165% and 175% by 2030. One estimate suggests that US AI data center power demand alone could surge thirtyfold, reaching 123 GW by 2035. To meet the build-out requirements implied by the financial models, the world needs to add between 122 GW and 150 GW of new generation and distribution capacity, a demand equivalent to adding a major industrialized nation’s entire energy consumption to the global grid.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!K_Ku!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe084da76-f36a-4175-a45e-193fe6f28ce9_1866x1050.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!K_Ku!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe084da76-f36a-4175-a45e-193fe6f28ce9_1866x1050.png)

This immense demand exposes a crucial time-horizon mismatch between software fantasy and physical reality. A modern hyperscale data center can be physically constructed and ready for servers in one to two years. However, the wait time to connect that facility to the local power grid in high-demand areas can stretch up to seven years. Grid infrastructure upgrades and new, long-distance transmission lines require planning, regulatory approval, and lead times often measured in decades.

The physical barriers are numerous and slow-moving. They include complex and lengthy interconnection processes, chronic permitting delays caused by poor coordination between agencies, and supply chain constraints for critical construction materials like steel and copper, which have driven construction costs up by 40% in recent years.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FfKq!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fbf7f35f7-1e61-481b-b936-779124055e19_1864x1048.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FfKq!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fbf7f35f7-1e61-481b-b936-779124055e19_1864x1048.png)

This mismatch between CapEx deployment speed and utilization readiness guarantees capital inefficiency, the 1990s telecom “dark fiber” glut revisited. Servers, cooling systems, and networking equipment are being purchased with debt and installed, but remain dormant “dark compute” for years while they await power, burning through capital and postponing the return on investment. The single most disruptive variable for the projected AI economy is not a technological rival, but the mundane reality of government bureaucracy and utility regulation. The ability to secure power quickly, not superior chips, has become the ultimate competitive moat.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!7Twm!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdcfb8325-1c86-4c5b-8160-ff03fbee9978_1866x1050.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!7Twm!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdcfb8325-1c86-4c5b-8160-ff03fbee9978_1866x1050.png)

Finally, the concentrated, massive, round-the-clock power demand is imposing significant externalities on the public. Utilities are being forced to undertake rapid, expensive grid upgrades. Research shows that these upfront infrastructure costs are often partially passed on to residential power customers through faster-than-average rate hikes in data center boom regions. This transforms a speculative private investment into a public utility burden, guaranteeing increased political and regulatory pushback.

## **The Perpetual Revenue Fantasy**

When the warnings about excessive capital deployment and financial leverage begin coming from the heart of Wall Street, the seventh inning stretch is usually over. Morgan Stanley’s Chief Investment Officer, Lisa Shalett, has stated plainly that the GenAI CapEx story is already showing “cracks” and is “closer to the seventh than the first inning”. The narrative has peaked; the field is crowded; and the easy money based purely on deployment announcements has been made.

The final, fatal stress test for the entire boom is monetization. J.P. Morgan analyzed the gargantuan CapEx required and calculated the hurdle: to achieve a mere 10% return on the colossal investment, the industry requires $650 billion in *annual AI revenue* generated in perpetuity.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!3Cfk!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2b14e81d-4932-496e-8bb8-ca7b0ab7d7b2_1868x1048.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!3Cfk!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2b14e81d-4932-496e-8bb8-ca7b0ab7d7b2_1868x1048.png)

To grasp the absurdity of $650 billion, one must contextualize it. It is the equivalent of collecting $34.72 per month from every single current iPhone user globally, indefinitely.

This is not a marginal cloud service fee, nor is it a simple corporate subscription; it is a permanent, high-value tax on a significant portion of the global digital population. This proposition strains credulity, especially when considering the inevitable competition and deflationary forces that technology historically imposes.

Crucially, the assumption fails to account for the nature of AI’s current utility. Much of the early enterprise adoption of generative AI is focused on internal cost-cutting and efficiency gains, tasks that reduce expenses but do not generate the external, permanent $650 billion in new revenue required to justify the infrastructure.

If AI’s greatest long-term effect is to create abundance and efficiency, the natural long-term impact on pricing will be deflationary, guaranteeing rapid erosion of the high revenue targets needed to justify today’s CapEx. The financial models are relying on a mass consumer-facing monetization miracle that has yet to arrive or stabilize.

Confirming the fatigue with this narrative, the broader market is already showing signs of rotation. Analysts note that expectations of monetary policy shifts have led to capital flows moving out of the concentrated megacap AI names and into “cyclical and small-cap stocks”.

This rebalancing is not a sign of generalized health; it is smart money discreetly exiting the crowded, highly valued concentrated CapEx trade and seeking unloved value elsewhere, a textbook late-cycle flight from peak exuberance.

## **The Uncanny Valley of Capital**

The current AI CapEx boom is not a flawless technological revolution; it is a profoundly flawed capital cycle. The market has confused the brilliance of the software with the discipline of the finance. Massive deployment, leveraged by $1.5 trillion in traditional debt, is running headlong into physical constraints that operate on a time scale (seven-year utility waits) entirely incompatible with the speed of digital obsolescence.

The systemic risk is not about a consumer crash or a failed social network. The next tech reckoning will be an industrial crash driven by structural credit risk and infrastructure failure. The risk resides in the vast, dark compute facilities sitting idle because the permits didn’t clear and the utility cables weren’t laid, financed by investment-grade corporate bonds now facing maturity. The obsession with “trillions of parameters” blinded us to the humility required when dealing with “trillions of dollars.”

In the end, it wasn’t intelligence that broke the machines. It was leverage, waiting patiently for the utility bill.
